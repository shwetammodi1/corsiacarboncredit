Walk through a sponge iron plant in Chhattisgarh or a cement works in Rajasthan and you will feel heat pouring out of kilns, stacks and coolers. For decades much of that energy simply went into the sky. Capture it to raise steam and turn a turbine, and the plant needs less power from the coal-heavy grid. That is a waste-heat recovery project, and it is typical of what sits in our Industrial category.

Industrial credits come from making a factory, boiler or process use less fossil energy for the same output. They tend to be large, data-rich projects run by established companies, which brings both strengths and particular things to check.

## How the credits are created

Three activity types make up most of the category:

- **Waste-heat recovery.** Hot exhaust gases from kilns, furnaces or engines feed a boiler that produces steam for power or process heat, replacing grid electricity or fuel.
- **Fuel switching.** A boiler or kiln moves from coal or furnace oil to a lower-carbon fuel, such as natural gas or sustainably sourced biomass like rice husk or bagasse.
- **Energy efficiency retrofits.** High-efficiency motors, variable speed drives, better insulation, upgraded compressors or process changes that cut energy use per unit of product.

In each case, the project compares actual emissions with a **baseline**: what the plant would have emitted doing the same work the old way. The baseline is normally expressed per unit of output, such as per tonne of clinker or per megawatt-hour, so that a drop in production is not mistaken for a climate gain. Meters on fuel, steam and power, together with production records, give the monitored figures. An auditor verifies them and the registry issues credits.

## What a buyer gets

These are **reduction** credits: less fossil fuel burned than in the baseline. Where waste heat displaces grid power, part of the claim behaves like avoidance. They are not removals. Permanence is not a concern, because fuel not burned cannot be un-saved.

## The main quality questions

### Is the baseline fair?

The baseline should reflect how the plant actually ran before the project, or what a typical new plant would do, not an inefficient worst case. Watch for baselines set during an unusual year, or that assume old equipment would have kept running long past its normal life. Our page on [additionality, permanence and leakage](/knowledge-base/additionality-permanence-and-leakage/) explains how baselines and additionality fit together.

### Additionality in a commercial setting

Efficiency often pays for itself through fuel savings. If an upgrade had a short payback and was standard practice in the sector, carbon revenue probably did not drive it. The validation report should show why the investment was not attractive without carbon finance, or which barriers it overcame. Projects at large companies deserve the same scrutiny as any other.

### Metering and data

Industrial projects live or die on their meters. Ask which parameters are measured directly, how often meters are calibrated, and how gaps are handled. Cross-checks against fuel purchase invoices and electricity bills are a good sign.

### Biomass sourcing

For fuel switch to biomass, the climate benefit depends on the biomass being a genuine residue or sustainably grown, and not diverted from another use that then turns to fossil fuel. Ask where the biomass comes from and how that is documented.

### Overlap with India's CCTS

India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS) is a domestic compliance market. Large plants in energy-intensive sectors such as cement, iron and steel, aluminium, refining, and pulp and paper are being given emission intensity targets, and those that beat their targets can earn tradable carbon credit certificates. The scheme also has an offset route for activities outside the obligated sectors.

For a buyer of voluntary industrial credits, this raises a practical question: is the same tonne being counted twice? If a plant uses an efficiency gain to meet its CCTS target, or sells certificates for it, that tonne should not also be sold as a voluntary or CORSIA credit. Ask whether the facility is an obligated entity under CCTS, whether the project's reductions fall inside its compliance boundary, and whether the developer will confirm in writing that no domestic certificates cover the same reductions. For units meant for CORSIA or other international use, host-country authorisation and a corresponding adjustment also come into play; our page on [corresponding adjustments](/knowledge-base/corresponding-adjustments-made-simple/) explains why.

## Co-benefits

Co-benefits are narrower than for household projects but still real: lower local air pollution from reduced coal burning, better working conditions around hot equipment, skilled jobs, and a more competitive plant. Biomass fuel switching can give farmers a buyer for crop residue that might otherwise be burned in the field. Typical SDG tiles are Industry, Innovation and Infrastructure (SDG 9), Affordable and Clean Energy (SDG 7) and Responsible Consumption and Production (SDG 12).

## What to ask the seller

1. The registry ID, methodology and crediting period.
2. How the baseline was set, and the data period it uses.
3. The investment analysis or barrier case from the validation report.
4. Meter list, calibration records and a sample of monitored data.
5. Biomass sourcing records, for fuel-switch projects.
6. The facility's status under CCTS and written confirmation of no double counting.
7. The latest verification report; see [project documents buyers receive](/knowledge-base/project-documents-buyers-receive/) for what to look for.
