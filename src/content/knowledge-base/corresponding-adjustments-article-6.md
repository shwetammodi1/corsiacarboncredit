Most of what makes the CORSIA market unusual traces back to one requirement. Scarce supply, premium prices, sound projects that cannot sell to airlines, contracts full of authorisation clauses: all of it follows from the corresponding adjustment. It is the single concept on this site we would ask a new client to learn first.

![Diagram showing how a corresponding adjustment prevents double counting](/images/corsia/corsia-article6-adjustment.svg)

## A ledger with and without the adjustment

Take an illustrative biogas project in a host country that avoids 50,000 tonnes of CO2. It sells 50,000 credits to an airline, which cancels them for CORSIA.

| | Host country's books | Airline's CORSIA claim | Total claimed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **No adjustment** | Counts the 50,000 t toward its Nationally Determined Contribution | Counts the same 50,000 t | 100,000 t claimed for 50,000 t of real reduction |
| **With adjustment** | Adds the 50,000 t back, so its NDC progress shows the outcome was sold | Counts the 50,000 t | 50,000 t claimed for 50,000 t of real reduction |

The first row is **double claiming**. It is not a paperwork point. It is how a global accounting system can look as though it is delivering while achieving much less than it reports.

The remedy comes from **Article 6 of the Paris Agreement**. When a host country authorises a mitigation outcome to be used toward another party's target, or toward an international purpose such as CORSIA, it applies a **corresponding adjustment**: it adds those tonnes back into its own accounts, by raising its reported emissions or lowering its reported reductions. The airline makes the claim; the country gives it up. One tonne, one claimant.

The idea is simple. Getting a government to do it is not.

## Why a government might say no

Authorisation means accepting a harder route to the country's own climate target in exchange for foreign investment and project income. Whether that is a good trade depends on the country, and positions fall into roughly five groups:

- **Reluctant:** countries with ambitious NDCs and little cheap mitigation left. Selling their cheapest reductions abroad means paying for dearer ones at home later. Several large developing economies have taken this view.
- **Selective:** countries that keep back the project types central to their own abatement plans and authorise others.
- **Conditional:** countries that authorise but attach fees, revenue sharing or benefit requirements.
- **Unable:** countries with no designated authority, no process and no precedent. Whatever their stated policy, that amounts to a no in practice. Developers who read declared intent as an implied yes are the ones who lose their validation budgets.
- **Willing:** countries whose mitigation potential comfortably exceeds what their NDC needs.

**Countries building their own carbon markets** add a further pull. They may want to keep mitigation outcomes at home, where they support a domestic carbon price and industrial policy. India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme is a current example of that tension, and it shapes what Indian developers can realistically expect when they seek authorisation from the Government of India. See [CORSIA in India](/knowledge-base/corsia-in-india/).

## How small the adjusted pool is

No reliable public figure exists for the share of global credit supply that carries a corresponding adjustment. Every serious assessment arrives at the same answer in words: **a small minority**. The reasons stack up:

1. Most credits were issued before the Article 6 rules existed, by projects never designed for authorisation.
2. Many host countries still have no process.
3. Where a process exists, it usually authorises specific volumes rather than whole categories.
4. Authorising each tranche takes real administrative effort.

For CORSIA this is the tightest constraint on supply, and the reason forecasts keep showing a shortfall against demand, discussed in [supply and demand outlook](/knowledge-base/corsia-supply-and-demand-outlook/).

## Three states a unit can be in

| Status | What has happened | Can it be used for CORSIA? |
|---|---|---|
| Authorised, adjustment applied or firmly scheduled | Authorisation granted and the accounting done or fixed | Yes |
| Authorised, adjustment not yet reflected | Authorisation granted; the national accounts have not caught up | Usually, with care |
| Awaiting authorisation | Nothing has been granted | **No** |

These are often blurred, sometimes on purpose. "CORSIA-ready", "CORSIA-aligned" and "eligible pending authorisation" all describe the third row.

## What counts as proof

A complete evidence file contains:

- **the authorisation document**, issued by the designated national authority;
- **identification of the units** in that document: project, vintage or monitoring period, and volume or serial numbers;
- **the registry record** showing authorisation status in the programme's registry;
- **national reporting** in which the adjustment appears in the host country's Article 6 reporting;
- **any conditions or expiry** attached to the authorisation.

None of the following counts: a seller's assurance, a claim on a project website, a broker's confirmation, a statement that the country "supports Article 6", or a "CORSIA-ready" label.

## What each side should do

| Buyers | Sellers |
|---|---|
| Make authorisation evidence a gate the deal must pass, not a filing task | Settle the authorisation question before spending money on anything else |
| Get the document, read it, and check it names your units | Talk to the designated national authority directly instead of reading policy from speeches |
| Confirm the issuer really is the designated national authority | Base fees, conditions and timelines on actual cases, not on what is possible in principle |
| Find out whether the adjustment is **applied** or only **committed** | Run authorisation alongside development where the process allows |
| Judge the revocation risk in that particular country | Keep a voluntary market route open in case the answer is no |
| Keep copies in your own files; a link is not a record | Never call unauthorised units CORSIA-eligible. Experienced buyers notice, and it undermines everything else you tell them |
| If authorisation is pending, decide consciously to take the risk, price it and allocate it in the contract | |

## Competition from outside aviation

Corporate buyers in the voluntary market increasingly want adjusted units as well. Corporate offsetting claims have been criticised heavily over double counting, and an adjusted unit is the strongest answer available, so some companies pay a premium for it with no aviation obligation at all. Sellers benefit from the wider market. CORSIA buyers should expect to **compete with corporate demand for the same small pool** and put that into their supply assumptions.

## What happens next

Article 6 implementation is still developing. More governments are setting up designated authorities and processes, and reporting under the Paris transparency framework is maturing, so adjusted supply should grow over time. Whether it grows fast enough for second-phase CORSIA demand is unknown. It depends on separate decisions by dozens of governments, each balancing its own target against investment.

That uncertainty is something to plan around, not to guess away. Spread purchases, write contracts that allocate authorisation risk explicitly, and avoid any strategy that only works if authorisation behaves predictably.

## Terms used above

- **NDC:** Nationally Determined Contribution, a country's climate target under the Paris Agreement.
- **DNA:** Designated National Authority, the body that grants authorisation.
- **LoA:** Letter of Authorisation.
- **ITMO:** Internationally Transferred Mitigation Outcome, the Article 6.2 name for an authorised, adjusted outcome.
- **Article 6.2:** cooperative approaches between parties.
- **Article 6.4:** the centralised UN crediting mechanism.

More definitions are in the [CORSIA glossary](/knowledge-base/corsia-glossary/), and the other eligibility tests in [the emissions unit criteria](/knowledge-base/corsia-emissions-unit-criteria/). Article 6 guidance is published by the [UNFCCC](https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/the-paris-agreement/article-64-mechanism).
