A compliance manager at an Indian carrier is told the airline is "in CORSIA". In practice that sentence means three recurring jobs: measure the fuel burnt on international flights, have the resulting CO2 figure checked by an independent verifier, and, for some of those flights, cancel carbon units to cover the sector's growth. Everything else about the scheme is detail around those three jobs.

This page sets out what the scheme is, where its authority comes from, and the points newcomers most often get wrong.

![Overview graphic for the CORSIA knowledge base](/images/corsia/hero-corsia-knowledge-base.svg)

## CORSIA in plain terms

The name expands to the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation. It was agreed at the ICAO Assembly in 2016 (Resolution A39-3), and it was the first market-based climate measure to apply to one industry across the whole world.

The idea is compact. International aviation's CO2 in 2019 is the reference point. When the sector emits more than that, the operators whose flights are covered must pay for the excess by buying reductions made elsewhere and retiring them. The flights still burn fuel and still emit; the scheme balances the increase, it does not remove it.

Two consequences follow, and both matter when you explain CORSIA to a board.

- **Only growth is paid for.** If the sector stayed at its 2019 level, no one would owe anything, however large their emissions.
- **The reduction happens somewhere else.** A tonne cancelled for CORSIA represents a cut at a project, say a renewable plant or a methane capture site, that is meant to have happened only because the credit was sold.

## Who writes the rules and who enforces them

ICAO is a UN specialised agency. It sets standards; it cannot fine an airline. The obligation reaches an operator through a chain:

1. The ICAO Assembly adopts the scheme by resolution.
2. The detailed standards sit in Annex 16, Volume IV to the Chicago Convention.
3. Each member State writes those standards into its own law.
4. The State's aviation authority then does the day-to-day work: approving monitoring plans, receiving reports, acting on non-compliance.

For an Indian operator that authority is the [DGCA](https://www.dgca.gov.in/). In the United States it is the FAA, and in Europe it is the national competent authority of each country.

The result is a uniform obligation with non-uniform consequences. What happens to an operator that ignores CORSIA is decided by its own State's law, so penalties differ from one country to the next.

A note on the word "voluntary". Whether a **State** takes part was optional during the pilot and the first phase, and becomes compulsory for most States in the second phase. Whether an **operator** complies, once its State is in, has never been optional. People mix these two up constantly.

## The three recurring jobs

Here is what an in-scope operator actually does, reduced to a checklist.

- **Monitor, report, verify.** An operator emitting above 10,000 tonnes CO2 a year on international flights needs an approved Emissions Monitoring Plan, tracks fuel by an approved method, files an Annual Emissions Report and gets it verified by an accredited body. This happens every year, including years in which nothing is owed. Detail in [our MRV guide](/knowledge-base/corsia-mrv-explained/).
- **Work out what is owed.** When ICAO publishes the growth factors, the operator applies them to its verified emissions on covered routes, then deducts any reduction claimed for CORSIA Eligible Fuels. The tonnes add up over a three-year compliance period. The arithmetic is in [how the offsetting requirement is calculated](/knowledge-base/corsia-offsetting-requirement-calculation/).
- **Buy, cancel, report.** The operator acquires CORSIA Eligible Emissions Units, cancels them in a registry with CORSIA named as the purpose, and sends an Emissions Unit Cancellation Report to its authority.

The third item trips people up. Owning units in an account does nothing for compliance. The cancellation, reported to the authority, is the act that settles the obligation.

## Why an ordinary carbon credit will not do

This is the fact that most surprises buyers coming from the voluntary market.

For a unit to count under CORSIA it has to clear three gates:

| Gate | What it means in practice |
|---|---|
| Approved programme | Issued under a crediting programme the ICAO Council has approved |
| Eligible vintage | The reduction falls inside the vintage window the scheme allows |
| Corresponding adjustment | The host country's government adds the tonnes back to its own national inventory |

The third gate is the hard one. By adjusting, the host government gives up that reduction against its own Paris Agreement target. Plenty of governments are unwilling to do so, and many have no procedure for it yet. So a large share of the world's credits, including good ones, are simply not usable for CORSIA, and what remains is scarce and priced accordingly. [Corresponding adjustments and Article 6](/knowledge-base/corresponding-adjustments-article-6/) covers the mechanics.

## What people assume, and what is actually the case

| Common assumption | The position |
|---|---|
| CORSIA caps aviation emissions | No limit is set. The sector can grow; it pays for the growth. |
| It is a fuel tax or a ticket charge | No duty is paid. The obligation is met by cancelling units. |
| It cuts aviation's own emissions | The flights still emit. The offsetting cut is made in another sector. |
| It makes flying carbon neutral | The aim is carbon-neutral *growth* from 2019. Emissions up to the baseline are untouched. |
| Domestic routes are covered | International flights only. |
| It is an emissions trading system | Operators buy project credits, not allowances from a capped pool. |
| We are a small airline, so it cannot apply | Test the threshold properly. 10,000 tonnes from international flying is not a large operation, and reporting may apply even where offsetting does not. |
| Our existing carbon credits cover us | Unlikely. Most voluntary credits have no corresponding adjustment. |
| CORSIA has replaced the EU ETS | Both can apply to the same operator, with scopes arranged to limit double obligation. |
| Flying on SAF gets us out of it | SAF lowers the obligation, and only when the fuel meets the eligibility criteria, holds certification from an approved scheme and has documented chain of custody. |

## For Indian operators and developers

Two points specific to India.

First, the DGCA is the body an Indian operator deals with at every stage: it approves the monitoring plan, receives the verified report and the cancellation report, and applies Indian law if something goes wrong. The rules are ICAO's, but the relationship is with the DGCA.

Second, Indian project developers hoping to sell to airlines face the corresponding adjustment gate directly. A well-run Indian project without authorisation from the Government of India can sell into the voluntary market, not into CORSIA. We cover both sides in [CORSIA in India](/knowledge-base/corsia-in-india/).

## The scheme on one card

- **Run by:** International Civil Aviation Organization
- **Adopted:** ICAO Assembly, 2016, Resolution A39-3
- **Rulebook:** Annex 16, Volume IV to the Chicago Convention
- **Reference year:** 2019 international aviation CO2
- **Operator threshold:** above 10,000 tonnes CO2 a year from international flights
- **Aircraft covered:** maximum certificated take-off mass above 5,700 kg
- **Scope:** international flights; offsetting only on covered route pairs
- **Compliance periods:** blocks of three years
- **Runs until:** 2035, with periodic reviews
- **Instrument:** CORSIA Eligible Emissions Units, cancelled in a registry
- **Enforced by:** the operator's national authority, under national law
