A retirement certificate is the document most buyers file away and never look at again. It deserves more attention. It is your evidence that the credits you paid for were used once, for you, and can never be sold to anyone else. This page explains what retirement is, how to read the certificate, and how to confirm it yourself without relying on anyone's word, including ours.

## What retirement means

A carbon credit represents one tonne of carbon dioxide equivalent (tCO₂e) reduced or removed. While it sits in a registry account, it is a tradeable asset that can change hands many times. Retirement ends that. The holder instructs the registry to move the unit into a retired state, and from then on it cannot be transferred or used again.

You will also see the word **cancellation**. Under CORSIA, aircraft operators cancel eligible units and report the cancellation to their national authority, the DGCA in India. Some registries use "retire" and "cancel" for slightly different actions, so read the registry's own terms, but for a buyer the principle is the same: the unit is used up and recorded as such.

Retirement is what turns a purchase into a claim. Owning credits does not offset anything. Only retiring them does.

## Serial numbers: the key to everything

When a registry issues credits, it gives each unit a unique serial number. Issuances are usually shown as a block, with a start and end serial, and the serial string often encodes information such as the project ID and vintage year.

Serial numbers are what stop the same tonne being sold twice. If two buyers were ever shown the same serial range, only one retirement could be recorded against it, and the registry would reveal the conflict. That is why you should always ask for the serial range, and why we hand it over with every retirement.

## What a retirement certificate usually shows

Formats differ between registries, but most certificates or retirement records include:

| Field | What to check |
|---|---|
| Project name and registry ID | Matches the project you agreed to buy |
| Standard or programme | The one your policy or CORSIA requirement accepts |
| Vintage | The year the reductions happened; inside your agreed window |
| Quantity | The number of tCO₂e you paid for |
| Serial number range | Start and end serials covering that quantity |
| Retirement date | When the retirement was recorded |
| Beneficiary | Your organisation's name, spelled exactly right |
| Retirement reason or purpose | A short note, such as the reporting year it relates to |

Some certificates also show the methodology, the host country, and a label or note on authorisation and corresponding adjustment, which matters for CORSIA units and for some Article 6 uses.

## Why the beneficiary name matters

The beneficiary is the organisation on whose behalf the credits are retired. When credits are retired for you by another account holder, this field is what links the retirement to you publicly.

Get it right before the retirement is made. Use your full legal name, or the name your auditors will expect, for example "Shree Textiles Private Limited" rather than "Shree Tex". If you want a group company or a specific event named instead, say so in writing. Correcting a beneficiary after retirement is difficult and sometimes not possible.

A clear retirement purpose helps too. "Scope 1 and 2 emissions, FY 2025–26" is far more useful to an auditor than a blank field.

## Checking a retirement yourself

The registry, not the seller, is the official record of issuance and retirement. Most major registries publish retirement data on a public website. To check:

1. **Go to the registry's public search.** Start from the programme's official site, such as [verra.org](https://verra.org/) or [goldstandard.org](https://www.goldstandard.org/), and follow its link to the registry. Do not use a link sent in an email.
2. **Find the project** using the registry ID from your certificate.
3. **Open the retirement or cancellation records** for that project.
4. **Look for your serial range.** Confirm the quantity, vintage, date and beneficiary match your certificate.
5. **Save a copy.** Take a dated screenshot or export of the public record and keep it with your certificate.

If you cannot find the record, or any detail differs, raise it straight away. Small differences, such as a date shown in another time zone, are usually harmless. A missing serial range or a different beneficiary is not.

## Building an audit file

On our desk, the final step of a purchase is proof: the retirement certificate and serial numbers come with a file auditors can check. Whether you buy through us or anyone else, a good audit file holds:

- the retirement certificate;
- the public registry record you saved;
- the project documents reviewed during due diligence;
- the written confirmation of the lot, vintage and quantity;
- a note linking the retirement to the emissions it covers.

Keep it with your emissions inventory. It is also the base for any public statement you make, which we discuss in [claims after retiring credits](/knowledge-base/claims-after-retiring-credits/).
