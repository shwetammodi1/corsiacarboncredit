Take an Air India flight from Mumbai to London. It burns fuel over the Arabian Sea, through a string of national airspaces and across much of Europe. When climate treaties began sharing out emissions by country, nobody could say whose tonnes those were. CORSIA is what the aviation world eventually built to deal with that orphaned CO2, after roughly two decades of not dealing with it.

This page covers the history in order, because each feature of the scheme makes more sense once you know which argument it settled.

![Illustration used across the CORSIA carbon credit pages](/images/corsia/hero-corsia-carbon-credit.svg)

## A tonne with no owner

The Kyoto Protocol of 1997 made countries responsible for their own emissions. Domestic flights were easy: a Delhi–Bengaluru sector sits in India's total. International flights were not, because every allocation rule gives a different answer and each can be defended:

- the State the flight departs from;
- the State it lands in;
- the State where the airline is registered;
- the State where the fuel was bought.

Kyoto did not pick one. **Article 2.2 passed the question to ICAO**, asking developed countries to pursue limits on aviation emissions through that body. ICAO then spent close to twenty years discussing it without adopting anything binding, and international aviation's emissions kept rising the whole time.

## Europe decides to act alone

By the late 2000s the European Union had stopped expecting ICAO to move. Its 2008 legislation brought **every flight landing at or taking off from an EU airport** into the EU Emissions Trading System from 2012. The contentious part was distance: a service from Shanghai or Chicago would be charged for its whole length, not just the stretch inside European airspace.

Other governments reacted hard:

- China and India told their airlines not to comply.
- The United States legislated to protect its carriers from the scheme.
- A group of States signed the Moscow Declaration against it.
- Trade retaliation aimed at European aircraft makers was openly floated.

Europe won in court. The Court of Justice of the European Union upheld the measure in 2011. It was losing diplomatically, though, and in 2012 it adopted **"stop the clock"**, which suspended the rules for flights to and from non-EU countries. The scheme was later cut back to flights inside the European Economic Area, on the stated condition that ICAO produce a global alternative.

That condition is the most direct cause of CORSIA. Without the EU's pressure, it is hard to see the scheme taking the shape it has, or arriving when it did.

## ICAO's route to an agreement

Some groundwork was already in place. The 2010 ICAO Assembly had set an aspirational goal of **carbon neutral growth from 2020**. In 2013 the Assembly committed to designing a global market-based measure. At the **39th Assembly in 2016, Resolution A39-3 adopted CORSIA**.

The central difficulty in the negotiations was reconciling two principles:

- **non-discrimination between operators**, from the Chicago Convention, which says an airline's nationality should not change the rules it faces; and
- **common but differentiated responsibilities**, from the UNFCCC, which says richer and poorer countries should not carry the same burden.

The phased design is how the negotiators squared that circle. The table below links each concession to the objection it answered.

| Objection raised in negotiation | Feature that answered it |
|---|---|
| Fast-growing aviation markets would not accept a limit on emissions | Operators offset instead of working under a cap |
| Established carriers did not want existing flying penalised | Only growth above a baseline is priced |
| Many States would not sign up on day one | State participation is voluntary in the early phases |
| Expanding airlines feared carrying a disproportionate share straight away | The burden is first shared out with a sector-wide growth factor |
| Poorer and vulnerable countries sought protection | The second phase is mandatory, but least developed countries, small island developing States and landlocked developing countries stay out unless they opt in |
| No appetite for a new capped market and fights over allocation | Operators use project credits, not allowances |

## 2020: the baseline is rewritten

As first agreed, the baseline was the **average of 2019 and 2020** emissions. Then the pandemic grounded most international flying.

Averaging a normal year with 2020 would have set the baseline far below any realistic level of future traffic. Every later obligation would have ballooned, loading a large and unintended cost onto airlines that were already fighting to survive. The ICAO Council responded in 2020 by **making 2019 alone the baseline**.

For planning, the lesson is wider than the arithmetic. CORSIA's settings have already been changed once when circumstances shifted. A compliance budget that assumes every parameter stays fixed until 2035 is assuming something the scheme's own record does not support.

## The objections that remain

The scheme is criticised from several sides. None of these criticisms alters what an operator owes, but they feed the periodic reviews, and those reviews can change the rules.

| Criticism | The substance | The usual reply or limit |
|---|---|---|
| Offsetting is not decarbonisation | Paying for cuts in other sectors lets aviation postpone changing its own fuel and technology | Supporters describe CORSIA as a bridge that covers growth while those technologies mature |
| The baseline is generous | 2019 was a record year, so everything up to that high level goes unpriced | Accepted as the price of agreement; emissions at or under the baseline are the large majority and are untouched |
| Credit integrity | Research has challenged additionality and baseline-setting in several common credit types | The ICAO Emissions Unit Criteria are tighter than the general voluntary market, especially on double counting, but inherit the same methodological arguments |
| Coverage gaps | Domestic flying is excluded, and voluntary participation left much traffic outside | Coverage widens considerably in the second phase |
| Only CO2 counts | Contrails, nitrogen oxides and other effects are ignored, and some assessments put them at roughly double the CO2 warming | Not addressed; the scheme is CO2-only |

## Where India fits in the story

India was one of the countries that instructed its airlines not to comply with the EU's extended scheme, so Indian carriers were directly involved in the dispute that produced CORSIA. Today the rules come from ICAO, but an Indian operator's working relationship is with the [DGCA](https://www.dgca.gov.in/), which administers them nationally. Domestic sectors remain outside the scheme for the reason set out at the top of this page: they already belong to a national inventory. For the present-day picture, see [CORSIA in India](/knowledge-base/corsia-in-india/).

## Chronology

- **1997:** Kyoto Protocol Article 2.2 refers international aviation emissions to ICAO.
- **2008:** EU law puts all flights touching EU airports into the EU ETS.
- **2010:** ICAO Assembly adopts the goal of carbon neutral growth from 2020.
- **2011:** CJEU rejects the legal challenge to the EU measure.
- **2012:** EU "stop the clock" suspends the rules for flights to and from third countries.
- **2013:** ICAO Assembly agrees to develop a global market-based measure.
- **2016:** 39th ICAO Assembly adopts CORSIA through Resolution A39-3.
- **2019:** Monitoring, reporting and verification start.
- **2020:** ICAO Council changes the baseline to 2019 emissions only.
- **2021:** Pilot phase starts.
- **2024:** First phase starts.
- **2027:** Second phase starts, and participation becomes compulsory for most States.
- **2035:** Scheme ends under its current design.
