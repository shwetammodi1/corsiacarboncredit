The ICAO Emissions Unit Criteria (EUC) are the rulebook a carbon credit is measured against before an airline may cancel it for CORSIA. They work as a pass/fail list. A unit has to satisfy all of them; strength on one cannot offset weakness on another, and a buyer cannot average quality across a portfolio.

We find the criteria easier to apply when grouped by the question each one answers. There are three questions: is the tonne genuine, is it counted only once, and is it acceptable to use.

![Diagram of the tests a unit must pass to be CORSIA eligible](/images/corsia/corsia-eeu-criteria.svg)

## Summary

| Question | Criterion | Where it most often fails |
|---|---|---|
| Is the tonne genuine? | Additionality | Renewables in markets where they are already cheapest |
| | Real, measurable, conservatively quantified | Monitoring the project cannot sustain |
| | Permanence | Buffer pools sized on past risk |
| | Independent verification | Verifier capacity and conflicts of interest |
| Is it counted once? | No double counting | **Missing corresponding adjustment, by a wide margin** |
| | Registry traceability | The buyer's own records |
| Is it acceptable to use? | Eligible vintage | Older stock falling outside the window |
| | No net harm | Disputes over land tenure |

## Question one: is the tonne genuine?

### Additionality

**Asks:** would the reduction have happened without carbon revenue? If the activity was already profitable, required by law or common practice, paying for it achieves nothing.

**Proof:** a credible baseline scenario; an investment or barrier analysis showing carbon revenue was needed; a common practice test showing the technology is not already normal in that setting.

**Weak spot:** this is the most argued-over criterion in carbon markets. Grid-connected renewables are the usual example. Claiming a solar plant needed carbon revenue was easy in 2012; it is very hard now that solar is the cheapest way to generate power. Several programmes have narrowed or withdrawn methodologies for that reason. A design that passed ten years ago may not pass today, and earlier success under a methodology is no guarantee.

### Real, measurable and conservatively quantified

**Asks:** did the reduction physically happen, can it be measured under a defined methodology, and were assumptions chosen to under-credit rather than over-credit?

**Proof:** an approved methodology; a monitoring plan stating exactly what is measured and how; calibrated equipment where needed; documented calculations.

**Weak spot:** monitoring more than arithmetic. A methodology may demand data at a frequency the project cannot keep up for its whole life; broken meters go unreplaced; field staff leave with knowledge nobody wrote down. Being conservative means settling real uncertainty in favour of fewer credits. Cookstove projects have been challenged over usage assumptions and forestry projects over baseline deforestation rates, and some of those challenges have succeeded.

### Permanence

**Asks:** will the reduction last? For avoided emissions this is simple, since a tonne never emitted stays that way. For carbon held in trees or soil it is not, because forests burn, are felled or die.

**Proof:** contributions to a buffer pool that holds back credits against reversals; long-term monitoring commitments; legal or contractual protection of the carbon stock.

**Weak spot:** whether buffers are big enough. As fire and drought losses mount, a buffer sized on historical risk may be too small for current risk. The question is still open.

### Independent verification

**Asks:** has a qualified body, independent of the developer, confirmed the reductions happened as claimed?

**Proof:** a verification report from a body accredited under the programme's rules, covering a stated monitoring period, with any qualifications spelled out.

**Weak spot:** there are not many accredited verifiers, so queues form. Conflict rules also bite: the body that validated a project's design often cannot verify its results.

## Question two: is it counted only once?

### No double counting

This single criterion removes more supply than all the others together. Double counting takes three forms:

- **Double issuance:** two credits issued for one reduction. Registry controls prevent it.
- **Double use:** one credit retired twice. Unique serial numbers prevent it.
- **Double claiming:** the host country counts the reduction toward its NDC while an airline counts it toward CORSIA. Only a **corresponding adjustment** prevents it.

**Proof:** an authorisation from the host country's designated national authority that refers to the specific units, and evidence that the adjustment appears in national reporting.

**Weak spot:** constant. A government has to trade investment and revenue against a harder route to its own target. Many say no; others have no authority, process or precedent for saying yes. For Indian projects the decision sits with the Government of India.

![Diagram showing how a corresponding adjustment prevents double claiming](/images/corsia/corsia-article6-adjustment.svg)

The mechanics are in [corresponding adjustments and Article 6](/knowledge-base/corresponding-adjustments-article-6/).

### Registry traceability

**Asks:** is every unit uniquely serialised, in a registry that records issuance, transfer and cancellation openly?

**Proof:** registry records tracing the unit's full history up to cancellation.

**Weak spot:** seldom the registry itself, since those systems are generally sound. The failures happen in the **buyer's** files, when the evidence cannot be pulled together three years later for a verifier. How registries handle this is covered in [registries and cancellation](/knowledge-base/corsia-registries-and-cancellation/).

## Question three: is it acceptable to use?

### Eligible vintage

**Asks:** did the reduction take place inside the vintage window the ICAO Council has set for the compliance period concerned?

**Proof:** the registry record of the monitoring period in which the reduction occurred. **Issuance date and vintage are different things.** A credit issued last year may carry a much older vintage.

**Weak spot:** older stock ages out, and windows have been moved before, so units close to a boundary are risky. See [vintages and eligibility windows](/knowledge-base/corsia-vintages-and-eligibility-windows/).

### No net harm

**Asks:** does the project comply with host-country law and avoid social and environmental harm?

**Proof:** an environmental and social impact assessment; records of stakeholder consultation; free, prior and informed consent where indigenous or local communities are affected; a grievance mechanism that works.

**Weak spot:** land tenure, especially in forestry. Consent from a government or a large landowner is not consent from the communities who actually use the land, and projects that treated them as the same have faced long challenges.

## Worked review: an illustrative Indian project

A hypothetical grid-connected solar project in India, registered years ago under an approved programme, offers credits to an airline. Run through the three questions:

- **Genuine?** Verification is in order, but additionality for new grid solar is now hard to argue, so newer issuance under the same methodology may face scrutiny.
- **Counted once?** No authorisation from the Government of India and no corresponding adjustment. **Fails.**
- **Acceptable?** Much of the older issuance sits outside the current vintage window.

Result: sound credits for the voluntary market, not CORSIA units. The unit-level tests, not the project's reputation, decide it. This is why "is the programme approved?" is only the first check, as explained in [approved crediting programmes](/knowledge-base/corsia-approved-crediting-programmes/).

Resolve every doubtful answer **before** paying. A cheap unit that turns out ineligible is the most expensive one you can buy. The criteria themselves are published by [ICAO](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/CORSIA-Emissions-Units.aspx).
