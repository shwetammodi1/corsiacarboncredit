Offsetting gets the attention, but monitoring, reporting and verification (MRV) is the part of CORSIA that never switches off. It runs every calendar year for every operator above the threshold, and its output is the verified data from which ICAO works out what the whole sector owes.

![Diagram of the yearly CORSIA MRV cycle](/images/corsia/corsia-mrv-cycle.svg)

## Who is caught, and under which rules

The requirements are in Annex 16, Volume IV to the Chicago Convention, given legal effect by each State's national law. ICAO's Environmental Technical Manual, Volume IV, carries the supporting guidance.

An operator falls in if its international flights produce more than 10,000 tonnes of CO2 a year, counting aeroplanes with a maximum take-off mass above 5,700 kg. Whether you are in is worked out in [scope and thresholds](/knowledge-base/corsia-scope-and-thresholds/).

## Reporting reaches further than offsetting

ICAO sets the sectoral growth factor from the verified emissions of the entire covered sector. Without every operator's report, there is no factor.

So an airline whose routes all touch non-participating States, and which therefore owes no offsets, still has to monitor, report and have its figures verified. Its data feeds the number that sets other operators' bills.

Keep two lists in your head. The reporting list is broad: all international flights. The offsetting list is narrow: flights on covered route pairs. Operators who size their paperwork to their offsetting obligation end up under-reporting.

## The cycle at a glance

| Stage | Who acts | What comes out of it |
|---|---|---|
| Emissions Monitoring Plan | Operator drafts; national authority approves before monitoring starts | Agreed method, data sources, systems, roles and controls |
| Monitoring through the year | Operator | Fuel use per flight and per aerodrome pair, by the approved method |
| Reconciliation | Operator | One dataset, with written rules for settling discrepancies |
| Annual Emissions Report | Operator | Calendar-year CO2 for all international flights, covered subset marked |
| Verification | Accredited verification body | Verification statement and any findings |
| Submission | Operator to national authority (the [DGCA](https://www.dgca.gov.in/) in India) | Verified report on file |
| State to ICAO | National authority | Aggregated data that goes into the sectoral growth factor |

The first stage deserves its own read: [what the Emissions Monitoring Plan must contain](/knowledge-base/corsia-emissions-monitoring-plan/). The method you commit to there is the one you will be verified against, so choose it with your real data in front of you. [Fuel monitoring methods compared](/knowledge-base/corsia-fuel-monitoring-methods/) helps with that choice.

## Reconciliation is where the hours go

Operators tend to expect the hard part to be the carbon calculation. It isn't. The arithmetic is simple: fuel mass multiplied by 3.16 kg CO2 per kg for Jet-A and Jet-A1 (3.10 for AvGas).

The hard part is deciding what the fuel mass actually was. Uplift dockets, flight operations records, aircraft systems and finance ledgers each hold a fuel figure, taken at slightly different moments and for slightly different purposes. They rarely agree, and usually nobody has made a mistake.

**A worked example (illustrative figures).** Take one Mumbai to Dubai sector. The flight operations record says 12,400 kg of fuel was used; the figure derived from the aircraft system says 12,310 kg.

- At 3.16, the first gives 39,184 kg CO2; the second gives 38,899.6 kg.
- The gap is 284.4 kg CO2 on one sector. Trivial on its own.
- Across a year of several thousand international sectors, gaps like that add up to a figure a verifier will ask about.

Which source wins is less important than having a rule, writing it down, and applying it the same way every time, with exceptions logged. A verifier will ask why a given discrepancy was settled as it was. "That is how we have always done it" does not survive the question.
## What the Annual Emissions Report holds

One report per calendar year, covering every international flight, with the in-scope flights identified. Expect it to include:

- the operator's identity and which version of the monitoring plan applied
- fuel burnt and CO2, broken down by aerodrome pair
- covered and non-covered route pairs, shown separately
- any CORSIA Eligible Fuels claimed, with certification behind them
- data gaps that arose and how each was filled
- changes to methods or systems during the year

## Verification: three things to plan around

::: accordion Your adviser cannot be your verifier
The body that verifies your report must be independent of it. A firm that wrote your monitoring plan cannot verify against that plan, and a body that validated something may be conflicted out of verifying the related performance.

This is normal assurance practice, but it surprises operators used to one firm doing both. Budget for two suppliers from day one.
:::

::: accordion Verifier capacity is limited, and everyone wants it at once
There are not many accredited bodies, and every operator works to the same reporting calendar, so demand bunches into the same months each year. Book early. Operators who leave it late take whichever verifier still has space, at that verifier's price.
:::

::: accordion What the verifier will test
Sampling of fuel records back to source documents. The reconciliation logic and the written rules behind it. Completeness of the flight list. How flights were sorted into in-scope and out-of-scope. How gaps and exceptions were handled. Evidence behind any fuels claim.

Having that evidence filed and indexed before the verifier arrives is what separates a quick verification from a costly one.
:::

## Materiality and data gaps

No fuel dataset is complete. Two ideas decide how imperfection is treated.

**Materiality** is the size of misstatement that counts as significant. Thresholds are set relative to reported emissions and are tighter for bigger emitters.

**Data gaps** will happen: a lost docket, a system outage, a record nobody can find. The monitoring plan has to say in advance how a gap will be filled, normally through a documented estimation procedure. Gaps patched by a method invented after the event are the ones that produce findings.

## Records you can defend in three years

Assume that in three years the person who prepared this report has left and the fuel system has been replaced. Your files should still let a stranger rebuild the reported figure. At minimum keep:

- every monitoring plan version, with its approval
- source fuel and flight data
- the reconciliation logic and its written rules
- the Annual Emissions Report as submitted
- the verification statement and the findings log
- evidence for any fuels claim
- acknowledgements received from the authority

Give one named person ownership of retention; records lost in a system migration are a common reason a figure cannot be defended.

## Mistakes we see, and the fix

- **Choosing a monitoring method on paper.** Test it against a sample of your real data before the plan is approved; a wrong choice surfaces at verification, when the year cannot be rerun.
- **Reconciling by hand each year.** Build the pipeline once, so years are consistent with each other.
- **Booking the verifier late.** Engage months ahead of the reporting season.
- **Settling discrepancies informally.** Write the rules; log every exception.
- **Classifying flights ad hoc.** Make in-scope logic systematic, or risk a material misstatement.
- **Assuming no reporting because nothing is owed.** Reporting scope is wider than offsetting scope, and missing it invites enforcement.

Your verified emissions are also the input to [baseline and growth factors](/knowledge-base/corsia-baseline-and-growth-factors/). The standards themselves are published by [ICAO](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/default.aspx).
