Most explanations of CORSIA start with carbon credits. Operationally, credits come last. The scheme begins with a fuel docket at an airport, passes through an auditor and an ICAO calculation, and only then reaches a registry where units are retired. This page walks through that chain in the order an operator lives it.

The short version: measure every year, verify every year, and settle the bill once every three years.

![Diagram of the annual and three-yearly CORSIA compliance cycle](/images/corsia/corsia-compliance-cycle.svg)

## The four stages at a glance

| Stage | Who acts | What exists at the end |
|---|---|---|
| A. Set-up | Operator, then national authority | An approved Emissions Monitoring Plan |
| B. Measure, report, verify (every year) | Operator, then an accredited verifier | A verified Annual Emissions Report |
| C. Sizing the sector's growth | National authority, then ICAO | A published growth factor |
| D. Settlement (every three years) | Operator, registry, national authority | Cancelled units and a filed cancellation report |

Stages B and C repeat annually. Stage D closes each compliance period.

## Stage A: agree how you will measure

Nothing is counted until the national authority has approved the operator's **Emissions Monitoring Plan**. For an Indian carrier that authority is the DGCA. The plan fixes:

- the **fuel use monitoring method**, chosen from those ICAO allows;
- where each number comes from, down to the IT system;
- the rule for deciding whether a given flight is in or out of scope;
- who is responsible for what, and the internal checks;
- what happens when data goes missing.

The method choice is the decision with the longest tail. An operator that commits to a method its systems cannot actually support will usually find out at verification, a year later, when the reporting period has closed and missing records cannot be rebuilt. The options are compared in [fuel monitoring methods](/knowledge-base/corsia-fuel-monitoring-methods/).

## Stage B: measure, report and verify

**Measurement.** Fuel is tracked for each flight, grouped by aerodrome pair, using the approved method. CO2 follows from fuel mass by fixed factors: **3.16 kg CO2 per kg** of Jet-A or Jet-A1, and **3.10** for AvGas.

The multiplication is trivial. The work is in reconciliation. Uplift dockets from the fuel supplier, flight operations data, onboard system readings and finance records seldom match to the kilogram, and turning them into one dataset with written rules for every discrepancy takes most of the year's effort.

**Reporting.** After each calendar year the operator prepares an **Annual Emissions Report** covering all its international flights and identifying which of them fall in scope.

**Verification.** An **accredited verification body** tests that report against Annex 16, Volume IV. It is a real audit, not a sign-off. Two practical limits:

- The verifier must be independent. A firm that advised on your plan or your report cannot then verify it.
- Accredited verifiers are few and every operator wants them in the same weeks. Late engagement means taking whoever has capacity left.

![Diagram of the monitoring, reporting and verification cycle](/images/corsia/corsia-mrv-cycle.svg)

The full sequence, with dates, is in [CORSIA MRV explained](/knowledge-base/corsia-mrv-explained/).

## Stage C: ICAO sizes the sector's growth

The authority passes verified data on to ICAO. ICAO needs every operator's figures because the obligation is driven by the growth of the **whole covered sector**, not by any single airline.

ICAO compares the sector's covered emissions for the year with the **2019 baseline** and publishes a **sectoral growth factor**, the share of covered emissions that has to be offset. From 2030 an **individual growth factor**, based on each operator's own growth, is blended in with a weight that increases over time.

![Chart comparing sectoral and individual growth factors](/images/corsia/corsia-growth-factors.svg)

This produces the result that most surprises new compliance teams: **an airline whose emissions went down can still owe offsets** if the sector as a whole went up. The operator's own emissions set its share; the sector's growth sets the size of the bill. The detail is in [baseline and growth factors](/knowledge-base/corsia-baseline-and-growth-factors/).

## Stage D: calculate, buy, cancel, report

**Calculate.** The operator multiplies its **covered emissions**, meaning international flights on route pairs where both States participate, by the growth factor. It then deducts any claimed reduction from qualifying CORSIA Eligible Fuels. Annual figures add up across the three-year compliance period. The arithmetic, with examples, is in [the offsetting requirement calculation](/knowledge-base/corsia-offsetting-requirement-calculation/).

**Buy.** The operator sources **CORSIA Eligible Emissions Units**, which must meet every part of the ICAO Emissions Unit Criteria.

![Diagram of the tests a unit must pass to be CORSIA eligible](/images/corsia/corsia-eeu-criteria.svg)

This is the hard step. Most carbon credits lack the host-State corresponding adjustment that eligibility demands, so eligible supply is thin, traded bilaterally rather than on a liquid exchange, and priced well above the ordinary voluntary market. See [eligible emissions units](/knowledge-base/corsia-eligible-emissions-units/).

**Cancel.** The units are cancelled in the registry of the programme that issued them, with CORSIA recorded as the purpose. Buying units does not settle anything, and neither does holding them. Cancellation does, and it cannot be reversed.

![Diagram of a unit's life from issuance to cancellation](/images/corsia/corsia-unit-lifecycle.svg)

**Report.** The operator files an **Emissions Unit Cancellation Report** with its authority, listing serial numbers, programme, vintage and the cancellation reference. The authority cannot see registry activity for itself, so until this report arrives, as far as the regulator is concerned the cancellation has not happened.

## Worked example: one compliance period, seen from the operator's desk

An illustrative Indian carrier flies international routes during a three-year period. In outline:

1. **Before year 1:** its monitoring plan is approved.
2. **Each year:** it collects fuel data, reports after year end, and has the report verified the following spring.
3. **Each year, once ICAO publishes the growth factor:** its obligation for that year becomes a firm number instead of a forecast.
4. **Across the period:** those annual numbers add up.
5. **After the period closes:** it must cancel units for the total and report the cancellation by the deadline.

The trap sits between steps 3 and 5. The moment the figure is firm and the moment units must be cancelled are close together, and every covered operator reaches that point at the same time. When the whole sector buys from a thin market in the same window, prices do not stay calm. Add registry account opening (weeks), transfers between registries (not instant) and processing time for cancellation and reporting, and the three years are shorter than they look.

## Mistakes we see at each stage

- **Set-up:** picking the monitoring method that looks most accurate on paper rather than the one the airline's systems can feed.
- **Measure:** leaving fuel-source reconciliation to year end, when discrepancies are hardest to explain.
- **Verify:** approaching verifiers in the reporting season and finding none available, or appointing one that advised on the report.
- **Calculate:** assuming falling own emissions mean no obligation.
- **Buy:** holding voluntary credits and treating them as CORSIA-ready without checking the corresponding adjustment.
- **Cancel and report:** treating the purchase, or the registry cancellation alone, as the end of the job.

The better practice is to estimate the obligation every year even though it settles every three, buy in tranches as the estimate firms up, and keep the final stretch for reconciliation rather than the main purchase.
