Renewable energy was the backbone of the early carbon market, and India supplied a large share of it. Wind farms in Tamil Nadu and Gujarat, solar parks in Rajasthan and run-of-river hydro in Himachal Pradesh all registered projects and sold credits for years. Today the category is still large and well supplied, but it is also the one where buyers ask the most pointed questions. This guide explains why, and how to buy renewable credits with your eyes open.

## How the credits are created

A grid-connected solar, wind or hydro plant feeds electricity into the grid. Had it not been built, that electricity would have come from the existing mix of power stations, much of it coal in India. The credit represents the emissions those other plants did not produce.

The calculation has two parts:

1. **Electricity delivered.** Measured by the export meter at the point of connection, cross-checked against the buyer of the power (often a state distribution company) through invoices or joint meter readings.
2. **The grid emission factor.** The tonnes of CO₂ the grid emits per megawatt-hour. Most methodologies use a "combined margin", which blends the plants running at the margin today with the plants most recently built. In India, the Central Electricity Authority publishes a CO₂ baseline database that projects commonly draw on.

Multiply the two and you have the emission reductions for the period. An auditor checks the meter data and the factor, and the registry issues credits.

Because the emission factor is fixed in the project documents for a crediting period, two similar solar plants on different grids, or registered at different times, can earn quite different volumes per megawatt-hour.

## What a buyer gets

These credits are usually labelled **Avoidance** on a listing: they represent fossil generation that did not happen, not carbon pulled from the air. That suits many voluntary goals, but a company that has promised removals for its residual emissions will need something else, such as the projects in our [forestry guide](/knowledge-base/forestry-and-agroforestry-credits/).

## The main quality questions

### Additionality is the big one

The central test for any credit is whether the project happened because of carbon revenue. For large grid renewables this has become hard to argue. Solar and wind are now often the cheapest new power in India, are supported by auctions and policy targets, and are financed on the strength of long power purchase agreements. A project that would have been built anyway does not create a real extra reduction, however well it is metered.

Standard-setters have responded. [Verra](https://verra.org/) and [Gold Standard](https://www.goldstandard.org/) have both narrowed the eligibility of new grid-connected renewable projects outside the least developed countries, and quality labels from independent bodies have been cautious about the main grid-renewable methodologies. Older registered projects can still issue credits for their remaining crediting periods, which is where much of the current supply comes from.

What this means for you: read the additionality section of the validation report, note when the project was commissioned and when it first considered carbon finance, and decide whether the argument would satisfy your own stakeholders. Small, off-grid or mini-grid projects, and those in least developed countries, generally have a stronger case. Our page on [additionality, permanence and leakage](/knowledge-base/additionality-permanence-and-leakage/) sets out the tests.

### Double claiming with renewable energy certificates

The same megawatt-hour can sometimes be sold as a renewable energy certificate (such as an I-REC or an Indian REC) and also counted towards carbon credits. Good practice is that it should not be claimed twice. Ask the seller to confirm in writing that no certificates were issued for the generation behind your credits.

### Vintage and crediting period

A plant may be on its second or third crediting period, with an updated emission factor each time. Old vintages from early periods are common in this category; check the vintage against what your goal or programme accepts. Our guide to [choosing vintage and volume](/knowledge-base/choosing-vintage-and-volume/) covers this.

### Permanence

Permanence is rarely an issue. Once fossil generation has been avoided, it cannot be reversed in the way a burnt forest can.

## Co-benefits

Co-benefits are real but modest for large plants: some local jobs during construction and operation, cleaner air from displaced coal, and support for India's energy transition. Typical SDG tiles are Affordable and Clean Energy (SDG 7) and Climate Action (SDG 13). Small hydro and village mini-grids can do more, bringing reliable power to places that lacked it, and should be able to show it.

## What to ask the seller

- The registry ID, methodology and crediting period, plus which period the offered vintage falls in.
- The commissioning date and the date carbon finance was first documented.
- The grid emission factor used and its source.
- Meter readings or generation records for the vintage on offer.
- Written confirmation that no renewable energy certificates cover the same generation.
- Whether the project is in a least developed country, or is small or off-grid.
- For CORSIA buyers, whether the units are from an eligible programme and vintage and carry host-country authorisation; our [CORSIA eligible units checklist](/knowledge-base/corsia-eligible-units-checklist/) lists what to request.
