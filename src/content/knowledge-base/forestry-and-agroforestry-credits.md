A teak plantation on degraded land in Madhya Pradesh, a few thousand smallholders in Andhra Pradesh planting melia dubia along field edges, a reforestation block on old mining land: all of these can produce carbon credits. They sit together in the Forestry category of the marketplace because they work the same way. Trees take carbon dioxide out of the air and store it in wood, roots and soil, and a project is paid for the extra carbon its trees hold.

This guide covers afforestation, reforestation and agroforestry. Forest protection projects (often called REDD+) are a different kind of credit with different risks, and we do not cover them here.

## How the credits are created

The project starts with a baseline: how much carbon the land would hold if nothing were planted. Degraded or bare land usually has a low baseline, which is why most projects use it.

After planting, the developer measures the trees at set intervals. Field teams record species, trunk diameter and height in sample plots, and growth equations convert those measurements into tonnes of stored carbon. Satellite images help check that the planted area is still standing. An independent auditor then verifies the numbers, and the registry issues credits for the carbon gained since the last check, after deductions for the baseline, leakage and the buffer (see below).

Two points follow for buyers:

- **Credits arrive slowly at first.** Young trees store little. Volumes usually rise over the first decade, so early vintages are often small.
- **Much of the market is forward.** Because issuance lags planting by years, many forestry lots are sold before the credits exist. Our page on [spot, issued and forward credits](/knowledge-base/spot-issued-and-forward-credits/) explains what that means for timing and risk.

## What a buyer gets

Planting projects produce **removals**: carbon taken out of the atmosphere, not just emissions avoided. On a listing they are marked as Removal. That label is the main reason buyers choose forestry, because many corporate net-zero plans give removals more weight than reductions for dealing with residual emissions.

## The main quality questions

### Permanence and the buffer

Carbon in a tree is only stored while the tree stands. Fire, drought, pests, illegal felling or a landowner changing their mind can release it. Standards deal with this through a **buffer pool**: a share of every issuance is set aside and never sold. If a stored stock is lost, credits are cancelled from the pool to cover it. The share held back depends on a risk assessment of the project, so a fire-prone site pays in more than a well-managed one.

Ask what percentage goes to the buffer, and whether any losses have already been reported.

### Additionality

A credit is only worth buying if the carbon revenue made the planting happen. A commercial timber plantation that would have been profitable anyway is a weak case. Smallholder agroforestry, where farmers need upfront support to give up crop space to trees, is often stronger. The validation report should explain the argument. Our page on [additionality, permanence and leakage](/knowledge-base/additionality-permanence-and-leakage/) goes into the tests in more detail.

### Leakage and land use

If planting displaces grazing or crops to the next village, emissions may simply move. Projects on genuinely idle land, or agroforestry that keeps the farm producing, carry less of this risk.

### Measurement honesty

Look at how often plots are re-measured, how plot locations were chosen, and whether satellite checks match the field data. A sudden jump in volume between two verifications deserves an explanation.

### Species and ecology

Monocultures of fast-growing exotics store carbon quickly but give fewer wider benefits, and can strain water in dry districts. Mixed native planting is slower but usually easier to defend in a sustainability report.

## Co-benefits

Good forestry and agroforestry projects can supply fruit, fodder and timber income to farmers, improve soil and water retention, and create habitat. On a listing these appear as UN Sustainable Development Goal tiles, often Life on Land (SDG 15) and No Poverty (SDG 1). Treat the tiles as a starting point and ask how each one is evidenced; our article on [SDGs and co-benefits](/knowledge-base/sdgs-and-co-benefits/) explains what good evidence looks like.

For agroforestry in India, the most useful question is how farmers share in the carbon revenue. A clear benefit-sharing arrangement, written into the project documents, protects both the trees and the buyer's reputation.

## What to ask the seller

| Ask for | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Registry ID and methodology | Lets you look the project up and confirm it is a planting project, not forest protection |
| Buffer percentage and any reversal history | Shows how the standard rated the permanence risk |
| Latest verification report | Confirms the volume was checked, and when |
| Planting dates and area by year | Explains why vintages are the size they are |
| Land tenure and farmer agreements | Shows who owns the carbon and how communities benefit |
| For forward lots, the expected issuance schedule | Tells you what happens if growth is slower than planned |

Standards such as [Verra](https://verra.org/) and [Gold Standard](https://www.goldstandard.org/) publish their forestry rules and buffer requirements, and the registry entry will show past issuances.
