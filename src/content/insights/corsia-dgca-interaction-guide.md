ICAO has no power to fine an Indian airline. It writes the standards; the obligations that actually bind an operator in India arrive through the [Directorate General of Civil Aviation](https://www.dgca.gov.in/), India's CORSIA national authority, under Indian implementing law. How well you work with the DGCA has a lot to do with how smoothly each compliance year runs.

This note is about the working relationship: when you will deal with the authority, what it looks at, and the habits that keep approvals from becoming the thing everyone waits on.

![Responsibility chart showing who owns each CORSIA task between operator, verifier, DGCA and ICAO](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-governance-raci.svg)

## Where the operator and the authority meet

Across a cycle, the DGCA's role touches you at six points:

1. **Monitoring plan approval.** Nothing else can proceed until this is done.
2. **Receiving the Annual Emissions Report**, verified, on the national calendar.
3. **Oversight of verification**, including recognition of accredited bodies.
4. **Reporting to ICAO.** Your figures feed the sectoral growth factor.
5. **Receiving the Emissions Unit Cancellation Report**, which closes the loop on each compliance period.
6. **Enforcement**, under Indian law rather than ICAO's.

The sixth point is widely misread. Penalties are set nationally. A general statement about "CORSIA penalties" means nothing unless it names the State, and for an Indian operator the answer lies in Indian law. Confirm the current position with the authority rather than relying on something written about another country.

It also helps to be clear about what the DGCA does not do. It does not verify your report; that is an accredited independent body, which must also be separate from anyone who advised you on the report. And it does not pick or pre-approve your emissions units. Eligibility is set by the ICAO criteria and evidenced by you; the authority receives the cancellation report after the fact. On verifiers, check the recognition requirements rather than assuming any accredited body will do. Our guide to [choosing a verification body in India](/insights/corsia-verification-body-selection-india/) covers that step.

## The monitoring plan: the first and biggest interaction

This is the submission with the most at stake, because the method it fixes is the one you will be verified against. A reviewer will look at six things:

- **Why this method.** Why this fuel use monitoring method for your fleet and systems? A choice without reasons invites questions.
- **Named data sources.** "Our fuel system" is not a source. Give the system its name.
- **Scope rules.** Exactly how a flight is sorted as in or out of scope, clearly enough that a new employee would apply it the same way.
- **Fleet list.** Whether the aeroplanes listed match your operating certificate.
- **Gap procedures.** Whether they are set out in advance or left for later.
- **Roles.** Whether responsibilities are real functions or just an organogram.

Two timing facts shape everything. The plan must be approved **before the reporting year it covers begins**, and a first submission rarely goes through without questions. That is normal and says nothing bad about the plan. It does mean that an operator filing in November for a January start has left no time for a round of revisions. Build in generous time, especially for a first plan or an unusual structure. The contents themselves are covered section by section in our [monitoring plan guide](/insights/corsia-emissions-monitoring-plan-guide/).

### Getting a plan through with fewer rounds

Authorities have limited staff, and a well-prepared document moves faster, not through favour but because it generates fewer questions.

| Do | Instead of |
|---|---|
| Explain the method choice against your actual fleet and systems | Stating the method and leaving it there |
| Name systems, with versions where relevant | Generic references that prompt a clarification request |
| Make the fleet list match the certificate exactly | A near match, which is the easiest error for a reviewer to spot |
| Assign roles to functions, e.g. "Head of Flight Operations" | Personal names that need an amendment when people move |
| Attach a short note on what changed since the last version, and why | Making the reviewer compare two documents line by line |
| Describe what you will really do, at your scale | Copying a large carrier's plan, which is harder to review and to follow |

Another operator's plan is useful as a structural reference and dangerous as a template. The plan has to describe your systems and your data; a gap between the plan and what you actually do is the most common finding of all.

## The yearly rhythm

Deadlines sit in national implementing law and can move. Read the DGCA's published calendar each year instead of assuming a date shared by every State, or that this year looks like last. The pattern that repeats:

- the monitoring plan approved before the reporting year starts
- the verified Annual Emissions Report after year end, by the national deadline
- the Emissions Unit Cancellation Report after each compliance period closes

Put slack into every one. A verification finding that needs a correction and re-verification can use up weeks, and a calendar with no margin turns a technical issue into a compliance failure. Our [compliance calendar](/insights/corsia-reporting-deadlines-and-calendar/) sets out how to plan the year.

## How to behave between submissions

**Talk early.** A question raised with plenty of time is a short delay. The same question a fortnight before year end is a problem.

**Ask rather than guess.** Where the implementation detail is unclear, authorities generally prefer a question beforehand to a correction afterwards.

**Stay consistent.** A position in one submission should match the next. A monitoring plan and a report that disagree invite scrutiny of both.

**Warn early about foreseeable trouble.** If eligible units cannot be sourced in time, or a verification finding will not close before the deadline, the authority can respond to a warning far better than to a missed date.

You do not need a consultant to deal with the DGCA; the submission is yours either way. An adviser who knows the authority's expectations can shorten the process, but that is a convenience, not a requirement.

## Situations worth raising before you file

Several arrangements come up often enough in India to be familiar and are unusual enough that general guidance does not settle them:

| Situation | Why it needs a conversation |
|---|---|
| A group with several Air Operator Certificates | Which entity is the operator decides who reports what |
| Wet lease or ACMI arrangements | The operating certificate, not the commercial contract, decides the operator |
| Emissions close to the 10,000-tonne threshold | The answer may change from one year to the next |
| A mixed network, mostly domestic | The covered share may be small relative to total flying |
| A new entrant | No history and no data against which to test a method |
| Discovering mid-year that CORSIA applies | Being late is handled far better openly than by rebuilding data quietly |

In each case, a position documented and agreed with the authority beforehand is worth much more than a reasonable position argued after the event. For the new-operator case, see [your first year of CORSIA compliance](/insights/corsia-first-year-compliance-india/).

## Keeping the paper trail

Letters and emails to and from the regulator turn into evidence later. When a verifier asks why a particular treatment was used, "the DGCA confirmed this in writing on this date" is a complete answer. Treat the correspondence accordingly.

Keep one register, held with the compliance file rather than in anyone's personal folder. An illustrative entry might look like this:

| Field | Example entry |
|---|---|
| Date | 14 March |
| Subject | Operator determination for a wet-leased aircraft |
| Our question | Which AOC reports sectors flown under the lease |
| Authority's position | As stated in the reply, quoted in full |
| Form | Written reply; earlier phone call confirmed back by email |
| People involved | Compliance lead, authority reviewer by role |
| Where it is used | Monitoring plan section on fleet and scope |

A few rules make the register worth having:

- Save the correspondence itself, not a summary. Exact wording matters when a clarification is relied on years later.
- Write down verbal guidance, and for anything material, confirm it back by email. A position agreed on a call and never recorded cannot be evidenced.
- Note who took part. People change on both sides.
- Keep it where the team can find it. The usual failure is a well-kept record that leaves with the person who kept it.

The register costs almost nothing while you keep it and cannot be rebuilt afterwards. It also stops the organisation drifting into a different position in a later submission, the kind of inconsistency that draws attention to everything else.
