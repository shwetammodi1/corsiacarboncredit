Follow a single fuel docket from a flight that matters to all three regimes. The same kilograms of kerosene show up in a CORSIA emissions report, may feed an EU ETS report if the route falls in European scope, and are part of the footprint behind the airline's net-zero pledge. One number, three audiences, three sets of rules.

Most of the pain in multi-scheme compliance comes from treating those three audiences as three separate projects. This article is about running them together without letting them blur.

![Comparison of CORSIA with the EU ETS and other aviation carbon schemes](/images/corsia/corsia-scheme-comparison.svg)

For the conceptual differences between the schemes (instrument, scope, legal force, price visibility), read [CORSIA vs EU ETS, UK ETS and the voluntary market](/knowledge-base/corsia-vs-eu-ets-and-other-schemes/). Here we deal with the day-to-day operating problem.

## Same fuel, different paperwork

The assumption that costs the most is that one emissions dataset, packaged once, will do for everyone. The raw fuel and flight data is largely shared. Almost everything built on top of it is not:

- **CORSIA** needs an Emissions Monitoring Plan approved by your national authority (the DGCA for an Indian carrier), follows ICAO Annex 16 Volume IV, covers international flights on covered route pairs, is checked by a verifier accredited under the CORSIA framework, and settles by cancelling eligible units and filing a cancellation report.
- **EU ETS** needs its own monitoring plan approved by the administering member State, follows the EU MRV Regulation, covers flights in European scope, uses verifiers accredited under the EU framework, and settles by surrendering allowances.
- **Voluntary commitments** need no approved plan. They usually follow the GHG Protocol, typically cover all operations, rely on assurance standards, and settle by retiring credits and disclosing them.

Three documents, three authorities, three verification regimes. One pool of underlying data.

## Build the data layer once

The arrangement that works is a single authoritative emissions dataset, with each scheme's boundaries and calculations applied on top of it.

The arrangement we more often find is three teams reconciling the same source records separately. That wastes effort, and it has a worse side effect: the three outputs drift. **When your own reports contradict each other, a verifier or auditor will start there.**

The shared layer tends to have no owner, because it sits between flight operations, finance and sustainability. Giving it one is usually the best return on effort a multi-scheme operator can find.

Expect the reported figures to differ between schemes even when the data layer is clean. The fuel-to-CO2 conversion is consistent, but scope rules, monitoring methods and reporting boundaries are not, so the same flight can legitimately appear with different numbers. Explain that in your own documentation before someone asks.

## Which scheme catches which flight

Take four routes an Indian carrier might operate alongside a European partner:

- **Paris to Frankfurt** sits in EU ETS scope and outside CORSIA.
- **Delhi to Mumbai** is domestic, so neither scheme applies.
- **Delhi to Frankfurt** falls under CORSIA if both States participate; EU ETS scope for such flights has been narrowed, and whether and how it applies depends on the arrangement in force.
- **Delhi to Singapore** falls under CORSIA if both States participate and is outside the EU ETS.

The interface between the two schemes was designed to limit duplicate obligations on the same emissions, and it has been changed several times as CORSIA developed. So do not assume a flight is double-covered, and do not assume it is exempt. Classify your actual network route by route, write down why, and revisit it when either scheme changes. A verifier will want the reasoning; "we assumed" is not reasoning. [CORSIA scope and thresholds](/knowledge-base/corsia-scope-and-thresholds/) covers the CORSIA side.

## Two budgets, not one carbon line

The instruments behave nothing alike as financial exposures.

**EU allowances** trade on a liquid exchange, with continuous public prices and a forward curve. Treasury can hedge them with ordinary tools.

**CORSIA units** trade bilaterally, without a dependable public reference price, and supply depends on governments deciding to authorise transfers. **You cannot hedge them the same way.**

A board that sees one "carbon cost" figure is being shown a blend of a manageable risk and an unmanageable one. Report them separately. [Carbon pricing and airlines](/insights/carbon-pricing-impact-on-airlines/) looks at how this reaches the income statement.

## One instrument, one claim

This is where the reputational damage is done.

Allowances surrendered under an ETS and units cancelled for CORSIA each discharge a **legal obligation**. They cannot then also support a voluntary claim such as carbon neutrality.

An airline that surrenders allowances, cancels CORSIA units and then announces it is carbon neutral on the strength of those same instruments has claimed them twice. Disclosure regulators increasingly look for exactly this, and it is not a hard finding to make. Keep compliance reported as compliance. If you make a voluntary claim, back it with separately retired credits.

## Sustainable fuel is the exception

SAF is the one lever that counts in all three places:

- under CORSIA, qualifying CORSIA Eligible Fuels cut the offsetting requirement directly
- under the EU ETS, SAF reduces the emissions that need allowances
- for voluntary commitments, it is the most defensible claim an airline has, because it reduces the emission rather than compensating for it elsewhere

Each regime has its own documentation, and meeting one does not meet another. The same fuel cannot be claimed twice. But a single purchase can serve all three, so **SAF judged against one scheme alone looks dearer than it really is.** Model it against your total exposure.

## Who owns what

Staffing tends to go wrong in one of two ways. Some operators treat CORSIA as a bolt-on to their EU ETS process, which it is not: different plan, different authority, different verifiers, and settlement by procurement rather than a screen purchase. Others build parallel teams and duplicate the shared data work.

| Area | Shared or separate | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel and flight data layer | Shared | One data owner |
| Scope mapping | Separate per scheme; heavy once, light upkeep | Each scheme's compliance lead |
| Monitoring plans | Separate documents, each needing real drafting | Each scheme's compliance lead |
| Verification | Separate, accredited per framework; only the data preparation is shared | Each scheme's compliance lead, sequenced jointly |
| Allowance management | Separate | Treasury |
| CORSIA unit purchasing | Separate, with due diligence | Procurement |
| Voluntary claims | Separate | Sustainability |
| Watching the interface | Shared | One named person |

Treasury and procurement are different jobs. Allowances are managed against a liquid market; eligible credits are bought more like a service than a commodity, with a diligence workstream attached. Handing both to one person because both say "carbon" usually means one is done badly.

Verification calendars are set by different authorities and the windows overlap. Verifier capacity is tight in both regimes, so sequence the two deliberately.

## Watch the join between the schemes

Three things move flights from one regime to another without you changing anything in your operation:

1. **EU ETS aviation scope**, which has been narrowed, extended and reviewed repeatedly alongside CORSIA's development.
2. **CORSIA participation**, since a State joining or leaving changes which route pairs are covered.
3. **Exemption arrangements**, the policy mechanisms that stop the same emissions being charged twice. They have been adjusted before.

A quarterly look at both regimes' published positions is enough. It only happens if someone's job description says so.

## Where first-year effort lands

For an operator running both schemes for the first time, the split usually looks like this. Data reconciliation is the largest piece, and it is shared. Scope mapping is a one-off effort followed by light upkeep. Each monitoring plan takes its own drafting. Verification is done twice, with only the data preparation in common. Settlement is completely different: surrender is administrative, while buying and cancelling credits is a procurement exercise.

The budgeting rule that follows: fund the data layer once, and generously; fund everything downstream twice.

::: accordion Can EU allowances be used for CORSIA?
No. They are different instruments serving different systems.

::: accordion Can one verifier handle both schemes?
Only if accredited under the applicable framework for each. Check before engaging.
:::

It depends on the shape of your network. CORSIA is driven by growth; an ETS by total in-scope emissions.
