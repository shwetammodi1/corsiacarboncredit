The rules are the same. CORSIA measures CO2, not payload, so a tonne emitted by a freighter counts exactly like a tonne from a full passenger widebody. What is different for a cargo airline is the effort needed to produce an emissions figure a verifier will accept. Passenger schedules are tidy; freight operations are not.

![CORSIA across different types of operator](/images/corsia-consultant/hero-corsia-operator-segments.svg)

## One untidy week

To see why, follow an **illustrative** Indian freighter through a peak-season week.

On Monday it flies its scheduled sector from an Indian hub to a Gulf station. On Tuesday a load becomes available at a station it does not normally serve, so it is routed there instead of home. On Wednesday it flies empty to reposition. On Thursday it operates a one-off charter under a separate commercial arrangement. At the weekend the group brings in a leased aircraft with crew to cover demand.

Each of those days creates a monitoring question:

- **The unplanned station** produces a flight record matching no timetable. Scope classification built around a published schedule will miss it.
- **The empty repositioning leg** is an international flight and counts, but earns no revenue, so it is the leg most likely to be missing from a commercially-oriented flight list.
- **The charter** may sit under a different arrangement, so the question of which entity is the CORSIA operator has to be answered per arrangement, not once.
- **The leased-in aircraft** raises the ACMI question: who is the operator when aircraft, crew, maintenance and insurance come from one party and the flying is sold by another?
- **The mix of types** (converted freighters, seasonal capacity) affects which monitoring method your data can support, because data availability varies by type.

A passenger carrier's monitoring problem is mostly one of volume. A cargo carrier's is one of irregularity.

## The scope tests, with a freight lens

The same five tests apply as for any operator. Domestic freight is entirely out of scope. The 5,700 kg mass threshold catches every commercial freighter. The operator must exceed 10,000 tonnes of CO2 a year from international flights. Exempt flight types are removed. And offsets are owed only on route pairs where both States participate.

Two of these deserve extra care in cargo.

**The operator threshold arrives sooner than fleet size suggests.** Long-haul freighters burn heavily, and a small fleet can pass 10,000 tonnes on international sectors alone. Calculate it; do not infer exemption from a modest fleet.

**Humanitarian exemption depends on the operation, not the goods.** A relief flight genuinely operated as humanitarian is exempt. Commercial carriage of goods that happen to be aid-related is not. Record the basis flight by flight rather than asserting it for the fleet.

## Who is the operator

For cargo groups, this is the first real piece of work, and getting it wrong is costly to undo. The principle is simple: the operator is the holder of the **Air Operator Certificate** under which the flight was conducted. Aircraft ownership and the commercial contract do not decide it.

| Arrangement | Whose emissions |
|---|---|
| Group with several AOCs | Each certificate is a separate operator with its own threshold test, plan and report. A group-level report will be rejected. |
| Capacity wet-leased in | Generally flown under your certificate, so yours |
| Aircraft wet-leased out, flown on the lessee's certificate | The lessee's |
| Interline and block-space deals | The operating carrier's, not the seller of capacity |

Write down the determination and the reasoning behind it. It will be tested at verification, possibly by someone who does not know your commercial structure.

## Choosing a method the outstations can feed

The classic cargo failure is a monitoring method picked on the strength of hub data. It works perfectly at the main base and falls apart at outstations that record uplift differently, or at charter destinations with no process at all.

Test against your worst month, not a typical one. Take a peak month with charters, diversions and outstation uplifts and try to produce the required figures without rebuilding anything by hand. If one month needs heroics, twelve will not hold.

Where per-flight tank data is simply unavailable across parts of the operation, a block-hour based method may be the sustainable choice even though it is less precise. Verification checks that you followed your approved plan, not that you chose the most sophisticated option. The methods are compared in [fuel use monitoring methods](/knowledge-base/corsia-fuel-monitoring-methods/).

## Reconciliation carries more weight in cargo

![A CORSIA data pipeline built to survive verification](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-data-architecture.svg)

Three sources describe each uplift: the handling agent's docket, the flight operations record and the fuel invoice in the finance system. They will not agree exactly, because each measures something slightly different at a slightly different time. In cargo they disagree more often than elsewhere.

Write the rule for settling each type of disagreement before the year begins, not case by case afterwards. And reconcile monthly: finding in month two that an outstation's dockets are not arriving leaves ten months to fix it; finding it in the reporting window leaves none.

## Getting data out of handling agents

Much of a cargo operator's fuel record starts outside the company, with handlers and fuel suppliers at stations you may serve only occasionally. Verification will test the completeness of your flight list and the traceability of each fuel figure, and "the handler never sent it" is no defence.

| What to arrange | Why |
|---|---|
| Data requirements written into the handling agreement: format, fields, level of detail, delivery schedule | Costs nothing at negotiation, very hard to add later |
| Uplift reported per flight, not per day | A daily total across several movements cannot be split between flights without assumptions, and assumptions are what verifiers probe |
| An agreed correction route and response time | Chasing a six-month-old docket at an outstation rarely works |
| Monthly reconciliation with each handler | A pattern of gaps is fixable early and permanent late |
| Keeping the handler's original docket | When a figure is questioned, the source document is the answer |

For ad hoc charter destinations with no standing agreement, put the data requirement on the trip planning checklist so it is captured at the time. More on this in [fuel supplier and handling agent data](/insights/corsia-fuel-supplier-data-coordination/).

## The 2027 question for freight networks

![Preparing for mandatory participation in the second phase](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-2027-readiness.svg)

Cargo routes follow trade flows rather than passenger demand, and several of the States they serve do not currently participate. From 2027, participation becomes mandatory for States above the activity thresholds, and route pairs that owe nothing today start to count. For a freight operator with heavy traffic to such destinations, the rise can be proportionally larger than for a passenger carrier on established routes between participating States.

Model it against your actual network. The effect is entirely network-specific, and a percentage uplift will be wrong one way or the other.

## Other questions cargo operators ask

**EU ETS.** Freight operators serving Europe may face both schemes. They use different instruments, credits under CORSIA and allowances under the EU ETS, and scopes are arranged to limit double obligation on the same emissions. Map your network against both explicitly. See [operating under CORSIA and the EU ETS](/insights/corsia-vs-eu-ets-voluntary-market/).

**SAF.** It can reduce the obligation where qualifying CORSIA Eligible Fuel is available at your stations, certified under an approved scheme, with documented chain of custody. Availability at cargo hubs varies widely.

## Where outside help pays, and what to keep

Advice earns its fee in five places: operator entity determination for complex groups, which is close to legal analysis most operators have never needed; method selection tested against real data including the awkward stations, the single most expensive avoidable error; reconciliation rule design; threshold assessment for operators near 10,000 tonnes; and second-phase modelling of a freight network.
