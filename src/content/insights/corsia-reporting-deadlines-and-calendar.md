Ask a compliance lead when their CORSIA deadline is and they will tell you the submission date. Ask when their year actually goes wrong and, if they have been through a bad cycle, the answer is usually "some time in late January". The external date is fixed and known. What decides whether you meet it calmly is a chain of internal dates that nobody outside the airline ever sees.

This piece lays out that chain.

![CORSIA compliance year laid out month by month](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-reporting-calendar.svg)

## Get the external date right first

The verified emissions report goes to the national authority in the first quarter after the compliance year. For Indian operators, the operative dates are set by the [DGCA](https://www.dgca.gov.in/) in the applicable Civil Aviation Requirement. Put those dates in your calendar, not the indicative months used in this article. Everything below assumes a calendar-year compliance period; shift it if yours differs. The wider timetable of periods and phases is in [compliance periods and deadlines](/knowledge-base/corsia-compliance-periods-and-deadlines/).

## The countdown to submission

Counting back from the submission date, these are the internal milestones we set:

1. **11–12 weeks out:** compliance year closed in all systems.
2. **10 weeks out:** data compiled and reconciled.
3. **9 weeks out:** internal review finished.
4. **8 weeks out:** evidence pack complete.
5. **7–8 weeks out:** verification fieldwork begins.
6. **5–6 weeks out:** fieldwork finished.
7. **3–4 weeks out:** every finding closed.
8. **1–2 weeks out:** verification report issued.
9. **Deadline:** verified report submitted.

Look at where the pressure lands. If the year is not closed and reconciled by late January, every later step squeezes into the same fixed window, and the step that gets squeezed hardest is closing findings, which is the one least able to take it.

## The year, quarter by quarter

### First quarter: finish last year, start this one

**January.** Close the previous year, compile the data. Monitoring for the new year is already running.

**February.** Internal review and reconciliation; finance and compliance assemble the evidence pack.

**March.** Verification fieldwork. Findings are raised and closed.

The owners change during these months: the compliance lead in January, compliance and finance in February, the verification body in March.

### Second quarter: submit, then learn

**April.** The prior-year report goes in, signed off by the accountable manager. Actions arising from findings start.

**May.** Update the monitoring plan for anything verification exposed.

**June.** A half-year data health check, including an audit of outstation records. This is one of the two most valuable months in the calendar. It tells you, with six months still to go, whether completeness and gap handling are in shape.

### Third quarter: look ahead to money

**July.** Quarterly plan review: capture fleet and route changes.

**August.** Where offsetting applies, estimate the current year's position with finance.

**September.** The commercial team assesses the unit market and confirms the registry account is active.

### Fourth quarter: the month that saves the next one

**October** is the other high-value month. Most of what makes the next first quarter bearable is done here:

- **Appoint next cycle's verifier.** Capacity shrinks near deadlines and fees tend to be better early. An early appointment also lets the verifier start their strategic analysis before your data arrives.
- **Review the data before the year closes.** Nine or ten months is enough to judge completeness, gaps and outstation records. A problem found in October can be fixed. The same problem found in February is a finding.
- **Check the plan is current** for every fleet, route, system or entity change so far.
- **Document this year's scope**: which route pairs carry an offsetting requirement, and why, while the reasoning is fresh.
- **Test retention.** Can you actually retrieve every source document for the year, outstations included?

**November.** Data owners fix whatever October found.

**December.** Prepare for year end and get the compliance lead, finance contact and data owners booked for the first quarter before other year-end plans fill their diaries.

With June and October done properly, January becomes compiling data you already understand rather than discovering what is wrong with it.

## What runs underneath every month

![The annual CORSIA MRV cycle](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-mrv-annual-cycle.svg)

None of the dates hold unless a short monthly routine runs all year:

| Check | Looks for |
|---|---|
| Completeness | Every sector flown has a fuel record |
| Anomalies by aircraft type | Values out of line with the type's normal burn |
| Units and duplicates | Kilograms entered as litres; the same uplift counted twice |
| Reconciliation to fuel purchases | Reported fuel that does not match what was bought |
| Gap log | Every substitution recorded, with approval |
| Plan-relevant changes | Anything to carry into the quarterly review |

Roughly a morning a month, with evidence kept. Skipping it saves nothing. The same work comes back in a month with no spare time, bringing findings with it. See [fuel data quality management](/insights/corsia-fuel-data-quality-management/) for how to run each check.

## The offsetting track

Where an offsetting requirement applies, a second line runs beside the reporting cycle. It has no fixed months of its own; it is driven by a sequence:

1. Estimate the requirement as soon as the emissions position is clear.
2. Assess the market and get budget approved well before the cancellation deadline.
3. Complete due diligence on units **before** contracting.
4. Contract, leaving time for registry transfer.
5. Have the registry account ready before any transfer is due.
6. Cancel and file the evidence by the applicable deadline.

The registry account is the item that most often trips operators. Opening and activating one involves papers and approvals and cannot be rushed at the end. Due diligence also takes real time, since eligibility, vintage, corresponding adjustment and registry status all need checking. The [procurement process for Indian operators](/insights/corsia-unit-procurement-process-india/) sets out the steps.

## Changes to log when they happen

Keep a running list, reviewed each quarter by a named owner, of anything that should trigger a plan review:

- aircraft added or removed, or a change in lease status
- a new station, especially an outstation with a new handling agent
- a change to a source system or a reporting extract
- a new AOC, merger or restructure
- changes to the requirements or the participating-State list
- a process change made in response to an earlier finding

Trying to recall a year of changes in January is how gaps between plan and practice reach the verifier.

## One page, held centrally

The calendar itself should be a single document containing:

- the external submission deadline for the current cycle
- each internal milestone with an owner and a date
- the verifier's name, contact and booked dates
- the monthly check schedule and who does it
- the year's plan change log
- offsetting track dates, where they apply
- where records are retained, and who owns that

Keep it somewhere shared, not in one person's inbox. If the compliance lead leaves in November, the calendar should not leave with them.

## Groups with more than one AOC

For a group with several operating entities, calendars multiply rather than combine:

- each entity may need its own monitoring plan, report and verification opinion
- milestones have to be staggered, because a verification body cannot field every entity at once
- a shared fuel accounting team must show clearly which figures belong to which entity
- aircraft moving between group entities need documenting on both sides

What works: one central calendar with a column per entity, one compliance lead, and a deputy for each entity. What does not: finding out in February that two entities were counting on the same person in the same fortnight.

## Short answers

**When do we appoint the verifier?** In the fourth quarter, before the year closes.

**How long should verification take in the plan?** Allow seven to eight weeks before the deadline for fieldwork, findings and the report.

**What if the deadline is at risk?** Missing it is a compliance failure with consequences set by the national authority. Talk to the authority early, not on the last day.

**Who owns the calendar?** The compliance lead, with the accountable manager able to see it.
