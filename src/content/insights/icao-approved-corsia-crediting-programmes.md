Most buyers treat programme approval as a box ticked during due diligence. It is closer to a credit rating: a status that someone else can change, on their own timetable, while your money is tied up in it. For CORSIA, that someone is the ICAO Council.

This article deals with living with that. It does not list which programmes are approved; the only authoritative list is ICAO's own, and a copy in an article goes stale. For how approval works and what the Technical Advisory Body assesses, see [ICAO-approved crediting programmes](/knowledge-base/corsia-approved-crediting-programmes/).

![ICAO-approved crediting programmes for CORSIA](/images/corsia/corsia-approved-programmes.svg)

## Who is exposed

**Operators holding units.** If the issuing programme's approval lapses after you buy but before you cancel, whether you can still use those units depends on Council decisions and on how your national authority treats the case. That is not a question you want to be researching in the week of your deadline.

**Developers partway through a project.** A project several years into development under a programme that loses approval is in serious trouble. Moving to another programme can sometimes be done, but slowly and at a cost.

The remedy is the same for both: check the status when it matters, and do not put everything in one place.

## A scenario, followed through

The programmes and dates here are illustrative and do not describe any real decision.

An Indian operator buys units for the current compliance period in three tranches, from Programmes A, B and C, in the first quarter of a year. It plans to cancel everything in the fourth quarter.

**Purchase.** Before each purchase, the compliance lead opens the [ICAO CORSIA emissions units page](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/CORSIA-Emissions-Units.aspx), confirms each programme's status and reads any conditions. Programme B's approval is conditional, restricted to certain methodologies; she notes that the units being bought fall under an included methodology. She saves a dated copy of the page with each purchase file.

**Mid-year.** At the quarterly check, she sees that a new condition has been attached to Programme C, affecting one project type. One of the operator's tranches from Programme C is of that type.

**Response.** She does not panic-sell or write the units off. She:

1. asks the national authority, in writing, how units issued while approval was unconditional will be treated
2. checks the purchase contract, which contains a warranty of status at delivery and a substitution remedy
3. notes that the units remain valid in the voluntary market, so the worst case is a loss of value, not a total loss
4. begins sourcing replacement units from Programme A as a fallback, and informs the authority early that the position is being managed

**Cancellation.** Before cancelling in the fourth quarter, she checks all three programmes again and saves the page. Whatever the answer on the Programme C tranche, only a third of the holding was ever in question, and she had months, not days, to deal with it.

Two things made the difference: the checks happened at the right moments, and the holding was spread.

## When to look

Four checkpoints, each with a reason:

- **Before opening a registry account**, so you do not onboard with a programme that has already lapsed.
- **At each transaction**, because that is the status that governs the purchase.
- **Before cancelling**, if time has passed since purchase. This is the check most often skipped. Units bought early in a year and cancelled late have been exposed to every Council decision in between.
- **Periodically while holding stock**, so a change is spotted while there is still time to act.

## How to look

- **Only at the source.** ICAO's emissions units page is the authoritative record. A seller's website, an article or a bookmarked list is a snapshot of a past moment.
- **Read past the name.** Approval can be full or conditional. Conditions often restrict methodologies or project types, and a programme's presence on the list does not tell you whether a condition reaches your units.
- **Note any reassessment date**, so you know when the position could next change.
- **Save what you saw, with the date.** A link in a compliance file does not prove what the page said on the day you relied on it, and a verifier may ask exactly that years later.

## What the file should show

For any parcel of units, someone who was not involved should be able to answer three questions from the file alone:

| Question | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Was the programme approved when we bought? | A saved, dated copy of the ICAO page from around the transaction date |
| Was approval full or conditional, and did a condition touch these units? | The condition text, saved, plus a short note on why it did or did not apply |
| Was it still approved when we cancelled? | A second saved copy from the cancellation date |

Three saved pages and two notes. Minutes at the time, and impossible to reconstruct later.

## Spreading the exposure

This is the structural protection, and it is cheap.

- **Use more than one programme.** With three, one lapse touches a third of your holding. Two already reduces single-point exposure meaningfully; three is comfortable for a large obligation. Past that, the administration outweighs the benefit.
- **Hold for shorter periods.** Every month units sit uncancelled is a month in which status can change.
- **Vary vintage and host State as well.** Approval is one regulatory variable among several. Concentration on any one of them is a risk.

For developers the equivalent is a **voluntary market fallback**, so that a lapse costs the CORSIA premium rather than the whole route to market.

## Putting it in the contract

Where settlement comes some time after signature, the agreement should cover a change of status in between.

- **Allocate the risk.** Neither party caused a Council decision, so decide in advance who carries it.
- **Choose the remedy.** Substitution with units from a programme still approved is usually worth more to a buyer than a refund. A refund leaves you needing units in a market you have just found to be unreliable.
- **Warrant status at delivery**, not only at signature.

On forward deals running a year or longer, this clause belongs alongside the authorisation and vintage clauses. Together they cover the three regulatory risks a well-drafted CORSIA contract should address. [Offtake agreement terms](/insights/corsia-offtake-agreement-terms/) covers the rest.

## If a lapse does happen

It has happened before. In order:

1. **Ask your national authority first.** Transitional arrangements sometimes cover units issued while approval was in force, and the answer may be better than you expect.
2. **Remember the voluntary market.** The units remain valid credits there, so the loss may be partial.
3. **Read your contracts** for a status warranty and a remedy.
4. **Plan replacement realistically**, and tell your authority early if compliance is genuinely at risk. A problem raised in advance is handled very differently from one found at the deadline.

Units already cancelled and reported against a compliance period are generally settled. The exposure is on stock not yet cancelled.

## Choosing a programme as a developer

Approval status is one of six things to weigh:

1. **Status now:** approved, and full or conditional?
2. **Stability:** how long has it held, and when is the next reassessment?
3. **Methodology:** is there one for your activity, and is it under review?
4. **Adjustment handling:** does the programme's process fit your host State's?
5. **Buyers:** will airlines already have accounts there?
6. **Cost and queues:** registration fees, levies, realistic validation times.

Choosing the standard you used last time is habit, and it is how developers find themselves mid-project under a programme whose approval was already conditional when they registered. ## Make someone own it

Status checks fail when they depend on memory. Build them into three places:

- the **purchase checklist**, as a gate before diligence continues, not a tick afterwards
- the **pre-cancellation reconciliation**, as a mandatory recheck if more than a few months have passed
- a **quarterly review** of every programme you hold units in or have accounts with, owned by whoever holds the compliance obligation

The quarterly review takes about fifteen minutes. It is also the natural moment to check vintage window decisions and participation list changes, since all three are set by the Council and all three can move your position without anything changing in your own operation.

::: accordion Can approval risk be insured?
Not as a standard product. Spread across programmes, hold for shorter periods, and allocate the risk in the contract with a substitution remedy.
:::

::: accordion How often does approval change?
Rarely, but it has. Conditions are attached and lifted more often than full approvals lapse.
:::

::: accordion Does a conditional approval make units unusable?
Not necessarily. Read the condition; it may exclude particular methodologies rather than the whole programme.
:::
