**How do you calculate carbon credits?** You estimate what emissions would have been without a project (the **baseline**). Then you subtract what the project actually emits, plus any **leakage** and other deductions the methodology requires, and convert the result into **tonnes of CO₂ equivalent (tCO₂e)**. After independent verification, the programme issues credits, generally **one credit per tonne of CO₂e**. For how credits are used in aviation, see our [CORSIA Carbon Credits](https://corsiacarboncredit.in/) resources.

Careful calculation matters because each credit is a promise that a real tonne of emissions was avoided or removed. Overestimates mean buyers pay for reductions that never happened. This guide focuses on the **formulas and worked calculations**. For a broader explanation of monitoring and verification, see our companion guide on [how carbon credits are measured](/blog/how-to-measure-carbon-credits/).

## What Are Carbon Credits?

A **carbon credit** is a certificate issued by a carbon-crediting programme. It represents a quantified, verified reduction or removal of greenhouse gases.

- **One credit generally equals one metric tonne of CO₂e**, under the issuing programme's rules.
- **Reduction credits** come from preventing emissions, such as replacing coal power with wind or capturing landfill methane.
- **Removal credits** come from taking CO₂ out of the air and storing it, such as growing forests, biochar or direct air capture.

Credits are linked to emissions because they let a buyer compensate for emissions elsewhere. That only works if the calculation behind each credit is accurate, conservative and checked.

## How Do You Calculate Carbon Credits?

1. **Identify the project:** what activity reduces or removes emissions, and over what period.
2. **Establish the baseline:** what would most likely have happened without the project, following the methodology's rules.
3. **Measure project emissions:** emissions the project itself causes, such as fuel used to run equipment.
4. **Calculate the difference:** baseline minus project emissions.
5. **Deduct leakage, uncertainty and other required adjustments.**
6. **Apply the crediting methodology**, the approved rulebook with the exact equations and parameters.
7. **Complete validation and verification** by an accredited independent auditor.
8. **Issue credits** through the programme's registry after approval.

The exact process depends on the **project type, methodology and standard**.

## Carbon Credit Calculation Formula

The core idea is:

> **Emission reductions = Baseline emissions − Project emissions**

A simplified version with adjustments:

> **Net emission reductions = Baseline emissions − Project emissions − Leakage − Other applicable deductions**

This is an **educational illustration, not a universal formula**. Real methodologies add terms for:

- uncertainty, through conservative discounts
- reversal risk, through buffer pool contributions for forestry and soil projects
- leakage, which can be market or activity shifting
- the monitoring boundary, meaning which sources and gases are included

The result for each monitoring period is expressed in **tCO₂e**. Each verified tonne can then become one credit, subject to the programme's issuance rules.

## Understanding CO₂ Equivalent (CO₂e) in Carbon Credit Calculations

Different greenhouse gases trap different amounts of heat. **CO₂ equivalent (CO₂e)** expresses all of them in the same unit: the amount of CO₂ that would cause the same warming.

The conversion uses **global warming potential (GWP)**, which measures a gas's warming effect over a set period, usually 100 years, relative to CO₂:

> **CO₂e = Quantity of greenhouse gas × Applicable global warming potential**

GWP values come from IPCC assessment reports:

- The **Fifth Assessment Report (AR5)** gives 100-year GWPs of **28 for methane** and **265 for nitrous oxide**.
- The **Sixth Assessment Report (AR6)** updates these to about **27–30 for methane**, depending on whether it is fossil or non-fossil, and **273 for nitrous oxide**.

**The correct values are the ones your methodology or reporting framework specifies.** Using the wrong edition changes the result.

### Illustrative CO₂e example (hypothetical)

*Figures are illustrative.* A landfill project prevents **100 tonnes of methane** from escaping in a year.

- Using AR5's GWP of 28: 100 × 28 = **2,800 tCO₂e**
- Using a GWP of 27: 100 × 27 = **2,700 tCO₂e**

That is a 100-tonne difference from the GWP choice alone. It shows why the specified values matter.

## Step-by-Step Carbon Credit Calculation Example

*All figures are hypothetical and are not official programme values.*

| Item | tCO₂e per year |
|---|---|
| Baseline emissions | 10,000 |
| Project emissions | 3,000 |
| Leakage and other applicable deductions | 500 |

Calculation:

1. Baseline − project emissions: 10,000 − 3,000 = 7,000
2. Minus leakage and deductions: 7,000 − 500 = **6,500 tCO₂e**

The project may show **6,500 tonnes of net emission reductions** for the year, **before** any further methodology-specific adjustments. That figure is **not yet 6,500 credits**:

- The monitoring data must be complete and verified by an accredited auditor.
- The programme may apply further deductions, such as a buffer contribution for nature-based projects.
- Credits are issued only after the programme approves the verification, and are then recorded in a registry.

The same logic applies to the cover image's solar example: 1,000 − 400 = 600 tCO₂e is a **potential** amount. A company's own rooftop solar also only earns credits if an applicable methodology covers it, additionality is shown, and the reduction is not claimed twice. Electricity savings, for example, may already count towards the grid operator's or the company's own reported emissions.

## How Are Carbon Credits Calculated for Different Projects?

| Project type | What is measured | Typical calculation basis |
|---|---|---|
| Renewable energy | Electricity delivered to the grid (MWh) | MWh × grid emission factor (tCO₂/MWh) |
| Afforestation and reforestation | Tree growth in sample plots, plus satellite data | Biomass equations convert growth to carbon, then to CO₂ |
| Forest conservation (REDD+) | Forest cover and deforestation rates | Avoided loss compared with a reference deforestation rate |
| Methane capture and landfill gas | Gas flow and methane concentration | Methane destroyed × GWP |
| Energy efficiency | Energy use before and after | Energy saved × fuel or grid emission factor |
| Improved cookstoves | Fuel use per household, stove usage | Fuel saved × emission factor, adjusted for usage rates |
| Carbon capture and removal | Tonnes of CO₂ captured and stored | Metered storage minus energy and process emissions |
| Soil carbon and agriculture | Soil samples, practice data, models | Change in soil organic carbon converted to CO₂ |

### Illustrative renewable energy example (hypothetical)

*Figures are illustrative, not an official emission factor.* A wind farm delivers 20,000 MWh. If the applicable grid emission factor were 0.7 tCO₂/MWh:

20,000 × 0.7 = **14,000 tCO₂e** of baseline emissions displaced, before project emissions and deductions.

## What Factors Affect Carbon Credit Calculations?

- **Baseline scenario selection.** An inflated baseline creates credits for reductions that never happened.
- **Project boundaries.** These decide which sites, gases and sources are counted.
- **Additionality.** If the project would have happened anyway, it delivers no real reduction.
- **Leakage.** Emissions that simply move elsewhere must be deducted.
- **Permanence and reversal risks.** Stored carbon can be released, so programmes hold back buffer credits.
- **Measurement uncertainty.** Imprecise data leads to conservative deductions.
- **Monitoring frequency.** Gaps in monitoring reduce the credits that can be claimed.
- **Data quality.** Uncalibrated meters or missing records weaken results.
- **Applicable methodology.** Each methodology has its own equations and default values.
- **Independent verification.** Auditors may correct or reduce claimed figures.

Together, these factors decide both **how many** credits a project earns and **how reliable** those credits are.

## How Are Carbon Credit Calculations Verified?

- **Project documentation.** A Project Design Document sets out the baseline, boundaries, methodology and monitoring plan.
- **Validation.** An accredited validation and verification body (VVB) checks the design before crediting.
- **Monitoring reports.** The developer reports measured data and calculations for each period.
- **Independent verification.** A VVB checks the data, evidence and calculations, often with a site visit, and raises findings that must be fixed.
- **Programme requirements.** The programme reviews the verification and approves issuance.
- **Registry issuance and tracking.** Credits receive unique serial numbers and are tracked until they are retired.

Programmes such as **Verra's Verified Carbon Standard**, **Gold Standard**, the **American Carbon Registry** and the **Climate Action Reserve** follow this general pattern, but their procedures, terminology and requirements differ. ## How Does Carbon Credit Calculation Relate to CORSIA?

**CORSIA**, the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation, is ICAO's global market-based measure for international aviation's CO₂. It involves **two separate calculations**:

1. **The airline's offsetting requirement.** The airline monitors fuel use on international flights and converts it to CO₂ using ICAO's fuel conversion factors, for example 3.16 tonnes of CO₂ per tonne of Jet-A or Jet-A1. Its State then applies ICAO's growth factors to emissions on routes between participating States. That determines how many units the airline must cancel.
2. **Project emission reductions.** These are calculated by carbon projects as described in this guide.

Accurate quantification matters on both sides. However, **ordinary voluntary credits are not automatically CORSIA eligible**. Units must come from programmes the ICAO Council approves for the relevant compliance period, and must meet unit-level conditions on vintage, scope, host-country authorisation and labelling. Our guide to [CORSIA eligible carbon credits](/blog/corsia-eligible-carbon-credits/) explains these checks.

## Common Mistakes in Carbon Credit Calculations

- **Using an unrealistic baseline** that assumes more emissions than would really occur
- **Ignoring leakage** from displaced activity
- **Overestimating reductions**, for example by assuming full usage of cookstoves or equipment
- **Applying the wrong GWP values**, or mixing values from different IPCC reports
- **Using incomplete monitoring data**, or filling gaps with optimistic estimates
- **Ignoring methodology-specific requirements**, such as default factors or required discounts
- **Treating estimated reductions as verified credits** before verification and issuance
- **Assuming one tonne always becomes one issued credit**, without allowing for buffers and other deductions

## Frequently Asked Questions

::: accordion How do you calculate one carbon credit?
Calculate baseline emissions minus project emissions, then subtract leakage and other required deductions to get net tonnes of CO₂e. Each verified tonne can then be issued as one credit under the programme's rules.
:::

::: accordion How much CO₂ does one carbon credit represent?
Generally one metric tonne of CO₂ equivalent (tCO₂e), which can include other greenhouse gases converted to CO₂ terms, under the issuing programme's rules.
:::

::: accordion What is the formula for calculating carbon credits?
A simplified formula is: net emission reductions = baseline emissions − project emissions − leakage − other applicable deductions. Each methodology has its own detailed equations.
:::

::: accordion How are carbon emissions converted into CO₂ equivalent?
Multiply the quantity of each gas by its global warming potential. For example, 1 tonne of methane at a GWP of 28 equals 28 tCO₂e. Use the GWP values your methodology specifies.
:::

::: accordion Can individuals calculate their own carbon credits?
Individuals can estimate their own carbon footprint, but they cannot create carbon credits themselves. Credits come only from registered projects that follow an approved methodology and pass independent verification.
:::

::: accordion Who verifies carbon credit calculations?
Accredited independent validation and verification bodies check the calculations against the methodology. The carbon-crediting programme then reviews the verification before issuing credits.
:::

::: accordion Are all calculated emission reductions eligible for carbon credits?
No. Reductions must be additional, measured under an approved methodology, verified, and approved by the programme. Deductions such as buffer contributions may also apply before credits are issued.
:::

## Key Takeaways

So, how do you calculate carbon credits? Start with a realistic baseline and measure actual project emissions. Then subtract leakage and other required deductions, convert all gases to CO₂e using the specified GWP values, and have the result independently verified before credits are issued.

Small errors in baselines, GWP values or data quality can greatly overstate results, so methodology rules and verification are essential.
