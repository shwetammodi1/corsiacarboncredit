Most offtake agreements that reach our desk arrive as the seller's draft, with a note from the buyer saying "looks standard, can you check quickly?" They are usually standard. That is the problem. A seller's template is written to put risk on the buyer, and anything it leaves unsaid defaults to the buyer anyway.

So we read these as risk documents, not as orders with a future delivery date. What follows is the order in which we mark them up.

![How risk is split between buyer and seller in a CORSIA offtake agreement](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-offtake-risk-allocation.svg)

## Why buyers sign forward at all

If there were an exchange for CORSIA-eligible units with deep volume, an operator could simply wait and pay the going rate. There is not. Volumes are thin, and supply depends on host-State authorisation decisions that nobody can predict with confidence.

A buyer who waits can discover that the units usable for their compliance period have already been taken by someone else. A forward agreement therefore buys **access to supply that exists**, and the price is almost secondary to that.

The seller has its own reasons: an anchor buyer makes a project easier to finance. Because both parties need the deal, the negotiation tends to be practical rather than hostile, which is a good reason to raise the difficult clauses openly.

## The six clauses we rewrite

| Risk | What the template often says | What we ask for instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Eligibility** | Units will be "CORSIA eligible" | Eligible at the point of cancellation, not just delivery; a named remedy if not, with replacement preferred over refund |
| **Authorisation** | Seller will seek authorisation "as soon as reasonably practicable" | A long-stop date, and a stated consequence if it passes |
| **Vintage** | Nothing | Explicit treatment if the eligible window moves before delivery |
| **Delivery** | Delivery on issuance | A delivery date set back from your cancellation deadline, with margin, and a remedy for lateness |
| **Volume** | A fixed number | A tolerance band and what happens outside it |
| **Documentation** | Serial numbers and a certificate | A listed evidence pack as a contractual obligation |

A few notes on each.

**Eligibility.** A unit can be eligible when it is delivered and not when you come to cancel it, if a programme's approval lapses in between. The warranty has to cover the moment that matters to you. On remedy, a refund is the weaker option: it hands you cash just as you have learned that supply is unreliable. Replacement with equivalent units is worth more.

**Authorisation.** For units not yet authorised, this is the biggest risk in the contract, and neither side controls it, because it is a government decision. The possible positions, from most to least protective for the buyer:

1. Seller carries it fully, with termination and refund.
2. A long-stop date, after which the buyer may walk away.
3. A price adjustment to reflect delay.
4. Buyer accepts non-eligible units at a lower price.

Whichever you land on, write down a date. "Reasonably practicable" is a hope, not a mechanism. Our piece on [corresponding adjustment risk](/insights/corresponding-adjustments-corsia-explained/) goes into how to price it.

**Vintage.** Eligibility windows are fixed by ICAO Council decision and have been changed before. On deals settling more than a few months out, say what happens if the window moves. Silence leaves it with you.

**Delivery.** A unit that arrives after your cancellation deadline is, for compliance purposes, a unit that never arrived. Anchor delivery to your deadline, not to the seller's issuance plan, and specify liquidated damages, replacement at the seller's cost, or termination.

**Volume.** Projects routinely deliver less than forecast: monitoring comes in lower, verification trims issuance, or a period is disallowed. A band avoids a dispute every time that happens.

**Documentation.** List the pack: the host-State authorisation document, evidence of programme approval, the verification report, registry records showing chain of custody, and the project design documentation. Without these you cannot satisfy your own verifier. What a complete pack looks like from the seller's side is set out in [the project documentation pack](/insights/corsia-project-documentation-pack/).

## Commercial terms

- **Price.** Fixed, indexed or collared. Fixed is simplest and puts market movement entirely on one side. Indexation is hard here because there is no dependable index for the eligible segment.
- **Payment.** Tie instalments to milestones such as signing, issuance, authorisation and delivery, not one payment upfront. Use escrow with a counterparty you do not know.
- **Exclusivity.** Can the seller sell the same output to someone else? This matters where issuance itself is uncertain.
- **Right of first refusal** on later issuance, which may be worth having as second-phase demand grows.

## The boilerplate that is not boilerplate

- **Governing law and forum.** Pick something you could actually use against a counterparty in that location. A forum you cannot realistically reach is decoration.
- **Assignment.** Can either party transfer the contract? This matters if the project is sold, and also if your own obligation shrinks and you want to pass the units on.
- **Change in law.** Programme approvals, vintage windows and phase design all change by Council decision. Say what happens if a change undermines the deal.
- **Termination,** including what happens to money already paid.
- **Confidentiality,** with an explicit carve-out allowing disclosure to your verifier and your national authority. You will need it.

## Security when the seller is small

With a first-time developer or a newly formed project company, an unsecured forward commitment is a credit exposure that price terms do nothing to address. The tools, roughly in order of complexity:

- **Parent company guarantee**, provided the parent has real substance and is not another thin entity in the same group.
- **Bank guarantee or standby letter of credit** where payments run ahead of delivery. It costs money, but usually less than the exposure it covers.
- **Escrow** for the delivery payment. Often easier to agree than people expect, since it answers a worry both sides share.
- **Step-in rights** over the project registration if the developer fails partway. Complex to draft; worth it only on large commitments.
- **Security over issued units** held in a registry account. Registry rules on this differ by programme, so check rather than assume.

Scale it to the exposure. A modest tranche from a developer with a track record needs little. A multi-year, staged-payment deal with a first-time developer in a jurisdiction where authorisation is untested needs several of these, and asking for them is not an insult.

## Worked example: a forward deal under strain

All figures are illustrative.

An Indian carrier signs a forward agreement with a developer of a biogas project for 50,000 units across two deliveries. Units are not yet authorised. Payments are staged: 20% at signing, the balance on delivery.

- **Month 9.** Verification cuts the first issuance. The developer can offer 41,000 units, 82% of contract volume. With a ±10% band in the contract, 82% falls outside it, so the pre-agreed remedy applies: the seller sources the shortfall or refunds the pro rata price. Without a band, this becomes a negotiation at the worst possible time.
- **Month 14.** Authorisation has not arrived. The contract's long-stop date is month 18. The carrier starts sourcing alternative supply in parallel, knowing its exit.
- **Month 18.** Still no authorisation. The carrier terminates and recovers its signing payment under the refund clause, backed by a bank guarantee because the developer is a single-project company.

Take away the long-stop date and the guarantee, and the same story ends with the carrier holding a commitment to units it cannot use, and an unsecured claim against a company with one asset.

Other failures worth walking through before signing: the programme loses approval; the vintage window moves; your network changes and you no longer need the volume. Each is foreseeable. Each is cheap to handle in drafting and expensive to argue about later.

## Where leverage sits

The buyer is strongest when the seller needs the offtake to finance the project, and weakest where eligible supply is truly scarce and other buyers are waiting.

The seller is strongest when authorisation is already in hand, because that is the scarce thing. Authorised units therefore come with better terms as well as higher prices, and a quicker negotiation, since most of the risk clauses stop mattering. Where authorisation is pending, the whole negotiation is really about who carries sovereign risk. Price it openly.

## Who should be at the table

Offtake agreements sit between carbon market knowledge and contract law. We rarely see one adviser do both well. What works is a carbon adviser who identifies the risks and what the market usually accepts, alongside a lawyer who drafts. Leave out the first and you get a well-drafted contract that ignores authorisation. Leave out the second and you get a term sheet you cannot enforce.

## Quick answers

**Do we need an offtake agreement for a spot purchase?** No, but even a spot deal needs an eligibility warranty and a documentation obligation. A bare purchase order is not enough.

**How long do these run?** Anything from a single delivery to several compliance periods.

**Can we contract for units not yet issued?** Yes. That is what a forward agreement is. Price delivery and authorisation risk explicitly.

**What is most often missing from a first agreement?** A named long-stop date for authorisation, and documentation as a binding obligation. For the steps around the contract, see [the procurement process for Indian operators](/insights/corsia-unit-procurement-process-india/).
