Picture a mid-sized Indian carrier in the middle of February. Its one person who understood the CORSIA monitoring plan has just handed in notice. The Annual Emissions Report is due in a few months, the verifier is booked, and nobody else knows which fuel figure the team uses when two sources disagree.

That carrier did not have a consultant problem or an in-house problem. It had a question it never answered properly: where should the knowledge sit? This piece is about answering it before February.

## Start from the calendar, not the org chart

CORSIA does not end. Monitoring runs every calendar year, a report follows, a verifier tests it, and the cycle begins again. Two consequences follow from that.

Anything you hand to an outside firm permanently becomes a fee you pay every year. Anything you build inside becomes a capability you have to keep alive through resignations, restructures and system changes. Neither is free. The decision is about which cost you would rather carry.

## Four things that stay with the operator whatever you choose

Before comparing models, strip out what cannot move. However much external help you buy, the operator keeps these:

1. **The signature.** The verified emissions report goes in under the operator's name, and the accountable manager signs it. A consultant can draft every page; the obligation does not transfer.
2. **The raw data.** Fuel figures come from your own operation: crew records, uplift dockets, station reports, ACARS or other flight data systems. An adviser cannot fix a number that was captured badly at the ramp.
3. **Changes to systems.** If the way data is recorded or stored needs to change, your IT and operations people do the work. An adviser writes the specification.
4. **Checks through the year.** Someone has to look for gaps and odd values month by month. Finding six missing weeks in January is far worse than finding them in March.

Once you accept that list, a purely outsourced model turns out not to exist. The real choice is how much of the yearly cycle runs inside the company.

## The three options, one at a time

**Consultant-led.** An outside firm runs the cycle and your staff supply data. It is the safest way through a first year, when nobody internally has seen a verification. It is also the most expensive route over time, because the full fee recurs, and unless transfer is written into the contract very little knowledge stays behind. Key-person risk sits with the consultant, and regulatory tracking comes as part of the service.

**In-house.** Your own team runs everything. Over several years it is usually cheapest, and knowledge stays where the accountability is. The weak points are year one, when an untested team faces its first verifier, and continuity: key-person risk now sits with you, and so does the job of tracking rule changes.

**Hybrid.** An adviser leads the first cycle with knowledge transfer built into the scope, then steps back to a lighter role while your team takes over the running. Fees fall year by year, verification confidence stays high, and risk is shared. This is where most operators end up after one cycle, and for good reason.

## How the money moves over five cycles

Comparing a single year misleads, because the three models spend at different points. Here is the shape, in words rather than rupees, since fees vary too much by operator to quote.

- **Year one.** Consultant-led and hybrid both pay a full fee; the hybrid fee includes time for training your people. In-house pays for recruitment and training and carries the most risk of a bad first verification.
- **Year two.** Consultant-led pays the full fee again. In-house pays salary. Hybrid pays a reduced fee as your team takes on more.
- **Years three to five.** Consultant-led keeps paying in full. In-house keeps paying salary, and by year five the chance of losing a trained person is real. Hybrid pays only for a short review before verification.

The pattern: in-house costs least over time but is riskiest at the start; consultant-led is safest at the start but never gets cheaper; hybrid spends early on transfer and then tapers.

### Two things that tilt the answer

**Size.** An operator with several AOCs, complicated routes and constant change has enough work to keep an internal function busy. An operator with a few international sectors does not, and is better off buying the skill.

**Existing reporting teams.** If you already run an environmental or sustainability function that handles other reporting, CORSIA can sit beside it cheaply. If the compliance lead would exist only for CORSIA, the case for building internally weakens a lot.

## The smallest team that works

Even a heavily outsourced model needs these people named:

| Who | Owns | How much of their time |
|---|---|---|
| Accountable manager | The obligation and the signature | Small, but they must stay engaged |
| Compliance lead | The cycle, the monitoring plan, the verifier relationship | Part-time, busiest at year end |
| Fuel data owner | Complete, correctly captured fuel data | Part of an existing job |
| Flight records owner | Sectors, aircraft, route pairs | Part of an existing job |
| IT or systems contact | Data extracts, retention, change control | Now and then |
| Finance contact | Tying fuel burn back to purchase records | Now and then, and worth it |

The compliance lead is the role that decides whether the year is controlled or chaotic, whatever outside support you have.

The finance contact is the one people leave off. Checking reported fuel burn against fuel purchases and invoices is one of the strongest independent tests available, and verifiers often run it themselves. Better that your team runs it first.

## Moving work inside without a cliff edge

If the aim is to bring the cycle in-house, plan the handover rather than hoping for it:

- Name the compliance lead before the first cycle starts, not once it has finished.
- Have that person work next to the consultant all year instead of receiving a folder at the end.
- Make the consultant hand over artefacts: written procedures, a data flow diagram, an evidence pack template and a worked example.
- Put the lead in the room during verification fieldwork. The verifier's questions are the clearest statement of what matters, and attending costs nothing extra.
- Appoint a deputy. One trained person is a single resignation away from none.
- Budget for a light outside review before verification in years two and three.
- Give someone the explicit job of tracking changes to participating States and emissions unit criteria.

## Where outside help still pays

A capable internal team still benefits from an external view at particular moments:

- the first cycle after a big change such as a new AOC, a merger or a large route expansion
- a move from one CORSIA phase to the next, when obligations shift
- the year after a material verification finding, when an independent review adds credibility
- buying emissions units, where market knowledge is specialist and needed only periodically
- corresponding adjustment questions, which involve dealing with government in a way most airlines rarely do
- a short challenge review before the verifier arrives

The last item is usually the best value. A few days of independent testing costs a fraction of closing findings against a deadline.

## A seven-question self-test

Answer honestly, and write the answers down:

1. How many international sectors and State pairs are in scope?
2. How many legal entities or AOCs?
3. Do you already have an environmental or sustainability reporting function?
4. Is fuel data already reconciled per flight, with source documents kept?
5. Can you name the person who would own the compliance calendar?
6. Would that person have a deputy?
7. How much change in fleet, routes or entities do you expect over three years?

If questions 3 to 6 are mostly yes and the operation is stable, in-house is realistic. If they are mostly no, or the operation is small, go hybrid. Nobody should adopt consultant-led as a permanent arrangement: beyond two years it is the dearest option, and it keeps the know-how outside the company that carries the liability.

## Back to February: protecting against the resignation

The risk operators underrate is not skill. It is continuity. A lone trained compliance lead is an asset and a single point of failure at once. If they leave three months before the deadline, the company can be worse off than if the work had been external all along, because the knowledge has walked out and any adviser hired in a hurry charges for the hurry.

What would have saved the carrier in our opening, roughly in order of value:

- **Current written procedures**, so the cycle does not depend on one memory.
- **A named deputy who has sat through a verification**, so two people have seen it done.
- **Evidence pack templates**, so nobody rebuilds the structure from scratch.
- **A standing arrangement with an outside reviewer** who already knows your monitoring plan.
- **Cross-training with the safety or quality team**, whose audit habits carry over well.
- **A central calendar and obligations register**, not one person's inbox.

The standing arrangement is the cheapest of these. A few days of review a year keeps an adviser familiar with your operation, so a resignation means a phone call, not a procurement exercise.

It is also why pure in-house is seldom the honest advice for a small operator. One person doing CORSIA alongside three other jobs looks like a capability on the organisation chart and behaves like a risk.

## Short answers

::: accordion Can an adviser take on the legal responsibility?
No. Preparation can be bought in. The report is still the operator's, and the accountable manager still signs it.
:::

::: accordion How long before an internal team can run the cycle alone?
Usually one full cycle working beside an adviser, followed by a lighter outside review in the second year.
:::

::: accordion Does the compliance lead need a carbon background?
Data discipline matters more. Someone comfortable with data flows, controls and audit evidence picks up the CORSIA specifics quickly.
:::

::: accordion What is the danger of going in-house too early?
A monitoring plan the authority does not accept, or material findings in the first verification. Both cost more to fix than the support would have cost.
:::

::: accordion What is worth keeping external for good?
Advice on buying emissions units, corresponding adjustment engagement, and an independent review before verification.
:::
