Every offer we see describes its credits as "high quality". The phrase is only useful once you can name the tests a credit passes and point to the documents that prove it. This is the method we use at the desk, arranged so that the checks which knock out the most supply come first and the slow, judgement-heavy work is only done on units that can actually be used.

![Checklist graphic showing the tests behind carbon credit quality](/images/carbon-credits/carbon-credit-quality-checklist.svg)

## The eight questions, grouped into three passes

| Pass | Question | Typical failure |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Gates** | Has anyone else claimed this tonne? | No corresponding adjustment |
| | Is it in a recognised registry, serialised and held by the seller? | Evidence only in the seller's hands |
| **2. Substance** | Would it have happened anyway? | Project already profitable or legally required |
| | Is the counterfactual believable? | Overstated deforestation or stove usage |
| | Were the numbers worked conservatively? | Generous default values |
| | Could the reduction reverse? | Buffer sized to past rather than present risk |
| **3. Process** | Was it independently checked? | Qualified opinion, or no verification |
| | Did it harm anyone? | Land tenure disputes |

Programme approval and vintage belong in Pass 1 as well whenever the credits are for compliance use.

## Pass 1: the gates

These are cheap to check and final. If a unit fails here, nothing later can rescue it, so there is no point spending time on its additionality.

### Double counting

For compliance, and more and more for serious voluntary claims, ask whether the host State has authorised the transfer and applied a **corresponding adjustment**.

Acceptable evidence is a formal authorisation from the designated national authority that names the units or a defined volume, together with evidence that the adjustment shows up in national reporting. A seller's word, a line on the project website, a broker's confirmation or a remark that the country "supports Article 6" are not evidence. If this test fails for a compliance purchase, stop. Background is in our [Article 6 explainer](/insights/article-6-paris-agreement-explained/).

### The registry trail

Pull the registry record yourself rather than accepting a summary. Check that each unit is uniquely serialised, that the chain of custody runs unbroken from issuance, that nothing has been retired already, and that the seller really holds the units. Then save copies into your own files. Evidence that lives only with an intermediary vanishes when the relationship ends. Our comparison of [carbon credit registries](/insights/carbon-credit-registries-compared/) explains what each registry shows publicly.

## Pass 2: the substance

This is where judgement comes in, and where most of the time goes.

### Additionality

Request the baseline scenario, an investment or barrier analysis showing why carbon revenue was needed, and a common practice test. Be most sceptical of grid-connected renewables where they are now the cheapest source of power. That argument was easy a decade and more ago and is very hard to make today, which is why several standards have narrowed or retired those methodologies.

One question cuts through: what was the internal rate of return with and without carbon revenue, and who signed that analysis off? Our piece on [additionality](/insights/additionality-in-carbon-credits-explained/) covers the tests in more detail.

### Baseline

The baseline differs by project type, and so does the risk:

- **Avoided deforestation.** The baseline projects forest loss that would have happened. Independent reviews have found some of these substantially overstated, which produced more credits than the project earned. Jurisdictional approaches are the structural answer.
- **Cookstoves.** The baseline rests on prior fuel use and the share of non-renewable biomass. Both have been disputed.
- **Methane capture.** The baseline is usually venting, generally the cleanest counterfactual in the market.

Ask how the baseline was set, when it was last revised, and whether it uses a jurisdictional or project-level reference.

### Quantification

Look at which way uncertainty was resolved. Where a methodology allows a range of default values and a project always sits at the generous end, question it. Then look at the monitoring plan. Does the project really produce the data the methodology asks for, at the frequency required, across the whole crediting period? A plan that describes an ideal rather than practice will produce disputed numbers.

### Permanence

This applies only where carbon is stored; avoided emissions cannot reverse. Ask what share of credits goes to the buffer pool, how that share was set and when it was last reassessed. A buffer sized for fire risk as it stood twenty years ago may not cover today's. Ask about the commitment period and what legally secures the carbon, and who bears a reversal if it happens.

## Pass 3: the process

### Verification

Read the verification report, not a summary. Is the verifier accredited under the standard's rules? Does the report cover the monitoring period your units come from? Does it carry any qualifications, and what do they say? A qualified opinion does not automatically rule a unit out, but you need to know what was qualified and why. Independence matters too: the body that validated a project's design generally cannot verify its results.

### Safeguards

In forestry especially, land tenure is where things go wrong. A government's or a large landowner's agreement is not the same as free, prior and informed consent from the communities who actually use the land. Ask for the environmental and social impact assessment, records of stakeholder consultation, consent documents where indigenous or local communities are affected, and evidence that a grievance mechanism works. Search the public record as well; community disputes are often reported in the press before they appear anywhere in a registry.

## How far to rely on ratings

Rating agencies are good for triage. They narrow a large market quickly and can surface issues you would not find alone. Three limits apply:

1. **A rating is about quality, not eligibility.** A highly rated project without a corresponding adjustment is still not usable for CORSIA.
2. **Agencies use different methods**, and can disagree on the same project. The disagreement usually points to a specific, real risk and is worth reading.
3. **A rating reflects one moment.** Projects change.

Use them to build a shortlist, then read the documents for the units you are buying. See [carbon credit ratings explained](/insights/carbon-credit-ratings-explained/).

## When to walk away

Some findings are not for negotiation:

- no host-State authorisation, and no believable timeline for one, where compliance use is intended
- the issuing programme's approval has lapsed, or its conditions touch these units
- the vintage falls outside the window for your compliance period
- the seller will not let you verify their holding in the registry
- only summaries are offered and the underlying evidence is withheld
- pressure to sign before diligence is finished

The last deserves a word. Real scarcity creates real urgency, and honest sellers will tell you so. But urgency is also the usual way diligence gets cut short, and a unit bought in a hurry that later fails is worse than a unit you never bought.

## Matching effort to the deal

| Check | Small repeat purchase, known project | Large first purchase, new developer |
|---|---|---|
| Double counting, programme approval, vintage | Always | Always |
| Additionality, baseline, permanence, safeguards | Rely on the earlier assessment if nothing has changed | Full review |
| Counterparty checks | Light refresh | Full review |
| Evidence file | Complete | Complete |

The evidence file never shrinks. Small purchases draw audit questions just as large ones do, and filing properly at the time costs almost nothing. For a first purchase, allow one to three weeks of diligence per tranche.

## Put it in a written policy

Buyers who assess every purchase from scratch end up with an uneven portfolio and no consistent basis for their decisions. A short written policy solves both and takes an afternoon. It should state:

- **the purpose**: compliance, voluntary claims or both, since the eligibility rules differ
- **accepted standards**, and whether ICAO approval is required
- **project types in and out**, including any excluded for weak additionality or reputational risk
- **vintage limits**, and how close to a boundary is acceptable
- **whether a corresponding adjustment is mandatory**, or only for compliance volumes
- **a removal and avoidance mix**, if you have a target
- **evidence required before purchase**, listed so procurement can apply it without judgement calls
- **who can approve an exception**, and how it is recorded

The exception route matters more than it looks. Exceptions will come up. A policy with no route for them gets ignored.

## Keep the reasoning, not just the result

The transaction file is what defends the purchase when someone asks about it three years later. Record the options rejected and why, the evidence for each test, the residual risks accepted, who approved and on what authority, and the price basis with the quotes received.

Save copies of pages rather than links. Approval status, participation lists and vintage windows all change, and a URL does not prove what a page said on the day you relied on it.

## Short answers

**What fails most often?** Missing corresponding adjustments, by a wide margin. Then additionality for renewables, and baselines for forestry.

**Can we rely on a broker's diligence?** Review it, but do not substitute it for your own. Your auditor or regulator holds you accountable.

**What if a problem appears after purchase?** Your contract decides the remedy, which is why the eligibility warranty matters more than the price.
