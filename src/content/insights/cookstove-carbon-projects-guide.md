Clean cooking projects do real good. Smoke from cooking fires is a serious health burden, carried mostly by women and children, and hours spent gathering fuel are hours not spent in school or earning. The same projects issue credits in large volumes and have faced more sustained criticism of their methods than almost any other category in the voluntary market.

Both statements are true. The useful work, for a developer or a buyer, is understanding why.

## The arithmetic behind a cookstove credit

![Flow diagram of how a cookstove carbon project generates credits](/images/carbon-credits/cookstove-project-flow.svg)

The idea is simple. A household cooking on an open fire or a poor stove burns a certain amount of biomass. A better stove burns less for the same meals. If part of the wood saved would have come from stocks that are not regrowing, burning less of it means less net CO2.

The credited volume is built from four inputs, multiplied together:

1. **Baseline fuel use.** What the household burned before. Moderately disputed.
2. **Efficiency gain.** How much less the new stove burns. Rarely disputed, because it can be measured in the lab and in the field.
3. **Usage rate.** How much the new stove is actually used. Heavily disputed.
4. **Fraction of non-renewable biomass (fNRB).** The share of the saved wood that was being harvested unsustainably. Heavily disputed.

The first two are manageable. The argument is about the last two, and because they multiply, errors in them compound.

## Worked example: how two small overstatements add up

The figures below are **illustrative**, chosen to show the arithmetic, not taken from any project.

Suppose a project's true usage rate and true fNRB would produce 10,000 credits. Now suppose the project's usage assumption is 20% too high, and its fNRB assumption is also 20% too high.

- Usage overstatement alone: 10,000 × 1.2 = 12,000 credits.
- Add the fNRB overstatement: 12,000 × 1.2 = 14,400 credits.

Two errors that each look modest produce 4,400 extra credits, 44% more than the real reduction supports. That is why reviewers focus on these two inputs above all others.

## Usage: a stove handed out is not a stove in use

Many things happen between distribution and daily cooking:

- **Stacking.** The family keeps the old stove and uses both, cooking some dishes on each.
- **Seasonal use.** The new stove is used for part of the year only.
- **Breakdown.** It stops working and is not repaired.
- **Resale.** It ends up somewhere else entirely.

Older methodologies leaned on distribution counts and periodic surveys. Distribution says nothing about use. Surveys suffer from courtesy bias: people tell the visitor what they think the visitor wants to hear. Independent field studies have repeatedly found real usage below the levels assumed for crediting, by enough to change credited volumes materially.

Credible projects now use **sensors** to measure usage, apply conservative assumptions wherever measurement is impractical, and measure and deduct stacking rather than assuming the old stove disappeared. Our piece on [digital MRV](/insights/dmrv-digital-monitoring-carbon-projects/) explains how sensor monitoring works, and our [clean cooking case study](/insights/cookstove-clean-cooking-carbon-credits-case-study/) follows a project through it.

## fNRB: the less visible multiplier

Burning wood is only a net emission if the wood is not growing back. Timber from a forest that regrows is treated as broadly carbon-neutral in this accounting. Wood from a stock being run down is not.

fNRB is the share treated as depleting. It is a **regional estimate**, not something the project measures, and different sources have produced quite different values for the same regions. Because it feeds straight into the credited volume, an fNRB that is too high inflates every credit the project issues. This has been one of the sharpest criticisms of the category. Standards have responded by demanding more evidence and, in some cases, lowering default values.

## Where the money goes

The cost structure explains why carbon revenue matters so much here. A programme pays for:

- making the stoves, which gets cheaper with volume and is often a smaller share than people expect
- **distribution to the last mile**, frequently the largest single cost in rural programmes
- awareness and training, which decide whether the stove is actually used
- monitoring: sensors, sampling and field visits across the crediting period
- validation and verification, recurring each cycle
- registration and per-credit programme fees
- maintenance and replacement over a crediting period of several years

Outsiders underestimate distribution. In many programmes, reaching scattered rural households costs more per unit than the stove itself, which is why a cheaply made stove still cannot be sold at a price the household can pay. Carbon finance fills that gap. Without it, many programmes would not happen, which is also the core of the [additionality](/insights/additionality-in-carbon-credits-explained/) case.

There is a built-in tension. Money spent on monitoring is money not spent reaching households. A programme that spends heavily on sensors reaches fewer families; one that spends nothing on monitoring produces credits nobody should buy. The answer credible projects use is a **statistically valid sample**: measure enough households properly rather than every household badly.

## The Indian picture: LPG changes the baseline

India has one of the largest populations that could benefit from clean cooking, and a great deal of policy effort has gone into LPG access. In many areas, that has shifted the baseline.

This matters for additionality. Where a government programme is already supplying cleaner cooking fuel, a carbon project claiming to replace traditional biomass has to show its intervention adds something beyond what was already happening. In districts with high LPG uptake that is genuinely difficult. In districts with low uptake it is easier.

For an Indian developer, the additionality case is now local. It has to be built for the actual project area, district by district, not argued for the country as a whole. If the project is meant for international buyers, the route to host-State authorisation also needs confirming early; our page on [CORSIA-eligible projects in India](/insights/corsia-eligible-projects-in-india/) covers that.

## Why the category is still worth engaging with

Writing off cookstoves entirely would be too simple. The development benefits are real and do not depend on the carbon accounting. Carbon finance is often what makes distribution viable at all. And the methodological problems can be fixed: sensor monitoring, conservative fNRB and honest treatment of stacking close most of the gap between credited and actual reductions. Projects that do this exist.

The sensible position is to judge each project on how it handles usage and fNRB, and to discount heavily where it relies on the older approaches.

## Diligence checklist

Whether you are buying or designing a project, check:

- **Usage**: sensor-based, on a statistically valid sample, not surveys alone
- **Stacking**: measured and deducted, not assumed away
- **fNRB**: from a current, defensible source, conservative where uncertain
- **Baseline**: measured in the project's own population, not borrowed from literature
- **Methodology version**: current, reflecting recent revisions
- **Stove life**: maintenance and replacement planned across the crediting period
- **Community engagement**: real consultation, not just distribution
- **Co-benefits**: measured, not asserted

If you ask only one question, ask how usage is measured and on what sample. An answer that names a sensor method and a sample size shows the project has thought about the problem. "Annual surveys" is the approach the criticism was aimed at.

## Four ways to buy, three of them sound

1. **Buy selectively from the top of the category.** Projects with sensor monitoring, conservative fNRB and current methodologies are meaningfully different from those that drew criticism. Pay the premium they command.
2. **Exclude the category.** Some buyers decide the reputational exposure is not worth the saving. That is a fair risk decision, not a verdict on the climate benefit.
3. **Buy for clean cooking access, and say so.** Presenting the purchase as support for clean cooking, with carbon as one outcome among several, is honest and holds up better than a pure tonnage claim.
4. **Buy the cheapest and make a strong carbon claim.** This one is not sound. The cheapest supply is cheap for reasons that are publicly documented, and a buyer doing this takes on exactly the exposure the criticism described.

The wider lesson applies well beyond cookstoves: price carries information. Supply far cheaper than comparable alternatives usually carries a risk that other buyers have already declined.

## Short answers

**Can cookstove credits be used for CORSIA?** Possibly, if the project sits under an ICAO-approved programme, the vintage is eligible and the host State has authorised the units with a corresponding adjustment. The category is not excluded, but the usual tests apply.

**What is stove stacking?** Using the new stove alongside the old one instead of replacing it.

**What is fNRB?** The share of displaced wood treated as unsustainably harvested. It multiplies directly into the credited volume.
