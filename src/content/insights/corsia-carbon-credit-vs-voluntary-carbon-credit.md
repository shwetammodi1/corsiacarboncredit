Consider a trader in Pune holding credits from a biogas project. In the same week two enquiries arrive. A European retailer wants credits to back a product claim and asks about the villages the project serves. An airline's carbon desk wants units for CORSIA and asks for exactly one thing: the government authorisation document. The credits in the account are identical in every visible way. Only some of them can answer the airline.

That gap is the most useful thing to understand about this market, whichever side you are on.

![Illustration of CORSIA within the wider carbon market](/images/corsia/hero-corsia-market.svg)

## In two sentences

A **voluntary carbon credit** is bought by an organisation that has decided to offset; no one obliges it to, and it sets its own quality bar. A **CORSIA Eligible Emissions Unit** is bought by an aircraft operator to meet a legal obligation under the [ICAO](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/default.aspx) scheme, and the bar is fixed by ICAO, not the buyer.

It follows that any CORSIA unit could be sold voluntarily, while only a small minority of voluntary credits could ever be sold into CORSIA.

## The seven differences at a glance

| | Voluntary credit | CORSIA unit |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Who sets the standard | Competing standards; buyers choose whom to trust | ICAO approves programmes; the buyer's preference does not affect eligibility |
| 2. Corresponding adjustment | Usually absent | Mandatory |
| 3. Vintage | A quality signal | A hard window set per compliance period |
| 4. Price | Very wide range; averages blend unlike products | A premium over comparable non-eligible supply |
| 5. What the buyer does with it | Retires it and makes its own claim | Cancels it and reports to the national authority |
| 6. Documentation | Varies from thorough to minimal | Must survive verification and later audit |
| 7. Market infrastructure | Exchanges, index prices, ratings, many intermediaries | Bilateral deals, no reliable public price |

The sections below take them in order of how much they affect supply, which is not the order in the table.

## The adjustment decides supply (difference 2)

![Diagram of the CORSIA eligibility criteria for emissions units](/images/corsia/corsia-eeu-criteria.svg)

Under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, when a host country authorises a credit for another country's target or for an international purpose such as CORSIA, it applies a **corresponding adjustment**: it adds the tonnes back into its own inventory. The reduction still happened on its soil, but it can no longer count it towards its Nationally Determined Contribution while someone else claims it.

Most voluntary credits have no such adjustment. The buyer claims the tonne and the host country counts it too. The voluntary market has mostly dealt with this by changing how buyers word their claims, not by requiring adjustments. CORSIA does not accept it: no adjustment, no eligibility.

Many host governments are reluctant. Authorising makes their own target harder in exchange for foreign currency and investment, and some have decided that is a poor trade for certain project types. Others have not built the administrative process. Either way the project cannot supply CORSIA. This one requirement removes a very large share of the world's credits from eligibility and is the main reason for the price gap. [Our corresponding adjustment guide](/insights/corresponding-adjustments-corsia-explained/) deals with the commercial side.

## Who writes the rules (difference 1)

In the voluntary market, standards compete. Verra, Gold Standard, the Climate Action Reserve and others publish methodologies, and buyers pick. A company may insist on a project type, co-benefits or a region. Ratings agencies exist because quality varies and buyers want help judging it.

Under CORSIA, the ICAO Technical Advisory Body assesses **programmes** against the Emissions Unit Criteria and the ICAO Council approves them. However much an airline likes a project, it cannot use the credits unless the programme is approved and the unit-level criteria are met. Voluntary buyers use judgement; CORSIA buyers procure against a fixed specification. The criteria are listed in [the ICAO emissions unit criteria](/knowledge-base/corsia-emissions-unit-criteria/).

## Vintage windows (difference 3)

A voluntary buyer may prefer recent vintages but will still buy an older credit from a good project. CORSIA sets **eligible vintage windows** by Council decision for each compliance period, and anything outside is ineligible whatever its quality ([how the windows work](/knowledge-base/corsia-vintages-and-eligibility-windows/)). Windows have been changed before. That creates uncertainty for anyone holding older stock, and it means nobody can honestly say today what will qualify in 2030.

For a seller the risk is concrete: credits that are issued but not placed before the window shuts can drop from the premium tier into the general voluntary pool.

## Price follows scarcity (difference 4)

The voluntary market has traded across an enormous range, from very cheap older renewable credits to high-integrity removals at many times that price. Headline averages mean little because they mix different products.

Eligible units trade at a premium over comparable non-eligible supply of the same project type (see [CORSIA credit pricing](/knowledge-base/corsia-credit-pricing/)), reflecting the cost of authorisation, the host government's administrative work, and plain scarcity. A buyer who budgets from voluntary averages will be wrong, usually by a lot. For a seller, the adjustment is where value is created: it is what lifts a credit out of the general pool.

## What happens at the end (differences 5 and 6)

A voluntary buyer retires a credit and makes a claim, such as carbon neutrality or offset flights. The wording is largely up to the buyer and increasingly scrutinised.

A CORSIA operator **cancels** the unit and reports it to its national authority in an Emissions Unit Cancellation Report. There is no marketing claim and no discretion in the description; the cancellation either discharges the requirement or it does not.

That changes what a seller is really offering. Voluntary buyers often buy the story; a CORSIA buyer buys the evidence. It needs proof of programme approval status at the time of the deal, the vintage, the authorisation for the adjustment, serial numbers, transfer records and the cancellation entry, all kept in a form that survives staff changes and system migrations. Sellers who can hand over that package cleanly win business. Sellers who cannot will see experienced buyers walk away, however good the project.

## A thin market (difference 7)

The voluntary market has spot exchanges, standard contracts, index prices and ratings. The CORSIA segment has small volumes, bilateral negotiated deals and no dependable public price. Three consequences follow.

- **Finding a fair price takes work.** You have to speak to several sellers and understand what each unit's documents actually support. Taking the first quote tends to mean overpaying; squeezing only on price tends to mean documentation gaps.
- **Waiting is riskier than it looks.** A late buyer may find the supply for its period already contracted, which gives forward contracts a value beyond price hedging.
- **Counterparties vary widely.** The premium has drawn in intermediaries with a thin grasp of the criteria. "CORSIA-ready", "CORSIA-aligned" and "eligible pending authorisation" all describe units that do not currently qualify.

## Back to the Pune trader (illustrative)

Suppose the trader's account holds 30,000 credits from the biogas project, and the host government has authorised 12,000 of them for international use, with the adjustment applied. These figures are illustrative.

- The 12,000 authorised units can go to the airline, at the eligible premium, with the authorisation document in the data pack.
- The other 18,000 go to the retailer and similar voluntary buyers, with no adjustment, at voluntary prices.
- If more authorisation arrives later, units not yet sold can move to the CORSIA side.

This is why serving both markets from one project is sensible: it hedges the authorisation risk. A developer whose whole financial model depends on CORSIA placement has nowhere to go if authorisation is slow or partial.

## Checklists for each side

**Buying:**
- Ignore voluntary price benchmarks.
- Check the programme's approval **on the day of the deal**, not from last year's list.
- Ask for evidence of the adjustment, not an assurance it is under way.
- Match the vintage to your compliance period's window.
- Keep the full evidence pack, not just a certificate.
- Allow for eligible supply being thinner than expected.

**Selling:**
- Test host-government authorisation before anything else.
- Choose an ICAO-approved programme on purpose, not by habit.
- Build the documentation as you go.
- Put the cost of the adjustment work into your model.
- Keep a voluntary route open in case authorisation stalls.

## Short answers

::: accordion Can I convert a voluntary credit into a CORSIA unit?
Not by anything you do yourself. If the programme is ICAO-approved and the host government authorises those units and applies the adjustment, they may become eligible. That is a government decision.
:::

::: accordion Are CORSIA units higher quality?
They are more tightly specified, especially on double counting, which is a real integrity difference. It does not automatically mean better co-benefits or a better project.
:::

::: accordion Why would a country refuse to authorise?
Because it makes its own climate target harder to meet. That is a rational choice, not obstruction.
:::

::: accordion Do voluntary buyers ever want adjusted units?
Increasingly. Companies worried about the integrity of their claims see the adjustment as the strongest protection against double counting, and some pay for it with no CORSIA obligation. That competition adds to the scarcity operators face.
:::
