Ask the finance team, the fuel team and the sustainability team of the same airline for "our emissions" and you can get three different figures. None of them is necessarily wrong. They have drawn the line around the company in different places, used different conversion factors, or counted different activities. GHG accounting is mostly the discipline of making those choices once, writing them down and sticking to them.

The GHG Protocol is the framework nearly everyone uses. Its three scopes are simple to state and harder to apply.

![The three GHG Protocol scopes and where carbon credits are reported](/images/carbon-credits/ghg-scopes-explained.svg)

## Start with the boundary, not the scopes

Before anything is measured, two lines have to be drawn.

**The organisational boundary** decides which entities are in. There are three recognised approaches:

- **Equity share:** count emissions in proportion to your ownership.
- **Financial control:** count all the emissions of entities you control financially.
- **Operational control:** count all the emissions of entities whose operating policies you set.

Operational control is the most common choice and usually what regulators expect.

**The operational boundary** decides which scopes, and which scope 3 categories, are included. Most organisations bring scope 3 in category by category rather than all at once.

### Worked example: one joint venture, three answers

*Illustrative.* An Indian logistics group owns 40% of a joint venture that runs a cold-storage chain. The venture emits 10,000 tCO2e a year.

| Approach chosen | What the group reports from the venture |
|---|---|
| Equity share | 4,000 tCO2e (40%) |
| Operational control, group runs the venture | 10,000 tCO2e (100%) |
| Operational control, partner runs the venture | 0 tCO2e in scope 1 and 2 |

Same venture, same emissions, three reported figures. That is why a number without a stated boundary cannot be interpreted, and why a boundary change between years must be disclosed. Otherwise it looks like a change in emissions.

## The three scopes, defined

| Scope | What it covers | In an airline | In a cement plant |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Direct emissions from sources you own or control | Jet fuel burnt by your own aircraft | Fuel burnt in the kiln |
| 2 | Indirect emissions from electricity, heat, steam or cooling you buy | Power for offices and hangars | Grid electricity for grinding |
| 3 | Every other indirect emission in your value chain | Fuel production, staff travel, waste | Purchased materials, transport, customers' use of the product |

Scopes 1 and 2 are usually manageable: you know what fuel you burnt and what power you bought, and the records are in your own systems. Scope 3 is where the difficulty lies, and for most organisations it is also the biggest, often several times the size of scopes 1 and 2 together.

## Why scope 3 is hard

Scope 3 is split into fifteen categories, upstream and downstream: purchased goods and services, capital goods, fuel and energy activities not already in scopes 1 and 2, transport, waste, business travel, commuting, leased assets, processing and use of sold products, end-of-life treatment, franchises and investments.

Three problems are built in.

1. **Someone else holds the data.** These emissions happen in suppliers' and customers' operations. They may not measure them, may not share them, or may calculate them differently.
2. **Estimates are unavoidable.** Where no primary data exists, you apply spend-based or activity-based factors. Two companies with identical operations can report quite different scope 3 figures depending on the factors chosen.
3. **Overlap between companies is intended.** Your scope 3 is another company's scope 1. The framework designs it that way, so scope 3 figures cannot be added up across companies.

Read scope 3 as a good guide to direction and a poor guide to precision. Comparing it between companies, or treating it as accurate to a few per cent, asks more of it than it can give.

A sensible way in: screen all fifteen categories for materiality, then measure the material ones properly instead of all fifteen superficially.

## Scope 2 has two versions

- **Location-based** scope 2 uses the average emissions intensity of the grid you draw from. It describes the physical position.
- **Market-based** scope 2 reflects contracts that assign particular generation to you: renewable energy certificates, power purchase agreements, green tariffs.

Where such instruments are used, the GHG Protocol asks for **both** figures. The market-based figure alone hides the physical reality; the location-based figure alone ignores procurement choices that do shift investment in generation.

A frequent and costly confusion: **RECs and Guarantees of Origin are not carbon credits.** They certify attributes of electricity generated. They cannot be retired against an emissions obligation.

## Emission factors: where accuracy slips

Nearly every line in an inventory is an activity figure multiplied by an emission factor. The activity figure (litres, kilowatt-hours, tonne-kilometres) is normally measured and fairly reliable. The factor is where most of the uncertainty sits.

- **Factors get revised.** Grid factors change every year as the generation mix shifts. Using last year's factor for this year's power creates an apparent change in performance that is really a data error.
- **Sources disagree.** Two published databases can give different factors for the same activity because their assumptions differ. Mixing sources in one inventory creates inconsistencies that are hard to explain later.
- **Spend-based factors are blunt.** They are economy-wide averages. If you move to a lower-carbon supplier at the same price, a spend-based estimate will not notice.

Record the source and version of every factor, keep to one source within an inventory where you can, say when a factor revision rather than an operational change moved a figure, and switch material categories from spend-based to activity-based factors as data improves.

## Structural changes that distort the trend

Most year-on-year comparison problems come from boundary events that look administrative.

| Event | What happens to the reported figure | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition or sale of a business | Emissions jump or fall with no environmental change | Recalculate the base year and say so |
| Leased buildings or aircraft | Can sit in scope 1 and 2 or in scope 3 depending on lease type and approach | State the treatment and apply it consistently |
| Joint ventures | Half, all or none, as in the example above | Disclose the approach and who controls |
| Franchises | Usually scope 3 for the franchisor, scope 1 and 2 for the franchisee | State the boundary |
| Outsourcing an activity | Moves from scope 1 to scope 3; the atmosphere sees no change | Explain the fall, or it reads as a reduction |

A trend line drawn across an unexplained boundary change is not a trend.

## Data habits that survive scrutiny

- **Name one authoritative source for each figure.** Fuel from the fuel system, electricity from meters or invoices, travel from the booking tool. When two sources disagree, and they will, record which prevails and why.
- **Mark what is measured and what is estimated.** Supplier-specific scope 3 data and spend-based averages are different grades of number. Say which you used.
- **Keep the workings.** In three years someone will ask how a figure was built, and its author will have moved on.

These are the same habits that get an airline's CORSIA report through verification. We describe them for aviation in [CORSIA data systems](/insights/corsia-data-systems-and-automation/).

## Where carbon credits go

This is the rule most often broken, so it gets its own section.

> Carbon credits are reported outside the scopes. They are never subtracted from scope 1, 2 or 3.

The inventory states what was emitted. Credits bought and retired go in a separate disclosure, with type, standard and quantity. A company that reports "net scope 1 after offsets" has misreported, because the single figure hides whether emissions fell or credits were bought.

Aviation has one further point. Units cancelled to meet a CORSIA obligation are a compliance action. They do not reduce the operator's reported scope 1 emissions, and they cannot also be used for a voluntary carbon-neutral claim. How to present credits in annual reports is covered in [ESG reporting and carbon credits](/insights/esg-reporting-and-carbon-credits/), and the reasons credits cannot substitute for cuts under a target are in our note on [science-based targets](/insights/science-based-targets-sbti-explained/).

## Quick answers

::: accordion Which scope is usually largest?
Scope 3, often several times scopes 1 and 2 together.
:::

::: accordion Do credits reduce scope 1?
No. They are reported separately and never netted.
:::

::: accordion Does my scope 3 double count my suppliers' emissions?
Yes, deliberately. That is why scope 3 totals cannot be summed across companies.
:::

::: accordion Are RECs carbon credits?
No. They certify energy attributes, not tonnes reduced.
:::
