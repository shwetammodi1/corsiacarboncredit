An Indian component supplier bidding for a European contract finds a new line in the tender questionnaire: "Do you have a validated science-based target?" It is not asking whether the company has a climate goal. It is asking whether that goal has been set by a recognised method and checked by someone independent. Those are different things, and the difference is what this note is about.

## Definition

A science-based target is an emissions-reduction goal matched to what climate science says is needed to keep warming well below 2°C, and ideally to 1.5°C. It is not a number a company picks because it sounds ambitious. It is worked out from a global carbon budget and the company's fair share of it.

The **Science Based Targets initiative (SBTi)** publishes the methods for setting these targets and then independently validates whether a company's submitted target meets them. That validation is what turns a stated goal into a checkable claim.

## Two horizons

The SBTi framework asks for targets on two timescales.

**Near-term targets** cover roughly the next five to ten years. They require deep, absolute cuts within this decade, so that action starts now rather than being deferred.

**Net-zero targets** set the end point, by around 2050. They require emissions to be cut by roughly 90% or more, with only the small remainder neutralised.

Net zero in this sense is not a new name for "carbon neutral". It requires the reductions to come first. We explain the difference in [carbon neutral vs net zero](/insights/carbon-neutral-vs-net-zero/).

## What counts towards the target, and what doesn't

This is where most companies go wrong, so here it is as a table.

| Action | Counts towards the target? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Cutting fuel and energy use in your own operations | Yes | Reduces your own emissions |
| Changing suppliers, materials or product design to cut value-chain emissions | Yes | Reduces your scope 3 |
| Buying carbon credits to compensate for emissions | No | Compensation leaves your own emissions unchanged |
| Using removals to neutralise the small residual at net zero | Only at the end point, for the residual | That is the role the framework gives them |
| Funding climate action outside your value chain | No, but encouraged | Treated as extra, not as progress |

Carbon credits have a role, but it is not the role many companies assume. They do not move a science-based target, because the target measures your own emissions and a credit does not reduce them. The related distinction between reducing inside your own supply chain and compensating outside it is covered in [insetting vs offsetting](/insights/carbon-insetting-vs-offsetting/).

## Scope 3 is where targets get hard

Science-based targets include scope 3, the emissions in a company's value chain. For most businesses these are far larger than their direct emissions. That makes the targets demanding: switching your offices to green power will not meet them. You have to influence what suppliers do and how products are made and used.

It also makes measurement a precondition. A company cannot set, let alone meet, a credible target on emissions it has not counted properly. Our guide to [scope 1, 2 and 3 accounting](/insights/ghg-accounting-scope-1-2-3-explained/) is the place to start.

## The commercial side

A validated target is increasingly treated as a credential. Large customers, tender processes and investors ask for it, as the supplier in our opening example found. A validated target tells them the company's climate claims have been tested against a common method. A vague, self-declared goal tells them the opposite. For suppliers to companies that care about ESG, it is moving towards a basic requirement.

## Mistakes we see

- **Announcing a target before measuring.** The target then has to be revised, which looks worse than waiting.
- **Counting credits as progress.** Neither the SBTi nor the other credible frameworks accept it.
- **Leaving out scope 3** because it is difficult. That is the part most targets depend on.
- **Calling a self-set goal "science-based"** without validation. Customers who know the term will ask for the validation.
