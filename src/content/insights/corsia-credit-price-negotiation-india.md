Indian developers who have sold into the voluntary market usually open a pitch the same way: the village, the families, the photographs. Put that deck in front of an airline's CORSIA procurement team and the first three questions will be about something else entirely. Is the unit authorised? Which programme, and is it approved for this scope? Is the vintage inside the window?

Those questions tell you how this market prices. Get the order right and you sell well. Get it wrong and you either leave money behind or price yourself out of a buyer's deadline.

## Why eligibility outranks everything else

A voluntary buyer is choosing a story it wants to tell. A compliance buyer is discharging a legal obligation, and a unit either counts towards it or does not. That makes eligibility close to an on-off switch, and it reorders the usual price drivers:

1. **Corresponding adjustment status.** Decisive. Authorised units carry a large premium.
2. **Programme recognition.** Very important: an approved programme, and the right scope within it.
3. **Vintage.** Inside the eligible window the unit works; outside it, the unit is useless for compliance.
4. **Removal or avoidance.** A moderate effect, and it weighs more with voluntary buyers.
5. **Volume and tenor.** Moderate. Bigger, longer offtakes earn a lower unit price.
6. **Co-benefits and narrative.** Worth less to a compliance buyer than to a voluntary one.

Community benefits are not worthless here; they simply come after the eligibility questions, not before.

## Where the scarcity comes from

Demand in this market is set by law, not sentiment. It comes from the offsetting requirement across participating operators, and it grows as more States take part and as traffic grows relative to the baseline.

Supply is held back by the corresponding adjustment. India has many carbon projects; far fewer carry authorisation. That bottleneck is a policy variable, not a market one, and it is what supports the price of authorised units.

So an authorised unit is a scarce product, and it should not be benchmarked against voluntary credits from the same project type. Our [comparison of CORSIA and voluntary credits](/insights/corsia-carbon-credit-vs-voluntary-carbon-credit/) and the note on [when compliance buyers tend to purchase](/insights/corsia-carbon-credit-price-guide/) give the wider market view.

## Homework before the first call

Walk into a negotiation knowing four things:

- **Your floor.** The all-in cost per credit: validation, verification, monitoring, registry fees, and development cost spread over a realistic volume.
- **Your real volume.** Verifiers cut claimed reductions. Plan on 10–15% below your own estimate until units are actually issued.
- **Your clock.** If you need cash by a certain date, a buyer who senses it will price it in.
- **Your fallback.** Could these units go to the voluntary market, and at roughly what price? That is your walk-away point.

The fallback is where leverage comes from. A seller with nowhere else to go takes what is offered. A seller who can place the units elsewhere, even for less, is negotiating rather than accepting.

## Choosing the shape of the deal

Price depends heavily on structure. The common forms, with what each trades away:

- **Spot sale of issued units.** The highest price per unit, because the seller has already carried the delivery risk.
- **Forward sale before issuance.** A discount; the buyer takes delivery risk and the seller gets price certainty.
- **Long-term offtake.** Commonly 20–40% below spot. It funds development and caps your upside for the term.
- **Option or call.** The buyer pays a premium up front for the right to buy later.
- **Floor with upside share.** Your downside is protected and any upside is split.
- **Conditional sale with authorisation pending.** A heavy discount, and the value rests entirely on how the condition is written.

For a first project, a sensible default is to sell part of the expected volume forward to pay for monitoring and verification, and hold the rest for spot. You get cash flow without locking your whole crediting period at today's price. The clauses that matter in those agreements are covered in [CORSIA offtake agreement terms](/insights/corsia-offtake-agreement-terms/).

## Conduct at the table

**Open with the eligibility facts.** Authorisation status, programme, vintage, serial ranges. It shows you understand the buyer's problem.

**Hand over the documentation pack on day one.** When the buyer has a deadline, fast diligence is itself worth money. The contents are listed in [the project documentation pack](/insights/corsia-project-documentation-pack/).

**Raise your weaknesses yourself.** A qualified verification opinion or a baseline assumption that can be challenged will be found anyway. Disclosed early, with the fix, it costs less.

**State scarcity as fact, never as theatre.** If your units are authorised and the buyer's other options are not, say so. Never invent a rival bid.

**Keep price and terms apart.** Delivery risk, revocation risk and payment timing all have value. A high headline price with harsh delivery terms can be worth less than a lower one with a cure period.

**Give away what costs you little.** Progress reports, site visits and co-benefit updates are cheap for you and valuable to a buyer who has to report publicly.

## What buyers say, and what to say back

::: accordion "This project type trades lower in the voluntary market."
It is a different product. Those units cannot discharge a CORSIA obligation; yours can.
:::

::: accordion "Your authorisation has conditions attached."
Deal with each condition specifically, or put a price on the risk. Do not wave it away.
:::

::: accordion "We need certainty of delivery."
Offer a cure period and a substitution right rather than cutting the price.
:::

::: accordion "We want the whole lot at one price."
A volume discount is reasonable. Tie it to better payment terms.
:::

::: accordion "Our budget is fixed."
Change the volume or the structure. Never change the integrity of the documentation.
:::

::: accordion "We have to cancel before a deadline."
Then speed and certainty are worth money. A seller with issued units, clean title and a ready pack is entitled to price that, and most developers undersell it.
:::

## Settlement terms to agree in advance

- Payment on registry transfer, not on invoice.
- Escrow for large trades where neither side wants to move first.
- Staged payments on forward contracts, tied to verification milestones.
- Who bears which taxes and levies.
- The settlement currency, and who pays for conversion.
- Written confirmation of cancellation when the buyer retires straight away.

Registry transfers take time. If the buyer is working to a deadline, build that time into the schedule rather than assuming transfer is instant.

## Worked example: an Indian seller with nothing to prove

Illustrative only. A developer holds 25,000 issued units from an authorised Indian project, under an approved programme, all inside the eligible vintage window. An airline needs units before a cancellation deadline.

Run the homework first:

| Item | Position |
|---|---|
| Floor | Known; development costs already recovered from an earlier tranche |
| Volume | 25,000, issued, so there is no verification haircut left to fear |
| Clock | No pressing need for cash |
| Fallback | The voluntary market, at a clearly lower price, but real |

The buyer has a legal obligation and a date. The seller has no urgency, a scarce product and a credible alternative. That imbalance is the negotiation, and it favours the seller, provided the pack is ready so that diligence does not become the buyer's reason to walk.

A good outcome here is a split rather than one price for everything. Say 15,000 units go now, at a firm price, for immediate transfer; the other 10,000 are held back. The airline gets certainty before its deadline. The developer keeps stock for the next compliance cycle, when supply may be tighter still.

The move to avoid is inventing a competing bidder. Compliance buyers are few, they talk to one another, and a fictional bid that comes to light follows you into every later deal.

## Mistakes that cost Indian sellers money

- Quoting against voluntary prices for the same project type.
- Leading with co-benefits when the buyer is asking about authorisation.
- Building the price on pre-verification volumes.
- Presenting authorisation that is pending as though it were granted.
- Pushing so hard on the first sale that the buyer does not come back.

That last one matters more than it looks. The airlines with obligations are a small group, and most of them have obligations every year. A seller who delivers cleanly, reports on the project afterwards and handles problems openly will be approached directly next cycle, with no intermediary margin to share. That margin is usually worth more than whatever an aggressive first negotiation would have won.
