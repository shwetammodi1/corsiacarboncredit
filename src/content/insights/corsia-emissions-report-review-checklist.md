The draft report is finished on a Thursday and the verifier arrives on Monday. What the team does on Friday decides how the following weeks go. A few hours of structured self-review now costs far less than days spent closing findings later, and the review is not complicated. It just has to be done in the right order, by the right person.

![Diagram of the areas an internal CORSIA review covers](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-gap-analysis-areas.svg)

## Who holds the pen

Not the person who prepared the report. Familiarity is what hides problems. At a small operator, a colleague from another department is enough, and for the reconstruction test at the end an outsider to the process is actually better. A larger operator should use internal audit or an outside reviewer, as long as that reviewer is not the firm that will verify. An adviser can help prepare and review the report; independence rules stop them verifying it afterwards.

Allow roughly half a day for a small operator and two to three days for a large one with sampling.

## Start with ten minutes of arithmetic

Before any process checks, look at the numbers themselves. None of these proves the report is right, but each catches the sort of error you would rather not have a verifier find.

**Worked example (illustrative figures).** Last year an operator reported 180,000 tonnes of CO2 from covered flying over 60,000 block hours: 3.0 tonnes per block hour. This year's draft shows 205,000 tonnes over 61,000 hours, which is about 3.36 tonnes per hour.

- Block hours rose about 1.7%. Emissions rose about 14%.
- Intensity per hour moved about 12% with no fleet change to explain it.
- That is not an operational story; it points to a fuel data or scope classification problem. In practice it is often a month counted twice or a set of flights reclassified.

Run these every year:

- **Year-on-year change.** Anything beyond a few percent should be explained by growth, a route change, a fleet change or a method change. Unexplained shifts are usually data.
- **Emissions per block hour.** Should be broadly steady and plausible for the fleet, as above.
- **Covered share.** The proportion of international emissions on covered pairs should fit your network and the participation list. A big unexplained move usually means the route-pair logic changed or the list was applied inconsistently.
- **Monthly shape.** Plot it. A dip usually means a data feed failed, not that fewer flights operated; a spike usually means a duplicate.
- **Independent re-sum.** Add the monthly figures in a separate sheet and compare with the annual total. Two minutes, and it catches aggregation errors that no process review will.

## The nine-stage review

Work in this order. Each stage depends on the one before it; checking arithmetic on an incomplete flight list tests the wrong thing.

| Stage | Questions to answer | What it typically catches |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Entity | Is the reporting entity the AOC holder these flights were operated under? Is each AOC in a group reported separately? Is the scope decision documented with its reasoning? Are mid-year restructures reflected? | Wrong or merged entities |
| 2. Completeness | Does the flight count reconcile to an independent source such as slot data, ATC records, or the schedule plus known ad hoc movements? Are positioning and ferry flights in? Diversions and technical stops treated consistently? Charters present? | Missing flights, above all positioning legs |
| 3. Classification | Is each flight marked international or domestic by a written rule? Are aircraft at or below 5,700 kg MTOM excluded, with the mass source recorded? Are humanitarian, medical, firefighting and State flights evidenced one by one? Is the covered-pair decision based on the participation list as at the relevant date, captured rather than linked? | Unevidenced exemptions, wrong pair list |
| 4. Fuel | Does total fuel tie to finance records within an explainable margin? Where sources disagreed, was the written rule applied? Are escalated cases logged with decision and reason? Any fuel figures implausible for the sector or type? | Discrepancies settled with no rule |
| 5. Gaps | Is there a register of every gap, its cause and its fill? Was the fill method set in the monitoring plan beforehand, and applied consistently? Is the share of estimated data stated? | Fill methods invented after the event |
| 6. Calculation | Is the right factor used per fuel, 3.16 kg CO2 per kg for Jet-A and Jet-A1? Is fuel in mass, with any density basis documented? Are covered and non-covered emissions split correctly? | Spreadsheet and conversion errors |
| 7. Fuels claims | If eligible fuels are claimed: batch certification under an ICAO-approved sustainability certification scheme, chain of custody to uplift, a stated life-cycle value, volumes tied to purchase records, and no claim of the same fuel under another scheme? | Claims that will be disallowed |
| 8. Plan conformity | Does practice match the monitoring plan version in force? If the plan changed mid-year, is it clear which version applied when? Are deviations explained rather than left to be found? | Drift between plan and practice |
| 9. Reconstruction | See below | Figures nobody can explain |

Three stages deserve a comment.

**Completeness** is the stage most likely to find something and the one most often skipped, because a missing flight is invisible when you look at the report. Positioning and ferry sectors earn no revenue and are routinely absent from lists built from commercial systems.

**Classification** has a recurring evidence problem: a web link is not proof of what a published list said during the reporting year. Save a copy.

**Fuels claims** fail expensively. If documentation is incomplete, the claim is disallowed and the obligation reverts to the unreduced figure, usually after the budget was set on the reduced one. See [how SAF reduces the offsetting requirement](/knowledge-base/saf-and-corsia-offsetting-reduction/) for the mechanics.

## The reconstruction test

This is the check that predicts findings better than any other. Choose five reported figures, deliberately awkward ones: a month with a system outage, a diverted flight, a figure that needed a manual decision. Give them to someone who had no part in preparing the report, with access only to the files, and ask them to explain how each was reached.

If they cannot, neither will the verifier. You have found the finding while it is still cheap to fix. The same logic applies to sampling generally: pick the difficult cases on purpose, not a representative spread.

## The pack behind the report

Verification examines the evidence, not just the report. Review these as though handing them to a stranger, and put in writing anything that would need a verbal explanation:

- **Every version of the monitoring plan**, with approval dates, so it is clear which applied when.
- **The reconciliation rule set**, checked for currency. Rules written for systems since replaced are worse than none, because practice will have drifted away from them without anyone noticing.
- **The decision log**, complete for the whole year. The usual pattern is a log that thins out after March.
- **The gap register**, with its total estimated share matching what the report says.
- **Captured evidence rather than links**: the participation list at the relevant date, programme approval status, fuel certificates. This is the most common evidence weakness of all.
- **Third-party records** such as handling agent dockets and supplier statements, the items most likely to be missing because someone else holds them. Our note on [fuel supplier data coordination](/insights/corsia-fuel-supplier-data-coordination/) covers getting them in on time.
- **Earlier findings and proof of closure**: what actually changed, not just a statement that it was closed. A repeat finding is taken much more seriously than a first one.

## If you find an error after submission

Tell the authority and the verifier promptly. An error you find and correct yourself is treated far better than one the verifier uncovers.

After submission comes verification, and then the offsetting requirement once ICAO publishes the growth factors.
