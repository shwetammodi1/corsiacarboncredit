Of all the suppliers an operator appoints for CORSIA, the verifier is the one whose output it cannot influence. The opinion is formed independently and it is not up for negotiation. What an operator does control is who it appoints, when, and on what terms. Those choices decide whether verification is a routine few weeks or a scramble.

This is a guide to that appointment, written the way we would help an Indian operator run it: as a small, structured tender.

![Stages of a CORSIA verification engagement](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-verification-steps.svg)

## What you are appointing someone to do

A CORSIA verification is an independent assurance engagement to a **reasonable assurance** standard. The verifier tests your report against the scheme requirements and your accepted monitoring plan, and gives an opinion on whether the reported emissions are free of material misstatement.

Reasonable assurance is the higher of the two common assurance levels. It needs more evidence and more testing than limited assurance: sampling, tracing individual figures back to source, and checking that your controls actually ran.

It is also not advice. A verifier can say that something falls short of the requirement. It cannot design your plan, rebuild your data flow or write your procedures, because it would then be verifying its own work.

The engagement usually runs through eight stages:

1. Engagement, with a conflict-of-interest check
2. Strategic analysis of your operation, fleet, routes and data flows
3. Risk assessment: where could a material misstatement arise?
4. A verification plan setting the sampling approach and evidence needed
5. Fieldwork and data testing
6. Findings raised
7. Your corrections, with evidence, and closure
8. The verification report, followed by your submission to the authority

First-time operators are often surprised by how much happens in stages 2 to 4, before any data is touched. The verifier has to understand your operation well enough to sample sensibly. A clear explanation of your data flow at that point reduces the sampling that follows. For what the testing itself looks like, see [running your own audit first](/insights/corsia-internal-audit-preparation/).

## Step 1: Draw up the shortlist

Screen on the things that are pass or fail before looking at anything else.

**Accreditation.** The body must be accredited for CORSIA verification under the applicable scheme and standard. Check it on the accreditation body's own register. A logo on a proposal is not a check.

**Acceptability to the DGCA.** For an Indian operator, confirm with the [DGCA](https://www.dgca.gov.in/) that the body is acceptable. The verification requirements themselves are set out in the [ICAO CORSIA documents](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/default.aspx).

**Independence.** More on this below, because it is where most mistakes happen.

Then screen on fit: has the body verified aircraft operators, not only factories and power plants? Does it have capacity in your deadline window, which is a genuine constraint?

## Step 2: Ask the right things in the request for proposal

A short request that forces comparable answers saves a lot of back and forth. We suggest asking each body for:

- the **named lead verifier and technical reviewer**, with their aviation experience
- a statement of **independence** covering the firm and the named individuals, including any advisory work for you or your consultant
- the **fee basis**: fixed, or a day rate with an estimate
- **what the base fee includes**, and what triggers extra charges
- the cost of a **re-visit** or re-testing after findings
- the **site work** they expect, and at which locations, since travel costs fall on you
- **indicative dates** for strategic analysis, fieldwork and the report
- what a **repeat year** would cost

Give them enough to price properly: fleet, number of international sectors, number of entities or AOCs, systems in use, and whether this is a first verification.

## Step 3: Compare the quotes

Fees for verification are driven by the same things as any audit. More aircraft and sectors mean more sampling. Each entity or AOC may need its own opinion. Poor data means more samples and more queries. A first year carries the strategic analysis. Outstation visits add travel. Findings mean re-testing. Engagements in the peak window cost more.

A worked comparison, with illustrative figures only:

| | Body A | Body B | Body C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fee basis | Fixed | Day rate with estimate | Fixed |
| Quoted fee | Lowest of the three | About 1.5 × Body A (estimate) | About 1.7 × Body A |
| Site work assumed | None stated | One day at head office | Head office plus one outstation |
| Re-testing after findings | Charged separately | Included in day-rate estimate up to two days | Included |
| Repeat-year indication | Not given | Around 70% of year one | Around 65% of year one |

Body A looks cheapest. But the required scope of testing is fixed by the assurance standard, so a much lower quote usually means a smaller assumed scope. Ask A what sampling it has priced, whether it has assumed no site work, and what findings would add. Often the gap to the others closes once those answers are in.

Body C's repeat-year indication is also informative. A firm that expects your systems to mature, and prices for that, is telling you something about how it sees the engagement.

Data quality moves this fee as much as anything. A verifier who can trace samples quickly does less work than one chasing documents across stations, and the difference shows up on the invoice as well as in the findings.

## Step 4: Test independence properly

The verifier must be independent of whoever prepared the report. In practice:

- your consultant cannot verify its own work
- the verification body cannot have advised on the same scope
- the individual verifiers, not just the firm, must be free of conflicts
- a long relationship may require the lead verifier to rotate

The common trap is a bundled offer of "compliance support and verification". The same entity cannot do both on the same scope. Where a large firm offers both through different divisions, the separation has to be real, and checking that is your job, not theirs. Our note on [consultant red flags](/insights/corsia-consultant-due-diligence-red-flags/) covers how bundled offers are often presented.

## Step 5: Appoint in the fourth quarter

Appoint before your compliance year closes. There are three advantages:

1. **Availability**, and usually a better fee, since capacity tightens near deadlines.
2. **An early start on strategic analysis**, so the verifier understands your operation before the data arrives.
3. **Early warning.** A verifier reviewing your plan against your process before year end can flag mismatches while they can still be fixed.

The third point does not compromise independence. Telling you that your plan says one thing and your stations do another is stating a fact, not designing your system. See [the compliance calendar](/insights/corsia-reporting-deadlines-and-calendar/) for where appointment sits in the year.

## After the appointment

Expect findings of four kinds: material misstatements, which must be corrected; non-material misstatements, corrected where practical; non-conformities, where practice departs from the plan; and observations, which are recommendations. Answer each with evidence rather than explanation, and contest a finding only where you have the documents to back your position. [Resolving verification findings](/insights/corsia-verification-findings-resolution/) goes into this in depth.

To make next year cheaper, keep the evidence pack structure the verifier used and fill it monthly, update the plan for anything verification exposed, close findings properly, and keep the same body where independence rules allow, so the strategic analysis is not rebuilt every year.

## Short answers

**Who can verify a CORSIA report?** An accredited body, independent of the operator and of the report's preparation, and recognised by the national authority.

**Does the verifier come on site?** Usually, at least in the first year. Travel costs sit with the operator.

**Can we choose a cheaper opinion?** No. The testing required is set by the assurance standard. A cheap quote usually means a narrower assumed scope.
