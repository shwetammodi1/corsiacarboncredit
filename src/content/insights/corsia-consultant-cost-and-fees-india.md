An operator sends the same brief to two firms and gets back two proposals. One is roughly a third of the other. The obvious reading is that one firm is expensive or the other is cutting corners. More often, neither is true: the two firms have made different guesses about how much work your data will need, and one of them has written that guess into the exclusions.

Nearly all of the price of CORSIA consulting comes down to the state of your fuel and flight data. Fleet size matters less than people expect. This article explains what drives the fee, how to structure the engagement so the price reflects reality, what else you will pay for, and how to read two proposals side by side.

![Chart of the factors that drive CORSIA consulting cost](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-consultant-cost-drivers.svg)

## What actually moves the price

In order of weight:

1. **The condition of your existing fuel data.** Per-flight data already reconciled is a review. Scattered station records are a data project. Two operators with identical fleets can differ threefold in cost on this alone, because one already reconciles fuel uplift to flight records and the other rebuilds it every year from paper.
2. **Fleet and route complexity.** More aircraft types and more State pairs mean more scope to define and test.
3. **Legal entities and Air Operator Certificates.** Each may need its own monitoring plan, report and verification.
4. **First cycle or repeat.** Year one carries the plan, the data flow and the procedures.
5. **Verification findings to close.** Unbudgeted rework, and the most avoidable cost on the list.
6. **The firm's brand and location.** Real, but the smallest factor.

### A two-day test before you ask for quotes

Pick flights at random from last year. For each, can you produce the fuel figure used, the source document behind it, and the person who owns the system it came from? If you can, say so in your brief; it should bring every quote down. If you cannot, that gap is the work, and leaving it out of the brief only turns it into a variation order later.

## Pricing structures, and when each fits

**Fixed fee per deliverable.** A price for each document: plan, report, verification support. Works for a well-defined first cycle.

**Annual retainer.** One fee covering the whole compliance cycle. Suits repeat years with predictable scope.

**Time and materials.** A day rate against an estimate. Reasonable only where the scope is genuinely uncertain.

**Phased.** A fixed fee for a gap analysis, followed by a priced scope for the rest. In our view this is usually the most honest structure for a first engagement. A paid gap analysis of a week or two lets both sides price the real work rather than guess, and gives you a cheap trial of how the firm actually operates before committing to the full cycle.

**Success-linked.** Part of the fee paid on plan acceptance or a clean verification. Rare, and it needs care. A verifier's opinion is meant to be independent, and a fee that rewards a particular opinion puts pressure exactly where there should be none.

## Worked comparison: two proposals for one brief

The details below are **illustrative**, with no real firms or prices. An Indian operator with two aircraft types, one AOC and messy outstation fuel records issues one brief and receives two proposals.

| | Proposal 1 | Proposal 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Headline fee | Lower | Higher |
| Structure | Fixed fee for plan and report | Paid gap analysis, then fixed fee for scoped work |
| Data remediation | Excluded | Priced after the gap analysis |
| Who does the work | Not stated | Named consultant, days by grade |
| Findings handling | Not mentioned | Defined; consultant time included up to a stated limit |
| Year two | Same fee | Lower fee, with handover items named |

Against clean data, Proposal 1 might be the better buy. Against this operator's data, its exclusion of remediation means the real cost arrives later as variation orders, probably near the deadline. Proposal 2's higher headline is closer to what the operator will actually pay, and it shows how the cost falls in year two.

The lesson: compare what is excluded before comparing totals. A low fee that excludes data remediation, set against messy data, is not a low fee.

## What to put in the brief

Proposals only compare if the brief is specific. Give every firm the same information:

- fleet list, aircraft types and approximate number of international sectors;
- the number of legal entities and AOCs in scope;
- a candid description of your fuel data and systems;
- whether a monitoring plan exists and has been accepted;
- the compliance year in scope;
- whether you need verification support;
- whether you need knowledge transfer, and for how many people;
- how much of the work your own team can do.

Then require every quote to set out deliverables by name, who will do the work, days by grade, exclusions, how findings are handled, and what year two costs. The questions to put to the firm itself are in [questions to ask a CORSIA consultant](/insights/questions-to-ask-a-corsia-consultant/).

## The costs outside the consultant's fee

The consultant is seldom the largest line in a compliance budget. Budget separately for the verification body's fee; internal staff time (compliance lead, fuel and operations data owners, finance); system changes such as reporting extracts, station data capture and retention; training so the cycle can be run in-house; emissions units where offsetting applies; registry account and transaction charges; legal review of unit purchase contracts and corresponding adjustment terms; and a contingency for findings, where 10–15% is realistic in a first cycle.

Keep verification as a separate purchase. It must be independent of whoever prepares the report, so a proposal offering "verification included" needs a closer look. Either the verifier is subcontracted at arm's length and disclosed, or the independence model is broken. See [choosing a CORSIA verification body in India](/insights/corsia-verification-body-selection-india/).

### How a first-year budget is typically split

**Illustrative** shares for a first cycle at a mid-sized operator, excluding units. Absolute figures depend on your data; the proportions are what transfer.

- Consultant, gap analysis and monitoring plan: 25%
- Consultant, data flow, procedures and dry run: 20%
- Consultant, verification support and submission: 15%
- Verification body: 20%
- Internal staff time, costed: 10%
- Systems and reporting changes: 5%
- Contingency for findings: 5%

The consultant accounts for about 60%, not 90%. Verification and internal time are substantial and often left off the first approval request, which is why CORSIA budgets are so often exceeded on paper when nothing has gone wrong. Emissions units are not in this list at all; where offsetting applies they are a separate and potentially much larger line, covered in [managing CORSIA compliance costs](/insights/corsia-compliance-cost-management/).

## Making year two cheaper

A properly run first cycle leaves five lasting assets: an accepted monitoring plan, a documented data flow with named owners, written procedures including the data gap procedure, a structure for the verification evidence pack, and a trained internal compliance lead.

With all five in place, year two is review, quality checks and verification support rather than a build. The three consultant lines should shrink noticeably, verification should stay about flat, and internal time should rise as the work moves in-house. If year two is priced the same as year one, the assets were probably not built or not handed over.

So write knowledge transfer into the contract with named items: a procedures document, a data flow diagram, an evidence pack template and a working session with your team. It is the clause with the best return in the whole agreement.

## Developer-side costs look different

For a project developer supplying CORSIA-eligible credits, the costs resemble project development rather than compliance advisory: eligibility and feasibility screening (early, and cheap relative to what it prevents); a substantial project design document; third-party validation; a programme registration fee; monitoring systems with ongoing running costs; verification for each issuance; support in seeking a corresponding adjustment; and a buyer documentation pack, which is small and tends to raise the price achieved.

The corresponding adjustment is the item that breaks timelines, because it depends on a host government decision rather than anything the developer or adviser does. Budget for elapsed time as well as effort. See [corresponding adjustments for Indian CORSIA projects](/insights/corsia-corresponding-adjustment-india/).

## Why cheap can cost more

The usual failure is not a badly written document. It is a document never tested against a verifier: a template monitoring plan and an unvalidated data flow that pass internal review and then produce material findings. Closing them takes consultant time, verifier rework, internal time and, close to a deadline, a good deal of stress. The cheapest real saving available is getting your fuel data in order before the engagement starts. It lowers the fee, lowers verification effort and reduces conservative adjustments where data is incomplete.

For repeat cycles, running the work in-house often saves money; for a first cycle, the risk of an unaccepted plan or failed verification usually outweighs it. The trade-off is set out in [consultant, in-house or hybrid](/insights/corsia-consultant-vs-in-house-team/).
