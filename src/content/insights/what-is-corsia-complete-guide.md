If you need to explain CORSIA to a chief executive in three sentences, try these. It is a global rule, agreed through the UN's aviation body, that makes airlines pay for growth in the CO2 from their international flights. They pay by buying and cancelling a special, scarce kind of carbon credit, or by using certified sustainable fuel. The rule is real and enforceable, but several of its settings are still moving.

The rest of this guide fills in each of those sentences.

![Overview of CORSIA and the carbon units it uses](/images/corsia/hero-corsia-carbon-credit.svg)

## The name and the idea

CORSIA is the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation. It was the first global market-based measure applied to a single industry.

Its stated objective is carbon-neutral growth. The sector can keep expanding, but emissions growth above a reference baseline must be neutralised with carbon units that are purchased and then cancelled.

Be clear about what that means. CORSIA does not itself cut aviation emissions. It makes the sector pay for reductions elsewhere in the economy that would not otherwise have happened. You can argue about whether that is enough. You cannot argue about whether it binds operators in participating States; it does.

## How aviation ended up with its own scheme

Emissions from a flight between two countries do not sit naturally in either country's national inventory. The Kyoto Protocol passed the problem to the [International Civil Aviation Organization](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/default.aspx) instead of solving it, and two decades of slow progress followed.

In the middle of that, the European Union tried to bring every flight touching an EU airport into its Emissions Trading System. The diplomatic pushback was strong enough that the EU narrowed the scope to flights within Europe while ICAO worked on a global answer. The ICAO Assembly agreed that answer in 2016. The history is told in more detail in [why CORSIA exists](/knowledge-base/why-corsia-exists/).

## Who is bound, and who is not

| In scope | Out of scope |
|---|---|
| Aeroplane operators flying internationally | Domestic flights, which stay under national policy |
| Emitting more than 10,000 tonnes of CO2 a year from those flights | Operators under the 10,000-tonne threshold (exempt from offsetting, though not always from reporting) |
| Using aeroplanes with a maximum certificated take-off mass above 5,700 kg | Humanitarian, medical and firefighting flights |
| | State aircraft, including military |

Two details change the practical picture.

**Offsetting follows routes.** An obligation arises only on routes between two participating States. A flight from a participating State to a non-participating one owes nothing, though its emissions are still monitored and reported. So when a State joins or leaves, an operator's obligation can move significantly without a single schedule change.

**Reporting reaches further.** Above the threshold, you monitor, report and verify every year, whether or not you owe offsets. [Scope and thresholds](/knowledge-base/corsia-scope-and-thresholds/) sets out the tests.

## How the obligation is sized

The requirement starts from reported emissions on covered routes, multiplied by a growth factor.

**The baseline moved once.** It was designed as the average of 2019 and 2020 emissions. The collapse in 2020 traffic would have made that line artificially low and every later obligation much larger, so the ICAO Council switched to 2019 alone. Practical, and a reminder that CORSIA's parameters can change.

**Two growth factors.** The sectoral factor reflects how far the whole covered sector has grown against the baseline. In the early phases, obligations rest entirely on it, so every operator shares the sector's burden in proportion to its own emissions. The individual factor reflects an operator's own growth. From 2030 weight shifts progressively towards it, so faster-growing carriers carry more of their own expansion.

**The fuels deduction.** Qualifying sustainable aviation fuel or lower-carbon aviation fuel reduces the requirement, provided it meets the sustainability criteria and the chain of custody is documented.

A worked example of the calculation is in [calculating a CORSIA offsetting requirement](/insights/corsia-offsetting-requirements-calculation/).

## The yearly routine

![The annual CORSIA compliance cycle](/images/corsia/corsia-compliance-cycle.svg)

1. **Monitor** fuel use under an Emissions Monitoring Plan approved by the national authority. ICAO allows several monitoring methods. The choice matters, because some need a level of data detail that older fleet systems do not produce.
2. **Report** the previous calendar year's emissions to the authority in an annual Emissions Report.
3. **Verify** through an accredited, independent verification body, which checks the report against ICAO Annex 16 Volume IV. This is neither optional nor a formality.
4. **Calculate** the requirement for the compliance period once ICAO publishes the growth factors.
5. **Acquire and cancel** CORSIA Eligible Emissions Units in a registry. Cancellation, not purchase, discharges the obligation.
6. **Report the cancellation** to the authority in an Emissions Unit Cancellation Report.

Compliance periods are three years long. That gives room on timing, and it tempts operators to leave buying to the end. Those who did have been caught out when eligible supply turned out thinner than they assumed.

## What a compliant unit is

This is the point most newcomers get wrong. A carbon credit and a CORSIA unit are not the same thing. A CORSIA Eligible Emissions Unit must meet every line below:

| Requirement | What it means |
|---|---|
| Approved programme | Issued by a crediting programme the ICAO Council approved after assessment by its Technical Advisory Body against the Emissions Unit Criteria |
| Sound methodology | Generated under an approved methodology, with additionality shown and reductions conservatively quantified |
| Independent verification | Checked by an accredited third party |
| Eligible vintage | Inside the vintage window for the relevant compliance period |
| Corresponding adjustment | The host State adds the tonnes back into its own national accounting, so the reduction is not counted twice |
| No net harm | Does not breach host-State law or social and environmental safeguards |
| Clean registry record | Uniquely serialised, with a transparent cancellation record |

**The corresponding adjustment is the constraint that binds.** A project can be well designed, fully verified, issuing and selling freely into the voluntary market, and still be unable to supply CORSIA because its government will not authorise. Governments often hesitate, because each authorisation makes their own Nationally Determined Contribution harder to meet.

The result is a two-tier market. Eligible supply is far scarcer than general voluntary supply and trades at a substantial premium. Benchmarking CORSIA prices against published voluntary averages means reading the wrong number. See [eligible emissions units](/insights/corsia-eligible-emissions-units-explained/) for the full set of tests.

## Programmes: approval is necessary, not sufficient

ICAO assesses programmes, not individual projects. Approval can be full or conditional, is reviewed periodically, and has lapsed for programmes that stopped meeting the criteria, notably on how they handled corresponding adjustments.

Programmes that have held approval include the American Carbon Registry, Architecture for REDD+ Transactions, the Climate Action Reserve, the Global Carbon Council, [Verra](https://verra.org/) and [Gold Standard](https://www.goldstandard.org/). The position changes, so rely only on the [ICAO emissions units page](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/CORSIA-Emissions-Units.aspx).

An approved programme still issues many units that are not eligible, because vintage, the adjustment and the other unit-level tests apply one unit at a time.

## Where India stands

India participates, so Indian carriers on international routes are in scope. The [Directorate General of Civil Aviation](https://www.dgca.gov.in/) is the national authority: it approves monitoring plans, receives annual reports and oversees verification.

For Indian project developers the position has more layers. India has been building its domestic Carbon Credit Trading Scheme under the Energy Conservation Act framework, and how that scheme relates to Article 6 authorisation and to CORSIA eligibility is still being settled. A developer who plans to sell abroad should decide which route the credits will take before choosing a methodology. Moving a project onto a different standard later is expensive and sometimes not possible. [CORSIA in India](/knowledge-base/corsia-in-india/) covers both sides.

## Common misreadings

- **"CORSIA is a tax on tickets."** It is an offsetting obligation on operators, settled in carbon units.
- **"Any good carbon credit will do."** Only eligible units count, a far narrower category.
- **"No offsets owed means nothing to do."** Monitoring, reporting and verification still apply above the threshold.
- **"ICAO enforces it."** Enforcement is by the national authority under domestic law. Penalties differ by State, but they are real.
- **"The rules are fixed until 2035."** See the next section.

## What is still open

A fair guide has to say plainly that CORSIA is still being built.

- **Participation** is voluntary until the second phase, and States have both joined and left.
- **Eligible supply** is uncertain. Analyses have repeatedly projected a shortfall of adjusted units against demand, and price risk follows from that.
- **Vintage windows** are set by the Council and have been extended before.
- **Programme approvals** change.
- **The link to Article 6** keeps evolving as the Paris Agreement mechanisms mature.
- **Second-phase details**, including exemption thresholds and review outcomes, are still negotiable.

None of this weakens the obligation. It does mean a strategy that assumes today's settings will hold unchanged to 2035 carries risk nobody has priced.

## Where to begin

**Operators:** confirm whether you are above the threshold, get the monitoring plan right before thinking about units, model the requirement over the whole compliance period, and understand how participation changes would affect your routes.

**Developers:** put the corresponding adjustment question to your host State before spending anything else. Everything downstream depends on the answer.

**Both:** write down the reasoning behind each decision. Verification and audit arrive years after the choice, and an undocumented judgement is hard to defend.

::: accordion Is CORSIA mandatory?
Monitoring and reporting have been mandatory for operators above the threshold from the start. Offsetting applies on routes between participating States, and participation becomes mandatory from 2027 for States meeting defined aviation activity thresholds.
:::
