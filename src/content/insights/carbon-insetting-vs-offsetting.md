Consider two ways an Indian clothing brand could spend the same climate budget. It could buy and retire credits from a forest project on another continent. Or it could pay the cotton farmers who supply it to change practices that cut emissions from the cotton it buys. The first is **offsetting**. The second is **insetting**. The example is illustrative, but the choice is one many companies now face.

Insetting is sometimes sold as the more credible cousin of offsetting, and sometimes it is just offsetting with a new label. The difference is real when it is done properly, and it changes both what you achieve and what you can claim.

## Where the tonne is cut

The whole distinction is about location.

- **Offsetting** funds a reduction **outside** your operations and value chain. You buy a credit from a project you have no business relationship with, and retire it.
- **Insetting** funds a reduction **inside** your value chain, usually among the suppliers who make or grow what you sell.

Put plainly: an offset pays for someone else's project, while an inset fixes emissions attached to your own product.

## Why companies are drawn to it

For most companies, the bulk of the footprint is in **Scope 3**, the emissions of their supply chain rather than their own sites and electricity. Offsets do not change those numbers. They compensate for them elsewhere.

A well-run inset can cut Scope 3 emissions at source: a farmer using lower-emission methods, a supplier switching fuel, a transport partner burning less diesel. Because the cut happens inside the value chain, it can lower the company's reported footprint rather than simply sit beside it. ## The measurement trap

Offsets come as discrete units, tracked in a registry with serial numbers. An inset is a change deep inside a supply chain, and it has to pass three tests an offset gets for free:

1. **Can it be measured?** Someone has to quantify the change on farms or in factories you do not run.
2. **Is it yours?** The reduction must be attributed to your company and your purchases specifically.
3. **Is anyone else claiming it?** The supplier, or another brand buying from the same supplier, may count the same reduction.

Weak insetting over-claims just as easily as weak offsetting. The failure looks different, but the discipline is the same: a credible baseline, genuine additionality and careful attribution. Our [additionality explainer](/insights/additionality-in-carbon-credits-explained/) applies here too.

## What each lets you report

| | Offset | Inset |
|---|---|---|
| Location of the reduction | Outside your value chain | Inside your value chain |
| How to report it | As compensation, separate from your footprint | As a reduction in your footprint, usually Scope 3 |
| Typical example | Retiring a REDD+ credit | Paying for regenerative practices at suppliers |
| Main risk | Credit quality and double counting | Measurement and attribution |

The reporting line is where companies go wrong. An offset should be disclosed as compensation, not netted off your emissions total as though you had cut it. A genuine inset can show up as a lower footprint. Blurring the two is a common route to greenwashing, and keeping them apart is what makes a net zero claim hold up. See [carbon neutral vs net zero](/insights/carbon-neutral-vs-net-zero/) for the claims side.

## Most companies end up doing both

Insetting targets the emissions you actually have, which is generally preferable. It is also slower and harder to evidence. So the usual pattern is to inset where the supply chain allows it, and to use high-integrity offsets for the residual you cannot yet reach.

## Short answers

**What is insetting?** Investing in reductions within your own value chain, typically among suppliers.

**Can it reduce Scope 3?** Yes, if the reduction is inside your value chain, additional and correctly attributed to you.

**Is it better than offsetting?** It addresses your real emissions, but it is harder to measure and attribute.
