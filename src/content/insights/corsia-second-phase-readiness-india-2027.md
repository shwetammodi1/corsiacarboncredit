Imagine two Indian carriers that each burn enough fuel on international flights to emit the same amount of CO2 every year. One mostly flies to States already in CORSIA. The other has built its network around trade and diaspora routes, many of them to States that are not participating today. Ask how much their CORSIA cost will rise in 2027 and the honest answer for the first is "not much", and for the second "possibly a great deal".

That difference is the whole story of the second phase for Indian operators. The dates are known. The effect depends on where you fly.

![Planning for mandatory participation in CORSIA's second phase](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-2027-readiness.svg)

## The two changes

**2027: participation becomes mandatory** for States above set aviation activity thresholds, broadly those with a significant share of international activity measured in revenue tonne kilometres. Least developed countries, small island developing States, landlocked developing countries and States with very small aviation sectors stay exempt unless they choose to join. Once a destination State is in, the route pair to it starts attracting an offsetting obligation where before it did not. The [phases and timeline](/knowledge-base/corsia-phases-and-timeline/) set out the full sequence.

**2030: your own growth starts to count.** Until 2029 the obligation uses only the sectoral growth factor. After that, an individual component is blended in:

- **2030 to 2032:** 85% sectoral, 15% individual
- **2033 to 2035:** 70% sectoral, 30% individual

An operator growing faster than the global sector carries more of its own growth from 2030, and roughly twice that weighting from 2033. [Baseline and growth factors](/knowledge-base/corsia-baseline-and-growth-factors/) explains how the two factors combine.

## A worked example: same emissions, different step

All figures are illustrative, chosen to show the mechanism. They are not forecasts and do not describe any real airline.

| | Carrier North | Carrier South |
|---|---|---|
| International CO2 per year (in scope) | 1,000,000 t | 1,000,000 t |
| Covered today (both ends participating) | 800,000 t | 300,000 t |
| Covered under a 2027 mandatory-participation scenario | 850,000 t | 800,000 t |
| Increase in covered emissions | +50,000 t (+6%) | +500,000 t (+167%) |

Carrier North's routes mostly count already, so the second phase adds little. Carrier South's covered emissions nearly treble because a large block of previously uncovered flying enters scope in one step.

Now try estimating either with a flat uplift, say "add 25%". North would be over-budgeted and South badly under-budgeted. There is no general multiplier. The only reliable estimate comes from your own route network.

## Why Indian carriers feel it more

Two features of the Indian market interact with these changes.

- **Network shape.** Indian carriers serve many destinations chosen for trade and diaspora demand, not for CORSIA participation. Some of those States are not in the scheme today. When they become mandatory participants, coverage widens.
- **Growth.** Indian aviation is one of the fastest-growing markets in the world. From 2030, growing faster than the sector means carrying proportionally more, with the weight rising again in 2033.

Put together, **the CORSIA cost curve of a growing Indian carrier rises faster than its emissions curve.** That is a feature of the rules, not a prediction, and it belongs in the fleet and network model.

## Building the model

1. **Current covered emissions.** International flights on aeroplanes above 5,700 kg, exempt types removed, on route pairs where both States participate today.
2. **The participation scenario.** List your destination States and mark which will fall above the activity thresholds. Some calls at the margins need judgement; write down each assumption.
3. **Re-run covered emissions** under that scenario on your actual routes.
4. **Take the difference.** For many operators this is the single largest figure in the CORSIA forward plan.
5. **Project it forward** on the fleet and network plan. Your 2027 network will not be today's.
6. **Add individual growth** from 2030 using the weighting schedule above.
7. **Stress the supply side.** Assume eligible units are scarce or much dearer when the whole sector's obligation rises at once, and see what that does to the budget.

Step 4 gates everything else. Without it, procurement, budget and board reporting are guesses. [Budgeting and forecasting CORSIA costs](/insights/corsia-budgeting-and-forecasting-india/) covers how to carry the number into the financial plan.

## Order of work before 2027

| When | Action | Why now |
|---|---|---|
| Now | Model the network change | Every other decision depends on the size of the step |
| Now | Fix any data process that relies on heroic manual effort | More covered flights means more data; fixing a pipeline under load is far harder |
| Within a year | Open and test registry accounts, with more than one authorised representative | Doing it while the whole sector transacts is the avoidable version |
| Within a year | Assess two or three unit suppliers | In a thin market, known counterparties beat a slightly better price found under pressure |
| Before 2027 | Decide the procurement stance: forward, spot or staged | Leaving it undecided is a decision to buy late |
| Before 2027 | Brief the board with the modelled number | A specific figure, not a general warning |
| From 2030 | Link fleet planning to the individual growth factor | So growth decisions show their compliance cost when they are made |

## The supply argument, briefly

On the same date, demand steps up across every covered operator. Supply depends on host-State authorisation decisions nobody can forecast, and analyses have repeatedly projected authorised supply falling short of obligations.

The downside is lopsided. If supply turns out better than feared, an operator who contracted early has overpaid a little. If it turns out worse, an operator who waited faces high prices, little availability and a legal obligation it cannot meet. Those outcomes are not equally bad. That imbalance, not any price forecast, is the case for acting early rather than trying to time the market. See [supply and demand outlook](/knowledge-base/corsia-supply-and-demand-outlook/).

## What the commercial team should hear

- **Route profitability changes.** A route to a newly mandatory State picks up an offsetting cost. On a marginal route that is not trivial, and it belongs in the route model.
- **Efficiency is worth slightly more.** The obligation scales with covered emissions, so a more fuel-efficient aircraft has a somewhat stronger case from 2027.
- **Growth gets progressively dearer from 2030.** Not an argument against expansion, but for making its cost visible when it is planned.
- **SAF becomes relatively more attractive** as unit prices and growth factors rise, since eligible fuel cuts the requirement directly. A comparison run three years ago may be out of date.

Framed this way, it is a cost line with a known step and several levers. Treated only as a compliance matter, it arrives as a surprise.

## Parameters can still move

CORSIA includes periodic reviews, and they have been used: after the pandemic, the baseline was changed from a 2019–2020 average to 2019 alone. Growth factor weights, vintage windows or phase design could be adjusted again. States can join or leave, and programme approvals change.

That is a reason to plan flexibly, not to wait. Avoid piling into one vintage, do not assume a fixed price path, spread purchases across programmes, and rerun the model whenever the ICAO Council takes a decision. An adviser who presents the 2027 and 2030 settings as fixed is not watching the scheme closely.

## Short answers

**Will our obligation rise in 2027?** If you fly to States that become mandatory participants, yes. By how much depends on your network.

**Which States will be mandatory participants?** Those above the activity thresholds, less the exempt categories unless they volunteer. Check the current ICAO position, not a list saved some time ago.

**Does the individual growth factor apply to everyone?** Yes, from 2030, at 15% then 30%. It bites hardest on operators growing faster than the sector.

**Should we contract forward now?** That depends on how confident you are in your volume forecast. A forward contract secures supply as well as price, and in a thin market that matters more.
