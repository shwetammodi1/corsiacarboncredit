Book a meeting room for a day, well before the compliance year starts. Bring the compliance lead, whoever owns fuel data, whoever owns the flight schedule, someone from finance and, for the last hour at least, the accountable manager. Work through the questions below. By the end of the day you will know where the next cycle is likely to hurt, while there is still time to do something about it.

Most CORSIA trouble can be seen months before it gets expensive. This checklist is built to find it early.

![Workflow of a CORSIA engagement from assessment to reporting](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-engagement-workflow.svg)

## How to score

Give each question one of three marks:

- **2**: fully in place, and someone in the room can show the evidence now
- **1**: partly in place, or in place but nobody can show it
- **0**: not in place

Two rules keep the exercise honest. A 2 with no evidence on the table is a 1. And a 1 that should have been a 0 does not save you anything; it just moves the discovery to verification.

We have ordered the six areas by **fix priority**, not by the order CORSIA is usually taught. The reason is lead time, explained at the end of this piece.

## Area A: Data and systems

This area predicts the verification outcome better than any other. See [fuel data quality](/insights/corsia-fuel-data-quality-management/) for the detail behind it.

- **A1.** Is fuel captured per flight for every international sector?
- **A2.** Can any source document be retrieved within a day?
- **A3.** Does outstation data arrive in a set format by a set deadline?
- **A4.** Is a completeness check run every month, with evidence it was done?
- **A5.** Are anomaly and duplicate checks run every month?
- **A6.** Is reported fuel reconciled to fuel purchase records every month?
- **A7.** Does a gap log record every gap, each substitution and who approved it?
- **A8.** Are unit and density conversions written down and applied the same way everywhere?

If A2 and A6 both score 0, expect a hard first verification however well the plan reads.

## Area B: The monitoring plan

- **B1.** Does a monitoring plan exist, accepted by the authority?
- **B2.** Is the accepted version clearly identified and under version control?
- **B3.** Does the fleet list in the plan match today's fleet?
- **B4.** Is the fuel monitoring method stated, and applied consistently?
- **B5.** Does the data flow section name the systems and the people who own them?
- **B6.** Is there a data gap procedure that is conservative and was set in advance?
- **B7.** Do control procedures say who checks what, and how often?
- **B8.** Is there a change control route that carries operational changes into the plan?

## Area C: Scope and applicability

- **C1.** Is there a written, signed-off applicability determination?
- **C2.** Is every legal entity and AOC in scope identified?
- **C3.** Has the aircraft mass criterion been applied to the current fleet?
- **C4.** Is international versus domestic classification written down and built into systems?
- **C5.** Are exempt flight categories identified, with evidence?
- **C6.** Are the route pairs that carry an offsetting requirement identified for this year?
- **C7.** Is someone tracking changes to the list of participating States?
- **C8.** Is responsibility for wet-leased and dry-leased aircraft documented?

C8 is the one teams most often over-score. If nobody can point to the lease clause or correspondence that settles responsibility, it is a 0.

## Area D: Verification readiness

- **D1.** Is a verification body identified and appointed?
- **D2.** Have its accreditation and acceptability been checked?
- **D3.** Is its independence from your consultant and your own team confirmed?
- **D4.** Does an evidence pack structure exist, filled as the year goes on?
- **D5.** Is there a worked trace of one flight from source record to report?
- **D6.** Is an internal dry run scheduled before fieldwork?
- **D7.** Are last year's findings closed with evidence, not just marked closed?

Our note on [preparing for the verifier](/insights/corsia-internal-audit-preparation/) covers D5 and D6 in practice.

## Area E: Offsetting, where it applies

- **E1.** Is the offsetting requirement forecast as a low, central and high case?
- **E2.** Does approved budget cover the high case?
- **E3.** Is a registry account open and active?
- **E4.** Is there a due diligence process for units that covers corresponding adjustment status?
- **E5.** Is buying spread out, rather than bunched at the deadline?
- **E6.** Does any SAF claim have certification and chain-of-custody evidence?
- **E7.** Is the cancellation date in the calendar, with time allowed for transfers?

E3 catches someone every year. Opening a registry account takes longer than people expect and cannot be hurried at the last minute. See [registry account setup](/insights/corsia-registry-account-setup-india/).

## Area F: Governance and continuity

- **F1.** Does a named compliance lead own the cycle?
- **F2.** Is there a named deputy who has been through a verification?
- **F3.** Is the accountable manager engaged, and do they understand what they sign?
- **F4.** Is the compliance calendar held centrally, not in one person's inbox?
- **F5.** Are there written procedures for the annual cycle?
- **F6.** Is someone watching for regulatory change and reporting it?
- **F7.** Does record retention meet the required period, and can records be retrieved?

## Reading the result

There are 45 questions, so the maximum is 90. Ignore the total. A healthy overall score can hide one area that will fail on its own. Convert each area to a percentage and read it against this:

| Area score | What it means |
|---|---|
| 80% and above | Under control. Maintain and refine. |
| 60% to 79% | Workable. Close the named gaps before the year begins. |
| 40% to 59% | Real risk in the coming cycle. Deal with the zeros first. |
| Under 40% | This is a first build, not a tune-up. Get help. |

## Worked example: from score to plan

Illustrative only. A small Indian operator running two A320-family aircraft on Gulf routes scores itself:

| Area | Score | Maximum | % |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: Data | 7 | 16 | 44% |
| B: Plan | 12 | 16 | 75% |
| C: Scope | 13 | 16 | 81% |
| D: Verification | 6 | 14 | 43% |
| E: Offsetting | 5 | 14 | 36% |
| F: Governance | 9 | 14 | 64% |

The total is 52 of 90, about 58%, which sounds middling. The area view tells a sharper story: data and verification are both in the "real risk" band, and offsetting is a first build.

Each 0 and 1 becomes a line with an owner and a date. For this operator the first lines were:

- **A2, source documents not retrievable** (outstation dockets held on paper by the handling agent). Owner: operations. Fix: scan on receipt, and write the requirement into the handling agreement. Lead time one to three months.
- **A6, no monthly reconciliation.** Owner: compliance with finance. Fix: define the routine, calendar it, keep evidence. Two to four weeks. Start this week.
- **E3, no registry account.** Owner: finance. Fix: begin the application now. Lead time unpredictable.
- **F2, no deputy.** Owner: accountable manager. Fix: appoint one and put them in the room at verification.
- **B3, plan fleet list out of date** after a lease return. Owner: compliance lead. Fix: update and resubmit. Four to eight weeks.

Notice that E3 went near the top despite offsetting being a later priority area. That is the second sequencing rule below.

## Why the order matters

**Data before documents.** A monitoring plan can be rewritten in a few weeks. A year of clean fuel data cannot be produced after the event at all. Anything that changes what happens at a station or inside a system goes to the front; anything that only changes wording can follow.

**Unpredictable lead times start now.** Registry accounts and dealings with the authority do not speed up under pressure. Starting early costs nothing, so start them immediately whatever their score.

Review the action list monthly until every line is closed. Run the full checklist again at mid-year; that June run is the one that still leaves time to act. Keep every completed sheet. The trend across years is evidence of a maturing control environment, and verifiers notice it.

## Using it with an adviser

If you bring in outside help, a completed checklist is the most useful thing you can give them. It shortens their gap analysis and lets you compare quotes, because every firm is pricing the same stated position.

Score the weak areas honestly for a practical reason: a consultant who quoted against a disclosed weakness has a hard time later claiming a variation for "unexpected data remediation". Hide the zeros and you get a lower quote and a larger final bill. After the first cycle, run the checklist again. The change in score is the plainest evidence of whether the engagement built capability or just produced a report.
