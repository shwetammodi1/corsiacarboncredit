Suppose an operator cancels a parcel of units, files its cancellation report, and months later learns that the programme's approval was conditional in a way that excluded the methodology those units came from. The money is gone, and so are the units. The obligation is still there, now discovered late, and replacement supply may not exist at any reasonable price.

That is why diligence on CORSIA units is not a box-ticking exercise. This checklist is arranged in the order we work through it: the tests that end a deal outright, then the eligibility checks, then the risk checks, then the evidence file.

![Diagram of the CORSIA eligibility criteria for emissions units](/images/corsia/corsia-eeu-criteria.svg)

## Walk-away tests

If any of these is true, stop.

- There is no host-government authorisation document and no credible timetable for one.
- The programme's approval has lapsed, or is conditional in a way that touches these units.
- The vintage is outside the window for your compliance period.
- The seller will not let you confirm the holding in the registry.
- You are offered only summaries or certificates, and the underlying evidence is withheld.
- You are being pushed to sign before diligence is finished.

The last one needs a word. Supply really is scarce, and honest sellers will say so. But urgency is also the standard way to cut diligence short, and a unit bought in a hurry that later fails is worse than one never bought.

## The three eligibility checks

These decide whether the unit works for CORSIA at all. They are cheap to run and must be done in full on every purchase, whatever the size. The underlying rules are set out in [the ICAO emissions unit criteria](/knowledge-base/corsia-emissions-unit-criteria/).

### Programme approval

| Check | What to look at | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Approved by the ICAO Council today? | The [ICAO emissions units page](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/CORSIA-Emissions-Units.aspx) on the day of the deal, not a saved list, a seller's site or an article | Approvals change by Council decision; some have lapsed |
| Full or conditional? | The approval text | Conditions often concern how the programme handles corresponding adjustments, or particular methodologies and project types |
| Does any condition reach these units? | The condition against the units' methodology | A condition excluding your methodology makes the units unusable |
| Next review date? | ICAO documentation | Matters if you are buying forward from future issuance |

### Vintage

| Check | What to look at | Why |
|---|---|---|
| When did the reduction happen? | The monitoring period in the registry record | The issuance date is not the vintage |
| Inside the window for *your* period? | The window for your compliance period specifically | Eligible for some period is not enough |
| How near the edge? | Distance from the window boundary | Boundaries can move, and you may not cancel in time |
| Who carries window risk on a forward? | The contract | If the window shifts before delivery, someone absorbs it |

Our [guide to vintages and windows](/knowledge-base/corsia-vintages-and-eligibility-windows/) explains how the windows are set.

### Corresponding adjustment

This check removes more supply than all the others together, so take it slowly.

1. **Is there a formal authorisation document** from the designated national authority? An email from the seller or a line on a project webpage does not count.
2. **Does it name these units**, by volume, vintage or project output? A general statement that the country supports Article 6 is not an authorisation.
3. **Has the adjustment been applied, or only promised?** Know which, and what happens if application does not follow.
4. **Does national reporting show it?** It should eventually appear in the host country's Article 6 reporting.
5. **Could it be withdrawn or narrowed?** Governments have changed position; judge the specific country.
6. **If the seller says "pending", what exactly is pending, and by when?** A unit pending authorisation is not eligible. If you go ahead, price and contract for that. The commercial side is covered in [pricing corresponding adjustment risk](/insights/corresponding-adjustments-corsia-explained/).

## The four risk checks

These do not decide eligibility on their own, but they decide whether the unit is sound and whether you will receive it.

**Verification.** The report should come from a body accredited under the programme's rules, cover the monitoring period the units come from, and be independent of the developer and free of any validator conflict. Read any qualifications. A qualified opinion is not automatically disqualifying, but you need to know what was qualified and why.

**Registry and custody.** Units must be uniquely serialised, with an unbroken record from issuance to the current holder. Confirm that no part of the parcel has been retired, cancelled or claimed already, that there are no liens, options or earlier commitments over it, and that the seller actually holds it. A broker selling units it does not hold adds a delivery risk.

**Project integrity.** Ask whether additionality still stands up for this project in this market today; a case that worked ten years ago may not now, especially for grid renewables where they are the cheapest option. Look at the assumptions behind quantification, particularly usage rates for distributed technologies and baseline rates for land-based projects. For stored carbon, check buffer contributions, monitoring commitments and legal protection, bearing in mind that buffer adequacy is under review as reversal losses mount. Check for environmental and social assessment, consultation, free, prior and informed consent where relevant, and a grievance mechanism; land tenure disputes are the most frequent failure, particularly in forestry. Note any public criticism or ratings downgrades. Our [guide to assessing credit quality](/insights/carbon-credit-quality-assessment/) goes further.

**Counterparty.** Identify the legal seller and its standing, whether it holds the units or is intermediating, and its record of delivering CORSIA units specifically. Structure payment to limit exposure: escrow, stages, or payment against delivery.

## The contract

Six questions the contract must answer:

- What does the seller warrant about eligibility, and what is the remedy if the warranty is broken?
- On a forward, who carries authorisation risk?
- Who carries vintage window risk?
- What happens if delivery comes too late for your cancellation deadline?
- Is delivery of the full documentation pack an obligation?
- Which law governs, how are disputes resolved, and would that actually work given where the seller is based?

## The evidence file

The standard to aim for: could someone who played no part in the deal rebuild the eligibility case from your files alone, three years from now, without calling the seller?

Keep at least:

- the signed contract;
- registry records showing serial numbers and the chain of custody;
- the authorisation document and evidence of the corresponding adjustment;
- a captured record of programme approval status on the transaction date;
- the verification report and any qualifications;
- project design documents;
- transfer and cancellation confirmations, with serials and references;
- the Emissions Unit Cancellation Report and the authority's acknowledgement.

Save copies rather than links. Web pages change, and a URL does not prove what a page said on the day you relied on it.

## How deep to go

Not every purchase needs the same effort.

| Part of the checklist | Depth |
|---|---|
| Walk-away tests and the three eligibility checks | Always in full, whatever the size |
| Project integrity and counterparty | In proportion to value and familiarity |
| Contract | In proportion to structure: a long forward needs real legal attention, a spot trade settling in days much less |
| Evidence file | Never reduced; small deals raise audit questions too, and filing properly at the time costs almost nothing |

### Worked example (illustrative)

An Indian carrier makes two purchases in one year. These figures are illustrative.

- **Purchase one:** 30,000 tonnes forward from a developer it has never dealt with, against units not yet issued. Full eligibility checks, a site-level review of project integrity, background on the counterparty, and a lawyer on the contract, with a long-stop date and staged payment.
- **Purchase two:** a 4,000 tonne spot tranche from a project it bought from last year, under the same authorisation. Eligibility checks rerun in full (programme status and window can change), project and counterparty files updated rather than redone, a short contract, and the evidence file completed exactly as for the larger deal.

A practical habit for repeat buying: keep a standing file per project and per authorisation, and record per transaction only what is specific to it, such as serials, volume, price, transfer and cancellation.

## Short answers

::: accordion Can we rely on the broker's diligence?
No. Your authority holds you responsible. Review the broker's work, but do not substitute it for your own.
:::

::: accordion How long does diligence take?
One to three weeks per tranche for a first-time buyer, less once you have a process and know the counterparty and programme.
:::

::: accordion Is a ratings agency score enough?
Ratings address project quality, which is useful, but not CORSIA eligibility. A highly rated project without a corresponding adjustment is still ineligible.
:::

::: accordion Should we recheck approval before cancelling?
Yes, if much time has passed since purchase. It takes minutes and guards against a serious loss.
:::
