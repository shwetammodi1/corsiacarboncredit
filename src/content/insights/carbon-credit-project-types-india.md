Developers often come to us with a project that clearly cuts emissions and assume it will therefore produce credits. In India that assumption has become less safe every year, for a reason that is actually good news: clean technology keeps getting cheaper. When the low-carbon option is also the cheapest, it is hard to argue that carbon money made it happen.

Below we rate the main project types open to Indian developers, then look at each in turn.

## The scorecard

| Project type | Additionality | Measurement difficulty | Volume | Time to first revenue | Our view |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Waste methane and biogas | Strong | Moderate | Good | 18–30 months | Strong |
| Wastewater treatment | Strong | Moderate | Good | 18–30 months | Strong |
| Industrial energy efficiency | Weak to moderate | Straightforward | Variable | 18–24 months | Moderate |
| Improved cookstoves | Moderate | Hard: usage | High | 24–36 months | Moderate, closely scrutinised |
| Afforestation and reforestation | Moderate | Hard: biomass | Slow build | 4–10 years | Moderate |
| Soil carbon | Moderate | Very hard | Uncertain | 3–5 years | Difficult |
| Grid-scale solar and wind | Weak | Easy | High | Usually ineligible | Difficult |

Read down the additionality and measurement columns together and a pattern appears. **The projects easiest to measure tend to have the weakest additionality, and the most clearly additional ones tend to be hardest to measure.** Waste methane sits at the best point on that trade-off, which is why most credible new project development in India is clustered there.

## Four questions that decide every type

Before any detail, each project must get past four gates:

1. **Methodology.** Is there an approved methodology for this activity? You cannot write your own accounting rules.
2. **Additionality.** Would it have happened without carbon revenue? This is where most Indian projects now fail. A credit is effectively a subsidy, and something that wins on its own economics does not need one.
3. **Measurability.** Can the reduction be monitored continuously, in a way an auditor accepts?
4. **Volume.** Will it produce enough credits to cover fixed costs? Small activities rarely do alone; bundling them into an aggregated project is the usual answer.

## Type by type

::: accordion Waste methane and biogas: strong
**The activity.** Capturing methane from landfills, farm waste, distillery effluent or manure, then flaring it or using it for energy.

**Why it works.** Methane warms the climate many times more than CO₂ over a century, so modest capture yields substantial credits. And for the operator, venting is the normal, cheaper practice, so capture is plainly not business as usual.

**What verifiers test.** Continuous gas flow metering with calibrated meters, regular analysis of the methane fraction, capture efficiency, logged downtime, and a clear boundary between gas captured and gas escaping. Gaps in the data are the main reason issuance is delayed, and verifiers apply conservative assumptions to any period without valid readings.
:::

::: accordion Wastewater treatment: strong
**The activity.** Treating industrial or municipal effluent so that anaerobic methane is prevented or captured.

**Why it works.** The logic mirrors landfill gas. Untreated or lagoon-treated effluent gives off methane, and treatment that stops it is a real, measurable cut that seldom pays for itself.

**The difficult part.** Setting the baseline: how much methane would the untreated effluent have produced? That depends on chemical oxygen demand, temperature, retention time and the treatment set-up, all of which must be measured and defended. Over-estimated baselines are the most frequent verification finding for this type.
:::

::: accordion Industrial energy efficiency: moderate
**The activity.** Process improvements, waste heat recovery, motor and drive upgrades, cogeneration.

**Problem one: additionality.** Efficiency usually pays for itself through lower energy bills. If returns look good without carbon revenue, the project is not additional. Credible cases have long paybacks, high perceived technology risk, or capital constraints that a documented barrier analysis can show.

**Problem two: overlap with CCTS.** If the plant is a notified obligated entity under the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, its efficiency gains already count toward its intensity target. Crediting them again through the offset mechanism would be double counting. These projects therefore fit best outside the obligated sectors. See [the Indian carbon market and CCTS explained](/insights/indian-carbon-market-ccts-explained/).
:::

::: accordion Improved cookstoves: moderate, under scrutiny
**The appeal.** Large volumes, health and gender co-benefits that earn price premiums, and a natural fit with aggregation.

**The scrutiny.** Credits depend on assumptions about usage, the share of displaced wood that was non-renewable, and real fuel savings. Independent research has found systematic over-crediting in some programmes, and buyers now check these assumptions closely.

| Assumption | What goes wrong | How to hold it up |
|---|---|---|
| Usage rate | Stoves handed out, then left unused | Sensor-based usage monitoring, not surveys alone |
| Non-renewable biomass fraction | Set too high | Conservative, regionally justified values |
| Fuel saved | Lab efficiency exceeds field efficiency | Kitchen performance tests in real homes |
| Stove survival | Breakage over the crediting period | Field checks and a replacement programme |

A programme with sensor-monitored usage and cautious parameters is a good project that prices well. One built on survey claims and optimistic defaults is exactly what buyers are screening out. Our [clean cooking case study](/insights/cookstove-clean-cooking-carbon-credits-case-study/) shows how that review is done.
:::

::: accordion Afforestation and reforestation: moderate
**The appeal.** These are removals, which buyers pay more for, with visible co-benefits.

**Three hard problems:**

- **Permanence.** Fire, disease, drought or felling can release the carbon. Programmes hold back a **buffer pool** of credits as insurance and require monitoring for decades.
- **Land tenure.** Who owns the land, and who owns the carbon? With fragmented holdings, community forest rights and complicated tenure, this is often the constraint that decides an Indian project. Document it first.
- **Measurement.** Allometric equations, sample plots and, increasingly, remote sensing. Achievable with sustained field capacity.

**On timing,** trees grow slowly. Meaningful volumes arrive four to ten years after planting, and crediting runs for decades. Plan it as a long-horizon business.
:::

::: accordion Soil carbon: difficult
**The activity.** No-till, cover crops, residue management and better rotations that build soil organic carbon.

**Why it is hard.** Soil carbon varies widely within one field, changes slowly against a large existing stock, and reverses quickly if practices stop. Measurement needs sampling at scale or modelling with wide uncertainty, and verifiers discount both conservatively. Methodologies exist and the science is improving, but for now we treat it as a frontier with high measurement cost, not a dependable income line.
:::

::: accordion Grid-scale solar and wind: difficult
**Why it no longer works here.** Renewables are now the cheapest source of new generation in India. A plant viable on its own terms cannot credibly claim carbon revenue was needed. Most programmes have restricted or excluded grid-connected renewables in countries where they are commercially established, and India is one of them. Older renewable credits still trade, cheaply, because the market discounts them for this reason.

**Narrow exceptions.** Off-grid and mini-grid systems serving people with no supply, and storage or integration projects whose economics are still marginal. Both need a documented barrier analysis. Our [renewable energy due-diligence case study](/insights/renewable-energy-carbon-credits-due-diligence-case-study/) shows how buyers now screen these.
:::

## Choosing a first project

- **Build on what you know.** Operational familiarity with a sector beats a theoretically better project in one you do not know.
- **Pick strong additionality over easy measurement.** Measurement problems can be engineered around. Additionality problems cannot.
- **Check the approved activity list** for your target market, domestic CCTS or an international programme, before spending anything.
- **Read registered project documents** for your type on the [Verra](https://verra.org/) or [Gold Standard](https://www.goldstandard.org/) registries. They show what actually passed validation.
- **Design monitoring before the project.** If you cannot say how each parameter will be measured, logged and audited, you are not ready.
- **Model at cautious volume and price.** Our guide to [carbon credit prices in India](/insights/carbon-credit-price-in-india/) sets out a stress test.

## What sinks projects at verification

Data gaps, instruments nobody calibrated, assumptions nobody wrote down, and baselines set too high. Almost all of it can be prevented by designing monitoring properly at the start.

## Quick answers

**Which types work best in India?** Waste methane capture and wastewater treatment.

**Why no credits for solar?** Grid renewables are the least-cost option, so they fail additionality.

**Are cookstoves still viable?** Yes, with rigorous usage monitoring and conservative parameters.

**Can small projects earn credits?** Rarely alone. Aggregation is the normal route.
