A bank does not lend on the strength of a glossy brochure. It reads the accounts, questions the assumptions and asks what happens if things go wrong. Buying carbon credits deserves the same treatment, and most of the controversies of recent years came from buyers who skipped it and chose on price and project story.

This applies whether you are an airline settling a compliance duty or a company putting a claim in its annual report.

## What you are actually buying

Each credit says, in effect: one tonne of CO2 was reduced or removed, and it happened because of the money this credit represents. Whether that holds depends on choices made when the project was designed. What would have happened without it? How long will the carbon stay out of the air? Could anyone else claim the same tonne?

Projects answer those questions with varying rigour. That is why two credits with the same face value, and even the same price, can be worth very different amounts to the climate. Quality lives in the assumptions, not the marketing.

## The five tests

| Test | The question | What a good answer looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Additionality | Would this have happened without carbon finance? | A credible counterfactual, and a clear reason credit revenue changed the decision |
| Baseline | Is the "otherwise" scenario honest? | Conservative and well evidenced, not a worst case |
| Permanence | Will the carbon stay stored? | Buffer pools, ongoing monitoring and long crediting commitments for nature-based and removal projects |
| Double counting | Is the tonne claimed once only? | A corresponding adjustment where the claim needs one, and no other party claiming it |
| Leakage and safeguards | Did the problem just move, or cause harm? | Leakage measured; community rights and environmental safeguards respected |

A few notes on each.

**Additionality** is the test that matters most and is abused most. A wind farm that was already the cheapest way to add power, or a forest nobody planned to cut, yields credits that stand for no extra reduction. [Additionality explained](/insights/additionality-in-carbon-credits-explained/) goes further.

**Baselines** are where phantom tonnes get made. Push the "what would have happened" line upwards and the project appears to save more than it does. Avoided-deforestation projects have been at the centre of this argument.

**Permanence** matters because a tonne held in a forest that later burns has not really been removed. Engineered removals usually do better on this test, and cost more.

**Double counting** is what corresponding adjustments exist to prevent, and why CORSIA requires them for its first phase. For voluntary buyers the adjustment is often optional, which leaves the risk with you. [Eligible emissions units](/insights/corsia-eligible-emissions-units-explained/) covers the CORSIA side.

**Leakage** is the forest that is protected while the logging moves to the next valley.

## Two credits at the same price

An illustrative comparison. Both projects are hypothetical.

**Project 1: a run-of-river hydro plant in Himachal Pradesh**, registered years after construction began. Its documents show the plant was financially viable on power sales alone. Additionality is weak. The baseline is the grid, which is reasonable, and permanence is not a concern. No corresponding adjustment.

**Project 2: a mangrove restoration project on the Gujarat coast.** Documents show no commercial reason for the planting without credit revenue. The baseline assumes continued degradation, supported by satellite history. Permanence is a real risk, from storms or cutting, and is handled by a buffer pool and monitoring. Community agreements are in the file.

Price does not separate these two. The tests do. Project 1 fails the most important one. Project 2 carries a risk but manages it openly.

## Help from existing frameworks

You do not have to invent a standard.

- **The ICVCM Core Carbon Principles** set out what a high-integrity credit should look like, and are used to label qualifying programmes and methodologies. See [ICVCM Core Carbon Principles](/insights/icvcm-core-carbon-principles/).
- **CORSIA's eligibility criteria** set a similar bar for compliance use, plus the corresponding adjustment. A unit eligible for the first phase has already cleared several of the five tests, which is part of why that segment is treated as higher integrity.

## Warning signs

Stop, or dig much harder, when you see any of these:

- a baseline that assumes very high business-as-usual emissions
- old vintages at low prices with little recent monitoring
- no corresponding adjustment when the claim you plan to make implies one
- an avoided-deforestation project whose baseline is opaque or conveniently generous
- a price far below the credible range for that project type (cheapness is often the giveaway)
- a registry or programme you cannot check independently

## A purchase routine

For each real purchase, work through the same order:

1. Identify the programme and methodology.
2. Check for an integrity or eligibility label, such as CORSIA eligibility or an ICVCM label.
3. Read the project documents for the additionality argument and the baseline logic.
4. Confirm corresponding adjustment status.
5. Check the registry entry and the vintage.
6. File the evidence.

It is the same discipline a verifier applies, and it is what separates airlines that hold defensible units from those holding problems. Retiring the units then follows the [airline compliance workflow](/insights/how-airlines-use-carbon-credits-corsia/).

::: accordion Are nature-based credits lower quality?
Not by nature. They carry permanence and baseline risks that need buffers and conservative assumptions.
:::

::: accordion Do CORSIA-eligible credits pass all five tests?
They clear several by design, including double counting through the corresponding adjustment. That is why they sit in the higher-integrity segment.
:::
