A dairy cooperative in Gujarat wants to report its emissions. It burns diesel, which releases carbon dioxide. Its cattle and manure release methane. Its fertiliser use releases nitrous oxide. Three gases, measured in tonnes, and no sensible way to add them together as they stand, because a tonne of each warms the planet by a very different amount.

CO2e, short for carbon dioxide equivalent, is the unit that solves this. It appears in every carbon credit, every corporate footprint and every climate target, and it is simpler than it looks.

## One scale for many gases

CO2 is not the only greenhouse gas. Methane, nitrous oxide and several industrial gases also trap heat, and most of them do it far more strongly per tonne. Adding raw tonnes of different gases would be like adding rupees, dollars and euros without converting them.

CO2e is the conversion. It expresses the warming effect of any greenhouse gas as the quantity of CO2 that would cause the same warming. Once everything is in CO2e, emissions can be compared and totalled on a single scale.

## The conversion factor: global warming potential

The factor used is the gas's **global warming potential (GWP)**: how much it warms the atmosphere over a set period, usually 100 years, relative to CO2. CO2 is the benchmark, with a GWP of 1.

- **Carbon dioxide:** 1
- **Methane:** roughly 28 to 30
- **Nitrous oxide:** roughly 265 to 273

So a tonne of methane counts as about 28 tonnes of CO2e, and a tonne of nitrous oxide as about 265. Climate science revises GWP values from time to time, and reports may use different time horizons, which is why you will see slightly different figures in different places.

## Worked example

*Illustrative figures, using GWPs of 28 for methane and 265 for nitrous oxide.* Suppose the dairy cooperative's annual emissions are:

| Gas | Tonnes of gas | GWP | Tonnes of CO2e |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon dioxide (diesel) | 500 | 1 | 500 |
| Methane (cattle and manure) | 10 | 28 | 280 |
| Nitrous oxide (fertiliser) | 1 | 265 | 265 |
| **Total** | 511 | | **1,045** |

Only 11 of the 511 tonnes of gas are methane and nitrous oxide, yet they make up more than half the warming. A footprint counted in raw tonnes would badly understate the problem.

The same arithmetic explains why methane-capture projects, such as landfill gas or biogas, can generate large volumes of credits from fairly small quantities of gas. Each tonne of methane captured is roughly 28 tonnes of CO2e avoided.

## Why a credit is one tonne of CO2e

A standard carbon credit stands for one tonne of CO2e avoided or removed, not one tonne of whatever gas the project dealt with. That common unit is what allows a forestry credit, a methane credit and an engineered removal to be priced and traded against each other. Retiring a credit cancels one tonne of CO2e.

For the same reason, footprints and [science-based targets](/insights/science-based-targets-sbti-explained/) are stated in CO2e rather than in tonnes of individual gases. How those footprints are organised is covered in our guide to [scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions](/insights/ghg-accounting-scope-1-2-3-explained/).

## A note for aviation

CORSIA is narrower. It counts CO2 only, calculated from fuel burnt: 3.16 kg of CO2 for each kg of Jet-A or Jet-A1, and 3.10 kg for AvGas. Aviation's other warming effects, such as contrails and nitrogen oxides, are outside the scheme. So when an airline reports its CORSIA emissions, the figure is in tonnes of CO2, not CO2e, even though the credits it cancels are denominated in CO2e.

## Common slips

- **Adding gases without converting them.** The result means nothing.
- **Mixing GWP sets in one inventory.** Pick one set and one time horizon, and say which.
- **Assuming "carbon" means CO2 only.** In most carbon market usage it means CO2e.

::: accordion Why do CO2e figures sometimes differ between reports?
Because GWP values are updated periodically and reports may use different time horizons, most commonly 100 years.
:::
