A buyer is offered two forest credits in the same week. One comes from a project that protects an existing tropical forest. The other comes from new planting on degraded land. Both are described as "nature-based", both come from recognised standards, and they are priced differently. The price gap is not arbitrary. The two credits make different promises and fail in different ways.

This note is for buyers, developers and anyone trying to make sense of the press coverage of forest carbon, which is more extensive than for any other category.

![The main risks in forestry carbon projects](/images/carbon-credits/forestry-carbon-risks.svg)

## Two products under one label

| | REDD+ (avoided deforestation) | A/R (afforestation and reforestation) |
|---|---|---|
| What is credited | Forest loss that would have happened but was prevented | New forest that absorbs CO2 as it grows |
| Type of credit | Avoidance: carbon stays in trees that already exist | Removal: carbon is taken out of the atmosphere |
| Where it is most exposed | The credibility of the projected deforestation it claims to prevent | Whether trees actually grow and survive for decades |
| Permanence risk | Yes: the protected forest can still be lost | Yes: the new forest can still be lost |

REDD+ stands for reducing emissions from deforestation and forest degradation, plus conservation, sustainable management and enhancement of forest carbon stocks.

The avoidance-removal split affects price as well. Buyers backing a net zero claim pay more for removals.

## Risk one: the baseline

To credit avoided deforestation, the project must say how much forest would have been lost without it. Nobody can observe that figure. It has to be projected.

The original approach was a **project-level baseline**: take historic deforestation rates in a reference region judged comparable, and project them onto the project area. The weakness is that the choice of reference region and period largely determines the answer, and developers had room to choose generously.

Independent analyses have concluded that some projects projected far more deforestation than actually occurred, and therefore issued more credits than their work justified. This is the most serious and most persistent criticism the voluntary market has faced.

The structural response is the **jurisdictional baseline**, set for a whole state or country rather than a single project. It removes much of the developer's discretion. ART TREES works this way, and the move towards jurisdiction-wide accounting is the biggest methodological change in forest carbon. We look at one example in the [jurisdictional REDD+ case study](/insights/jurisdictional-redd-plus-carbon-credits-case-study/).

## Risk two: leakage

If a project stops logging in one valley and the loggers simply move to the next, the atmosphere gains nothing.

Project-level methodologies deal with this by subtracting a **leakage deduction**, an estimated percentage, from the credited volume. It is an estimate, and hard to check.

Jurisdictional accounting deals with it by drawing the boundary wide enough that displaced clearing is still inside it. This is the strongest case for the jurisdictional approach, and the reason a project-level REDD+ credit and a jurisdictional one should not be treated as the same product.

## Risk three: reversal, and how buffers work

Carbon stored in trees can be released by fire, drought, disease, illegal felling, a new owner or a change in government policy. Any of these can undo a reduction that was credited as permanent.

The standard protection is a **buffer pool**. Every time credits are issued, a percentage is held back in a shared reserve. If a project suffers a reversal, credits are cancelled from that reserve to make up the loss.

**Worked example (illustrative figures only).** A planting project is verified for 10,000 tonnes in a monitoring period. Its risk assessment sets a buffer contribution of 20%.

| | Tonnes |
|---|---|
| Verified in the period | 10,000 |
| Sent to the buffer pool (20%) | 2,000 |
| Issued to the developer for sale | 8,000 |

If a fire later destroys part of the stand, credits are cancelled from the pool to cover it. The buyer's 8,000 are unaffected, provided the pool is large enough.

That proviso is the issue. Three questions to ask about any buffer:

1. **How was the percentage set?** A risk-based assessment gives a figure specific to the project. A flat rate treats a fire-prone eucalyptus plantation and a protected wetland alike.
2. **When was it last reassessed?** Buffers sized on historic fire risk may be too small for today's climate-driven risk. Losses in fire-prone regions have built up faster than historic rates suggested.
3. **Is the pool shared across projects?** It usually is. That spreads risk, but a correlated event, such as a bad regional fire season, draws on the same reserve for many projects at once.

Buffer adequacy is still an open question. Treat it as a risk you assess, not one somebody else has solved.

## Risk four: land and consent

The most common serious failure in forest carbon has nothing to do with carbon accounting. Forests are used by people, and a signature from a government or a large landowner does not amount to consent from the communities who depend on the land.

Projects have been disrupted, suspended and publicly attacked when communities found a carbon project had been set up over their land without real consultation.

Before buying, look for:

- Tenure that is clear and written down, including customary rights
- Evidence, not assertions, that free, prior and informed consent was obtained
- A grievance mechanism that works and has been used
- A documented benefit-sharing arrangement that is being honoured
- Any public dispute about the project

The last check costs nothing and often tells you a great deal. Disputes are usually reported publicly well before they surface in a registry.

## Risk five: how well the carbon is measured

Monitoring has improved considerably, and the approach a project uses is a fair indicator of its quality.

- **Satellite imagery** is the core tool. Higher resolution and more frequent passes let forest cover change be detected without a site visit, and historic imagery lets anyone compare a baseline with what really happened. That is why criticism of forest carbon has been better evidenced than criticism of other categories.
- **Ground plots** calibrate the satellite data. Imagery shows canopy, not carbon; plots convert one into the other for the forest type in question.
- **Allometric equations** turn measurable tree dimensions into biomass and so into carbon. They are specific to species and region, and borrowing an equation from a different forest type introduces errors that are easy to miss.
- **LiDAR**, where it is used, measures canopy structure and biomass much more precisely than optical imagery, at higher cost.

Ask how often imagery is updated, how many plots there are and how they were placed, which equations were used and whether they fit this forest, and whether the verifier sees raw monitoring data or only a summary. A project with sparse imagery and few plots is not necessarily wrong, but it is harder to check, and harder to check should mean a lower price.

## Pre-purchase checklist

Tick each before committing:

- Buffer contribution, how it was sized and when it was last reviewed
- Leakage handled by deduction or by a jurisdictional boundary
- Baseline method: project or jurisdictional, and when it was last revised
- Tenure documents, consent evidence and grievance records
- Conservative quantification
- Frequency of remote sensing and extent of ground checks
- Methodology version: current, or an older revision

## Reading the criticism sensibly

Forest carbon is scrutinised more than any other category, and buyers need to read the coverage without either brushing it aside or panicking.

- **Is it about a methodology or about this project?** A finding that a class of baselines was inflated is a reason to check how your project set its baseline. It is not in itself a finding against your project.
- **Which methodology version?** Much of the published criticism concerns projects registered under versions that have since been tightened.
- **How did the project respond?** A detailed public response that engages with the analysis tells you something. So does silence, or a reply that only defends reputation.
- **What does independent work say?** Peer-reviewed studies and independent ratings count for more than either journalism or developer statements, especially where several agree.
- **Carbon or community?** A project can have sound accounting and a serious land dispute, or the reverse. They are different risks, and a single verdict usually blurs them.

The practical upshot: criticism should raise your discount, not automatically rule the category out. Writing off all forest carbon throws away the projects that took the criticism seriously and changed.

## For Indian developers

India has large potential for afforestation and forest management projects, but several features decide whether a project is viable.

- **Land rights are layered.** Statutory, customary and community forest rights overlap. Getting real consent is hard and cannot be skipped; it is the step most likely to sink a project later if done superficially.
- **Additionality needs care** where government programmes already fund planting. Credits for trees that would have been planted anyway will not survive review.
- **Timelines are long.** Forest projects run for decades with crediting periods to match. Early revenue is unlikely.
- **International sales need authorisation**, including for CORSIA, where forestry credits must also come from an approved programme and fall in an eligible vintage. Settle the government's position before choosing a methodology; see [corresponding adjustments and Article 6](/knowledge-base/corresponding-adjustments-article-6/).
