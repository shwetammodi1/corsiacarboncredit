Buyers who want removals rather than avoidance tend to arrive at the same shortlist, and biochar is usually on it. It lasts far longer than a forest, costs much less than direct air capture, and can be bought in volume now. This piece explains where the credit comes from and what to test before buying one.

## From crop waste to a credit

Start with a pile of biomass: crop residue after harvest, offcuts from forestry, other organic waste. Left alone, it rots or is burnt, and within a few years most of its carbon is back in the air.

Now heat the same material with very little oxygen. That process is **pyrolysis**, and the product is biochar, a stable, charcoal-like solid. The carbon in it is now in a form that breaks down extremely slowly.

A biochar credit represents that carbon: kept out of the atmosphere, in durable form, instead of being released as the biomass decayed. That makes it a **removal**, not an avoided future emission.

For Indian readers the feedstock will sound familiar. Crop residue is abundant across the country, and much of it is burnt in the field. Whether a given residue stream is a sustainable feedstock is still something the project has to show, not assume.

## How it compares with other removals

Permanence, meaning how long the carbon stays out of the air, is the first question for any removal. Here is how biochar sits between the other two options buyers usually weigh:

| | Forestry | Biochar | Direct air capture with storage |
|---|---|---|---|
| How long carbon stays stored | Decades, and can be reversed | Centuries | Millennia |
| What can undo it | Fire, felling, disease | Very little, once in soil or a durable use | Very little |
| Relative cost | Low to mid | Mid | High |
| Can the stored carbon be measured directly? | Harder, especially below ground | Yes, by lab analysis of each batch | Yes |

A forest can burn down. Biochar in the ground does not simply revert. That difference is why biochar is treated as a serious removal and why it sells at a premium to avoidance credits. For the expensive end of the table, see our piece on [direct air capture](/insights/direct-air-capture-carbon-removal/).

## The farm benefit

Biochar is unusual among removals in doing something useful after it is made. Worked into soil, it can help the soil hold water, reduce the fertiliser needed and raise yields. The same tonne of stored carbon therefore comes with an agricultural benefit, which gives the credit a stronger story for buyers who report on more than tonnes.

## Why its integrity case is comparatively strong

Two things help. First, the quantity of carbon in a batch of biochar can be measured in a laboratory, which is not true of carbon spread through a forest's roots and soil. Second, pyrolysis is a discrete industrial step with inputs and outputs that can be recorded and verified. Methodologies and registries for biochar have developed quickly, and third-party verification works as it does for any other credit.

## What still needs checking

Strong does not mean automatic. Before buying, we look at:

- **Feedstock.** Is the biomass genuinely waste, sourced sustainably? Growing something specifically to burn it would undermine the claim.
- **End use.** Where did the biochar go, and is that use durable? The long storage life depends on it.
- **Quantification.** Are the calculations conservative, with the lab results to back them?
- **Verification.** Has an independent body checked the batches the credits relate to?

Our general framework for these questions is in [how to evaluate carbon offset quality](/insights/evaluating-carbon-offset-quality-airlines/).

## Where it fits in a portfolio

For a buyer building a removal portfolio, biochar often does the bulk of the work: durable enough to count as a real removal, measurable enough to defend, and affordable enough to buy at volume. It sits between reversible, low-cost forestry and permanent, high-cost engineered removals.

## Short answers

**Are biochar credits removals?** Yes. They represent carbon taken out of the natural cycle and stored.

**How long does the carbon stay stored?** Typically centuries.

**What makes them credible?** Measurable carbon, a verifiable process and high durability, provided feedstock and quantification are sound.
