We are regularly asked to "check the ISCC CORSIA credits" in a portfolio. There are no such things. ISCC CORSIA certifies **fuel**. It says nothing about carbon credits, which are issued under entirely separate crediting standards such as Verra or Gold Standard. Getting that straight is the first step. The second is understanding that the certificate is only one link in a chain, and an operator's claim fails if any link is missing.

![Graphic introducing carbon standards and fuel certification](/images/carbon-credits/hero-carbon-standards.svg)

## What the certificate is for

CORSIA lets an operator reduce its offsetting requirement by using **CORSIA Eligible Fuels**: sustainable aviation fuel (SAF) or lower carbon aviation fuel (LCAF) that meets the scheme's sustainability criteria. A reduction can be claimed only when all three of these are true:

- The fuel meets the **CORSIA sustainability criteria**.
- It is certified by a **Sustainability Certification Scheme that ICAO has approved**.
- There is documented **chain of custody** from production to the point where it was loaded onto the aircraft.

ISCC CORSIA is one of the approved schemes. It audits and certifies that fuel meets the criteria, and it supports the chain of custody that links a certified batch to the aircraft that burnt it.

> Fuel that is genuinely sustainable but lacks approved certification and chain-of-custody records delivers a real environmental benefit and no CORSIA reduction at all. Operators find this out at verification, after they have budgeted on the lower figure.

## The criteria a fuel must meet

![Summary of CORSIA Eligible Fuels and their criteria](/images/corsia/corsia-eligible-fuels.svg)

- **A lifecycle saving of at least 10%** against the conventional jet fuel baseline, measured well-to-wake. Well-to-wake means everything: growing or collecting the feedstock, transport, conversion, distribution and combustion. That is why a fuel that burns just like fossil jet fuel can still show a large saving. The benefit lies upstream.
- **No feedstock from high-carbon-stock land converted after 1 January 2008.** This stops land rich in carbon being cleared to grow fuel crops. The date is fixed, so producers need evidence of land status going back to it.
- **Induced land use change (ILUC)** added to the lifecycle figure where relevant. ILUC captures the emissions caused when fuel feedstock pushes food production onto other land. Values differ a great deal by feedstock, which is one reason wastes and residues are favoured: they displace nothing.
- **Wider themes**: water, soil, air, conservation, waste, human and labour rights, land-use rights and food security.

## One batch, from collector to wing

Take, for illustration, a batch of used cooking oil collected from restaurants in an Indian city and turned into SAF. Here is who touches it and what each party needs.

| Link in the chain | Their role | Certified? |
|---|---|---|
| Collector or aggregator | Proves where the oil came from and that it is an eligible waste | Yes |
| Conversion plant | Certifies the production pathway and its lifecycle value | Yes |
| Trader or blender | Keeps the chain of custody intact while handling and blending | Yes |
| Airport fuel supplier | Delivers the certified volume with its documents | Yes |
| Aircraft operator | Uses the documents to make the claim | No, but must hold the evidence |

The operator does not need its own ISCC certificate. It does need to be able to prove the chain, which means collecting and keeping the documents, not assuming they exist somewhere upstream.

## Mass balance and book-and-claim

Jet fuel is mixed in pipelines, tanks and hydrant systems. The SAF molecules that entered the system are not the ones that reach your aircraft. Two accounting models deal with this.

| | Mass balance | Book-and-claim |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Physical mixing is allowed; the sustainability attribute is tracked and quantities must balance over a set period | The attribute is separated from physical delivery; a buyer claims SAF used elsewhere |
| Problem it solves | Commingled supply chains | SAF is not available at most airports |
| Position under CORSIA | The model CORSIA's requirements are built around | More restrictive treatment than in voluntary programmes |

Confirm which model applies before you contract for fuel. An operator that buys on a book-and-claim basis expecting a CORSIA reduction, and then finds the treatment does not allow it, has paid a fuel premium for no compliance benefit.

## Default or actual lifecycle values

The size of the reduction depends on the lifecycle value attached to the fuel. There are two routes to it.

| | Default value | Actual value |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Published by ICAO for a defined pathway (a feedstock and a conversion technology) | Calculated by the producer for its own operation |
| Evidence and audit effort | Lower | Considerably higher |
| Typical result | Conservative, so a smaller reduction | Usually lower emissions, so a larger reduction |
| Suits | Small volumes | Producers selling into a market that pays for the saving |

An operator should know which route was used for its fuel. It changes the size of the claim and the evidence to keep. A claim resting on an actual value depends on the producer's calculation holding up, which makes the certification file more important, not less.

## Getting certified: the producer's side

For a producer, trader or other supply-chain business, certification runs roughly like this:

1. **Set the scope**: which sites, products and pathways.
2. **Build the system**: written procedures for feedstock traceability, mass balance accounting and record-keeping.
3. **Prepare the evidence**: feedstock origin, land status, conversion data and the lifecycle calculation.
4. **On-site audit** by an ISCC-approved certification body.
5. **Close any non-conformities** the audit raises.
6. **Certificate issued**, valid for a defined period.
7. **Surveillance audits** during that period, then recertification.

Preparation usually takes longer than the audit. The most common delay is feedstock traceability: proving where material came from and that the 2008 land test is met.

## The operator's evidence file

To get a fuel claim through verification, keep:

- Certification documents from the approved scheme, for each batch
- Chain-of-custody records linking the batch to your uplift
- The lifecycle value applied and where it came from
- Fuel volumes and the aerodromes where they were loaded
- A reconciliation of purchase records to claimed quantities
- Evidence that the same fuel is not claimed under another scheme

The last item matters more each year. SAF used to meet a European or UK mandate, or claimed in a voluntary programme, may not be claimable again under CORSIA. Verifiers look for exactly this.

## Why fuel claims fail

In rough order of frequency:

1. **The chain breaks.** The certificate exists upstream but nothing links it to your specific uplift. This is usually a procurement process failure, not a supply problem.
2. **No batch-level documents.** The certificate covers a site or period; the claim needs evidence for your volumes.
3. **Double claiming.** The same fuel counted under a blending mandate and under CORSIA.
4. **Book-and-claim assumed to count.** It is treated more restrictively under CORSIA.
5. **Volumes do not reconcile** with fuel purchase records.
6. **Wrong scheme.** A valid certificate from a scheme ICAO has not approved for CORSIA supports no claim.

All six are prevented by agreeing the evidence path before signing a fuel contract. At that point it costs nothing. After uplift it cannot be fixed.

## For Indian operators

India has been developing SAF policy and blending targets, and domestic production is growing from a small base. Two practical points follow. Supply is thin and uneven across stations, so do not model a reduction on fuel you cannot actually buy where you fly; it overstates the saving and understates the credit budget. And the paperwork is substantial, so settle certification and chain-of-custody requirements with the supplier before contracting.

## Short answers

::: accordion Does ISCC CORSIA certify carbon credits?
No. It certifies fuel. Credits come from crediting standards such as Verra or Gold Standard.
:::

::: accordion Does an airline need its own ISCC certificate?
Generally not. Certification sits with the supply chain. The airline must obtain and keep the documents that support its claim.
:::

::: accordion How much does eligible fuel reduce the obligation?
By the lifecycle saving against the conventional baseline, applied to the fuel mass. A high-saving fuel can remove a large share of an obligation. The calculation is in [how SAF reduces your offsetting requirement](/knowledge-base/saf-and-corsia-offsetting-reduction/).
:::

::: accordion Can the same fuel count for CORSIA and an EU mandate?
No. Decide which scheme a batch will be claimed under before you buy it.
:::
