**What is carbon credit price history?** It is the record of how prices for carbon credits and related instruments have changed over time. There is **no single historical price**, because each market has its own history:

- **Voluntary credits** averaged about **USD 7.3 per tonne in 2008**, fell to **USD 2.51 in 2020**, peaked at **USD 7.37 in 2022** and were **USD 6.34 in 2024** (Ecosystem Marketplace).
- **Kyoto-era CERs** fell from about **USD 20 before the 2008 financial crisis** to **below €1 in late 2012**.
- **EU allowances** went from near zero in 2007 to **over €100 in February 2023**.
- **CORSIA units** have only been priced since 2024.

Prices rose and fell with **supply, demand, regulation and buyer confidence**. For aviation-specific guidance, see our [CORSIA Carbon Credits](https://corsiacarboncredit.in/) resources.

*All prices in this article are nominal, not adjusted for inflation, unless stated.*

## Understanding the History of Carbon Credit Pricing

### Early Development of Carbon Trading

The idea of putting a price on pollution through tradable permits was tested in the **United States sulphur dioxide trading programme** in the 1990s. Its success encouraged negotiators to apply market-based approaches to greenhouse gases: set a limit, then let emitters trade so that reductions happen where they are cheapest.

### Kyoto Protocol and the Beginning of International Carbon Markets

The **Kyoto Protocol**, adopted in 1997 and in force from 2005, gave developed countries binding emission targets for its **first commitment period (2008–2012)**. It created three market mechanisms:

- **Clean Development Mechanism (CDM):** projects in developing countries earned **Certified Emission Reductions (CERs)**.
- **Joint Implementation (JI):** projects in developed countries with targets earned **Emission Reduction Units (ERUs)**.
- **International Emissions Trading:** countries with targets could trade their assigned emission allowances.

These mechanisms created the first large-scale international market for project-based carbon credits. The EU let companies use CERs and ERUs, within limits, in its new emissions trading system, which linked CER prices to European demand.

### Development of the Voluntary Carbon Market

Alongside the regulated markets, a **voluntary carbon market** grew as companies and individuals bought offsets by choice. Independent standards emerged, such as **Gold Standard** (founded in 2003) and the **Verified Carbon Standard**, now run by **Verra**, each with its own registry. Buyers paid for **project-based credits** from forestry, renewable energy, cookstoves and methane projects, and corporate climate commitments became the main source of demand.

### Paris Agreement and the New Carbon Market Framework

The **Paris Agreement** (2015) replaced Kyoto's split between developed and developing countries with targets for all countries. **Article 6** sets rules for international cooperation:

- **Article 6.2:** countries can transfer **internationally transferred mitigation outcomes (ITMOs)** bilaterally, applying **corresponding adjustments** so the same reduction is not counted twice.
- **Article 6.4:** a new UN-supervised crediting mechanism succeeds the CDM.

The Article 6 rulebook was agreed at COP26 in 2021, and further details were completed at COP29 in 2024. **Article 6 is a framework between governments, not the voluntary market**, although voluntary and compliance buyers increasingly want credits with host-country authorisation.

## Historical Carbon Credit Prices: How Have Prices Changed Over Time?

**Coverage and methodology have changed over time**, so the figures below come from different sources and instruments. They should not be joined into one continuous line.

### Early years (before 2005)

Trading volumes were small, and reliable public price series are limited. **We have not found a consistent price series for this period.**

### 2005–2010: Kyoto and the first EU ETS phases

- The **EU ETS** launched in 2005. Over-allocation of allowances in Phase 1 pushed prices down to **around €1 or less during 2007**, because Phase 1 allowances could not be carried into Phase 2.
- **CER prices** were high before the financial crisis, around **USD 20** for secondary CERs, according to an academic review.
- **Voluntary credits** reached an average of about **USD 7.3 per tonne in 2008**, a peak driven partly by expectations of a US cap-and-trade system, according to Ecosystem Marketplace.
- The **2008–2009 financial crisis** cut industrial output and emissions, reducing demand for allowances and credits.

### 2011–2015: Oversupply and collapse

- **CERs collapsed.** A large surplus built up, and demand from the EU was limited. Point Carbon put the **average 2012 CER price at €2.54**, and CERs fell **below €1** by late 2012.
- From 2013, the EU **restricted new CERs**, excluding credits from industrial gas projects and limiting most new credits to projects in least developed countries.
- **EU allowances** stayed low because of a large surplus that had built up since 2009.
- **Voluntary prices fell** after countries failed to agree on a strong second Kyoto period: Ecosystem Marketplace reported a drop from **USD 4.9 in 2013 to USD 3.8 in 2014**.

### 2016–2020: Reform and low voluntary prices

- The EU agreed the **Market Stability Reserve (MSR)** in 2015, which began operating in 2019. Reforms between 2015 and 2018 helped restore confidence, and **allowance prices began recovering in 2018**.
- **CORSIA** was adopted by ICAO in 2016, but its pilot phase only began in 2021.
- **Voluntary prices stayed low**, at **USD 3.07 in 2019** and **USD 2.51 in 2020**, as abundant older renewable energy credits dominated supply.

### 2021–2023: The net-zero boom and integrity concerns

- **Corporate net-zero pledges surged**, and voluntary prices rose to **USD 4.04 in 2021** and a record **USD 7.37 in 2022**.
- **EU allowances** exceeded **€100 for the first time in February 2023**, and averaged about **€83.6 in 2023** (European Environment Agency).
- In 2023, **media and research investigations** questioned the quality of some forest credits, cutting confidence. Voluntary averages eased to **USD 6.53 in 2023**.

### 2024–2025: Quality gaps and CORSIA demand

- **Voluntary averages** were **USD 6.34 in 2024** (Ecosystem Marketplace).
- **CORSIA's first phase** began in 2024. The Platts benchmark for Phase 1 eligible units rose from **USD 11 at the start of 2024** to a record **USD 20 on 19 December 2024**.
- **EU allowances** averaged about **€64.8 in 2024** (EEA) and about **€74 in 2025** (ESMA).
- Sylvera data put the average price of voluntary credits retired at **USD 5.40 for Q1–Q3 2025**. That uses a different method from Ecosystem Marketplace.

### 2026: Latest available data

- **Voluntary:** USD 6.92 per tonne in Q3 2026, and USD 6.12 for Q1–Q3 2026 (Sylvera). The **gap between higher- and lower-rated credits** widened to USD 4.19.
- **CORSIA:** USD 19.00 on 28 January (Fastmarkets), a record low of USD 9.45 on 1 July (Platts), then USD 13.00 on 18 November (Fastmarkets).
- **EU ETS:** around €90 in mid-November 2026.

## Historical Carbon Credit Price Trends by Market Type

### Voluntary Carbon Market Price History

Voluntary prices have been shaped by **corporate demand and buyer confidence**:

- **2008 peak:** expectations of compliance demand in the US.
- **2011–2020 decline:** oversupply, especially from older renewable energy and industrial projects, and weak policy signals.
- **2021–2022 boom:** net-zero pledges and interest in nature-based credits.
- **2023–2024 correction:** integrity concerns, especially around some REDD+ credits.
- **2025–2026:** a **two-tier market**, with higher-rated credits and **carbon removals** commanding more while lower-rated credits stay cheap.

### EU Emissions Trading System Price History

The **EU ETS** is a cap-and-trade system for power, industry and aviation within Europe. It has run in phases:

- **Phase 1 (2005–2007):** a pilot phase with over-allocation, and prices fell to near zero.
- **Phase 2 (2008–2012):** coincided with the Kyoto period. The financial crisis created a large surplus.
- **Phase 3 (2013–2020):** more auctioning and the MSR. Prices recovered from 2018.
- **Phase 4 (2021–2030):** tighter caps under the EU's 2030 climate targets. Record prices above €100 in 2023.

**EU Allowances (EUAs) are compliance permits, not voluntary carbon credits.** A voluntary credit cannot be surrendered in the EU ETS, and EUA prices should not be used to value voluntary credits.

### Kyoto-Era CER and ERU Price History

CERs and ERUs rose with demand from the EU ETS and Kyoto countries before 2008, then collapsed as:

- **supply surged**, as thousands of CDM projects issued credits
- **demand was capped** by EU limits on offset use
- **regulatory uncertainty** grew over Kyoto's second period
- the EU **excluded industrial gas credits** from 2013

Today's voluntary credits are **not directly comparable** with Kyoto-era CERs. They follow different rules, have different buyers and were traded in a different policy environment.

### CORSIA Carbon Credit Price History

**CORSIA**, the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation, is **ICAO's** global market-based measure for international aviation. It has three implementation periods:

- **Pilot phase (2021–2023)**
- **First phase (2024–2026)**
- **Second phase (2027–2035)**

**Eligibility rules limit supply.** First-phase units must come from approved programmes and meet unit-level conditions, including host-country authorisation with a corresponding adjustment and CORSIA labelling. **Airline demand** is tied to compliance deadlines.

The CORSIA price history is **short**, and comes from **price reporting agency assessments**, not completed transactions:

| Date | Assessment (USD per tCO₂e) | Agency |
|---|---|---|
| Start of 2024 | 11.00 | S&P Global Platts |
| 19 Dec 2024 | 20.00 (record at the time) | S&P Global Platts |
| 28 Jan 2026 | 19.00 | Fastmarkets |
| 1 Jul 2026 | 9.45 (Phase 1 record low) | S&P Global Platts |
| 7 Oct 2026 | 12.75 | S&P Global Platts |
| 18 Nov 2026 | 13.00 | Fastmarkets |

*Different agencies use different methodologies.* **We did not find a reliable public series for 2025.** ICAO's own modelling for the 2025 periodic review estimated that average first-phase prices could range from **USD 5.70 to 17.20 per tCO₂**. ### India's Carbon Market Price History

India's carbon market history involves **several different instruments**:

- **CDM credits:** India was one of the largest host countries for CDM projects, and Indian CERs traded at international prices.
- **Energy Saving Certificates (ESCerts):** under the **Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT)** scheme, launched in 2012, industries that beat energy-efficiency targets earn ESCerts. Trading began on the **Indian Energy Exchange on 26 September 2017**, with CERC as regulator, BEE as administrator and POSOCO as registry. **ESCerts measure energy saved, not tonnes of CO₂.**
- **Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs):** represent renewable electricity generated, traded on power exchanges since 2011. **RECs are not carbon credits.**
- **Carbon Credit Certificates (CCCs):** created under the **Carbon Credit Trading Scheme**, notified in June 2023, with CERC trading regulations notified in February 2026.

**As of November 2026, no official CCC price history exists**, because trading had not started at scale. Our guide to [carbon market policy in India](/blog/carbon-market-policy-in-india/) explains how the CCTS replaces and builds on PAT.

## Major Events That Influenced Historical Carbon Credit Prices

1. **Kyoto Protocol implementation (2005):** created compliance demand for CERs and ERUs.
2. **Growth of the CDM:** thousands of projects brought large supply.
3. **Global financial crisis (2008–2009):** lower output cut demand for allowances and credits.
4. **Kyoto-era oversupply:** CER prices collapsed by 2012.
5. **EU ETS policy changes:** limits on international credits from 2013, and the MSR from 2019.
6. **Paris Agreement (2015):** new framework, but uncertainty over how old credits would be treated.
7. **Corporate net-zero commitments (2019–2022):** drove voluntary prices to a 2022 record.
8. **Integrity concerns (2023):** cut demand for some credit types.
9. **Growth in demand for removals:** created a premium segment.
10. **CORSIA's first phase (2024):** created a separate, supply-constrained market.
11. **National carbon markets:** such as India's CCTS and Singapore's international credit framework.
12. **Recent integrity initiatives:** ICVCM's Core Carbon Principles labels and ratings.

These events **coincided with** price movements. Several factors usually acted together, so single causes should not be assumed.

## Why Have Carbon Credit Prices Increased or Decreased Historically?

- **Supply:** large CDM issuance in 2008–2012 pushed CER prices down. Limited CORSIA-labelled supply supported prices in 2024.
- **Buyer demand:** net-zero pledges lifted voluntary prices in 2021–2022.
- **Regulatory requirements:** EU limits on CERs cut demand. CORSIA created new demand.
- **Project quality and additionality:** doubts about some forest credits lowered their prices in 2023.
- **Certification standards:** new labels and ratings split the market into tiers.
- **Vintage:** older vintages, often pre-2016, generally sell for less.
- **Market liquidity:** thin markets, such as early CORSIA trading, can swing sharply.
- **Removal technology costs:** high costs keep durable removal prices high.
- **Integrity and confidence:** headlines affect demand quickly.
- **Government policies and climate agreements:** Kyoto, Paris and Article 6 changed who must buy and what they can use.
- **Aviation-sector demand:** airline procurement drives CORSIA price moves.

**Example:** In 2012, a CER from an industrial gas project faced both oversupply and an upcoming EU ban, so buyers had little reason to pay. In 2024, a CORSIA-labelled unit faced scarce supply and compliance deadlines, so its price rose even while average voluntary prices were flat.

## Historical Price Differences Between Carbon Reduction and Carbon Removal Credits

**Avoidance and reduction credits** include:

- renewable energy
- methane capture
- energy efficiency
- waste management
- forest protection (REDD+)

**Removal credits** include:

- afforestation and reforestation
- soil carbon
- biochar
- direct air capture
- other durable removal technologies

Historically, avoidance credits made up most supply and set the market average. Removals were a small segment, but are **priced much higher**: Ecosystem Marketplace reported **USD 19.50 on average for all removals** and **over USD 160 for biochar** in 2024. That compares with **USD 2.67 for renewable energy** credits the same year.

The difference reflects:

- **permanence and durability**, meaning how long carbon stays stored
- **higher costs** of delivering removals
- **monitoring and verification** requirements
- **buyer preferences**, especially for net-zero claims that favour removals

## How to Read and Understand a Carbon Credit Price History Chart

- **X-axis:** time, such as years or days.
- **Y-axis:** price, usually per tCO₂e.
- **Currency:** USD for voluntary credits and CORSIA units, EUR for EU allowances.
- **Annual average versus daily price:** averages smooth out swings, while daily prices show volatility.
- **Market indices:** benchmarks that track a defined basket of credits.
- **Volatility:** sharp moves often reflect thin markets or policy news.
- **Peaks and declines:** link them to dated events, with care.
- **Comparisons:** only compare like with like.
- **Nominal versus inflation-adjusted:** a USD 7 price in 2008 was worth more than USD 7 today.
- **Data gaps:** missing years should be left blank, not filled in.

**Use separate charts** for voluntary credits, EU allowances, Kyoto-era CERs, CORSIA assessments and Indian instruments. Our [carbon credit price chart](/blog/carbon-credit-price-chart/) shows voluntary averages and 2026 prices by category.

**Suggested visuals for this topic:**

- **Timeline:** key milestones, from the 1997 Kyoto Protocol to the 2027 start of CORSIA's second phase.
- **Voluntary price chart:** Ecosystem Marketplace annual averages, 2019–2024, as in the cover image.
- **Comparison graphic:** voluntary credits, EU allowances and CORSIA units shown side by side as separate instruments, with their units and sources.

## Historical Carbon Credit Prices vs Current Carbon Credit Prices

- **Market structure:** Kyoto-era markets were compliance-led. Today, voluntary, compliance, CORSIA and Article 6 markets coexist.
- **Buyer expectations:** buyers now expect ratings, transparency and recent vintages.
- **Quality requirements:** ICVCM labels and programme reforms exclude some older methodologies.
- **Removals:** now a distinct, higher-priced segment.
- **Methodologies:** newer methodologies use more conservative baselines.
- **Regulation:** CORSIA and national schemes now shape demand.

The Q3 2026 voluntary average of **USD 6.92** is close to the 2008 peak of about **USD 7.3** in nominal terms, and lower after inflation. The market now has a much wider range between low- and high-quality credits.

## What Does Carbon Credit Price History Tell Us About the Future?

**Historical evidence:** prices have been driven by policy and confidence, and surpluses have caused sharp falls.

**Current developments:** demand is rising for high-integrity and removal credits. CORSIA's second phase starts in 2027. Article 6 and national markets, including India's, are developing.

**Forecasts and possibilities:**

- possible growth in voluntary demand if corporate commitments hold
- supply and demand imbalances in CORSIA, where IATA estimates first-phase demand of 200–250 million units
- more transparency and verification, which could widen quality price gaps
- regulatory changes that could raise or lower demand

**Past price trends do not guarantee future prices or investment returns.**

## How Can Businesses and Investors Use Historical Carbon Credit Price Data?

- **Understand volatility:** CER and CORSIA histories show how fast prices can move.
- **Compare project categories:** removals versus avoidance.
- **Evaluate procurement strategies:** spot buying versus long-term offtakes.
- **Assess historical demand:** which credit types buyers favoured.
- **Understand price risks:** regulatory changes can make some credits much less valuable.
- **Compare instruments:** keep voluntary, compliance and CORSIA markets separate.
- **Plan budgets:** use ranges, not a single price.
- **Evaluate long-term climate strategies.**

Historical performance does **not** guarantee future returns. Our guide to [carbon trading and carbon credits](/blog/what-is-carbon-trading-and-carbon-credit/) explains how these markets work.

## Common Mistakes When Analysing Carbon Credit Price History

- **Comparing voluntary credits with compliance allowances**
- **Treating every carbon credit as the same product**
- **Ignoring credit vintage**
- **Using outdated prices**
- **Comparing currencies without conversion**, such as EUR allowances with USD credits
- **Confusing market assessments with completed transactions**
- **Ignoring inflation**
- **Combining incompatible datasets** into one line
- **Assuming past increases will continue**
- **Treating historical prices as guaranteed future values**

## Conclusion

Carbon credit prices have moved through clear phases: the **Kyoto-era boom and collapse**, **low voluntary prices** through the 2010s, a **net-zero boom in 2021–2022**, an **integrity correction**, and today's **two-tier market** with separate CORSIA demand.

Prices changed because **supply, demand, regulation, project quality and buyer confidence** changed. Voluntary credits, compliance allowances, Kyoto-era CERs and CORSIA units all have **separate price histories** that should not be mixed.

Historical data is valuable, but only when it is **dated, sourced and compared like with like**. Buyers and sellers should rely on credible, current market information.
