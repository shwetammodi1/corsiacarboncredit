Buyers often choose credits by the logo on the certificate. That is a reasonable starting point and a poor finishing one. The standard behind a credit decides the rules it was made under, but most of what separates a good credit from a weak one sits a level below, in the methodology and the project itself. This guide explains what standards do, how the main ones differ and where their approval stops.

![Illustration of carbon crediting standards](/images/carbon-credits/hero-carbon-standards.svg)

## Following one project through a standard

The easiest way to see what a standard does is to follow a project from idea to retired credit.

1. **A methodology must exist.** The standard publishes methodologies: the rulebooks for how a given project type sets its baseline, shows additionality and calculates reductions. If no methodology covers the activity, no credits can be issued, however worthy the project.
2. **The project is registered.** Its design is validated by an independent body and it enters the standard's public record: what it is, where, and under which methodology.
3. **The project operates and monitors.** It collects the data the methodology requires over a monitoring period.
4. **An independent body verifies the results.** The standard does not do this itself. It accredits and supervises third-party verifiers and sets the rules they work to.
5. **Credits are issued into the registry.** Each unit gets a serial number. The registry records every later transfer and, finally, retirement or cancellation.
6. **Throughout, the standard governs.** It revises methodologies, retires ones that no longer hold up, and handles disputes.

Step 4 explains a common misunderstanding. A "Verra certified" credit means a project was registered under a Verra methodology and checked by an accredited third party. It does not mean Verra staff inspected the site.

## The main standards side by side

![Comparison graphic of the main carbon crediting standards](/images/carbon-credits/carbon-standards-compared.svg)

| Standard | Unit name | Known for | Keep in mind |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Verra](https://verra.org/) (VCS) | VCU | Most volume issued, widest methodology range | Scale draws scrutiny; several methodologies revised |
| [Gold Standard](https://www.goldstandard.org/) | VER | Community and SDG-linked outcomes | Smaller volumes, higher prices |
| American Carbon Registry | ERT | North American industrial and land-use projects | Regional methodology focus |
| Climate Action Reserve | CRT | North American protocols, strong governance | Narrow geography |
| Global Carbon Council | ACC | Gulf origin, broad scope | Newer, shorter track record |
| ART TREES | TREES credit | Forest carbon at jurisdictional scale | Credits a whole jurisdiction, not a project |
| Article 6.4 mechanism | A6.4ER | The UN mechanism under the Paris Agreement | Rules still settling |

A few notes on character:

- **Verra's** size cuts both ways. It has the deepest liquidity and widest coverage, and it has drawn most of the public criticism of the voluntary market, especially over avoided-deforestation baselines.
- **Gold Standard's** focus on community benefit supports higher voluntary prices. A compliance buyer, who needs to discharge an obligation rather than tell a story, gets less for that premium.
- **ACR and CAR** grew up in the North American regulatory setting and both have good governance reputations.
- **Global Carbon Council** is younger, and its Gulf origin gives it regional relevance the older standards lack.
- **ART TREES** credits a whole state or country. That structure deals with leakage, which project-level forest crediting finds hard.
- **Article 6.4** succeeds the Clean Development Mechanism. Being new, it offers both opportunity and uncertainty. Our [Article 6 guide](/insights/article-6-paris-agreement-explained/) explains where it fits.

## Methodologies decide more than standards do

Two projects under the same standard but different methodologies can face completely different requirements. A methodology fixes:

- **applicability conditions**: which projects may use it at all
- **the baseline approach**: how the counterfactual is built
- **the additionality test**: what must be shown and how
- **quantification equations**: exactly how reductions are calculated
- **default values**: used where measuring is impractical
- **monitoring requirements**: what, how often, how precisely
- **leakage treatment**: how displaced emissions are deducted
- **uncertainty deductions**: how conservatism is applied

Three checks follow. **Version**: standards revise methodologies, sometimes heavily, and a project registered under an early version may rest on assumptions since tightened; the registry record says which version applies. **Review status**: a methodology out for consultation may be revised or retired, a planning risk for developers and a prompt for buyers to look harder. **Applicability**: projects have failed validation on a condition nobody read carefully, such as a geographic limit, a technology age limit or a size threshold.

So when someone says "it's a Verra credit", ask which methodology and which version. The standard names the institution. The methodology gives the rules.

## ICAO approval: what it covers

For CORSIA, the ICAO Council approves **programmes**, on its Technical Advisory Body's recommendation, against the Emissions Unit Criteria. Three points are regularly misread:

- **The programme is approved, not each credit.** An approved programme issues huge volumes, and only a fraction is CORSIA eligible. Vintage and corresponding adjustment still apply unit by unit.
- **Approval can be full, conditional or lapsed.** Conditions often concern how a programme handles corresponding adjustments and labels authorised units, and a conditional approval can exclude particular methodologies.
- **The list moves.** Any list in an article, including the table above, is a snapshot. Check the [ICAO emissions units page](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/CORSIA-Emissions-Units.aspx) at the time of each transaction and keep a copy of what it said.

### What the Technical Advisory Body looks at

Its assessment covers governance and independence from commercial pressure; how methodologies are developed and retired; additionality; conservative quantification; permanence and buffer design; oversight of verifiers; registry controls and transparency; **safeguards against double counting**; and social and environmental safeguards.

Double counting has produced most of the conditions attached to approvals. Programmes designed before Article 6 existed have had a lot of retrofitting to do, and not all have satisfied ICAO. More on this in [ICAO-approved crediting programmes](/knowledge-base/corsia-approved-crediting-programmes/).

## Choosing a standard as a developer

Beyond whether a programme is approved today, weigh five things:

- **Methodology fit and stability.** Is there a methodology for your activity, and is it under review? One retired mid-project is a serious problem.
- **How settled the approval is.** How long has the programme held ICAO approval, is it full or conditional, and when is it due for reassessment?
- **Fit with your host State's process.** For an Indian project, does the programme's handling of corresponding adjustments line up with how India authorises transfers? Mismatches cause delay.
- **What buyers already use.** Buyers with existing accounts and familiar processes are easier to sell to. That is a real commercial factor.
- **Fees and timelines.** Registration, issuance levies, validation and verification requirements, and realistic queue times.

Using whichever standard you used last time is habit, not a decision. Moving a project between standards later is sometimes possible, but slow and costly.

## Reading a standard as a buyer

- **Registration is not verification.** A registered project has had its design validated. Credits only exist after a monitoring period has been verified. Check which stage you are looking at.
- **Look for corrective actions.** Standards publish disputes, suspensions and corrective actions. A history does not disqualify a project, but you should understand it.
- **Trust the registry, not the brochure.** Serial numbers, status and retirements are all in the registry. Serialisation is what stops a unit being used twice; each serial encodes programme, project, vintage and batch position. A compliance file that lists volumes without serials cannot be reconciled, and reconciliation is the first thing an auditor tries.
- **Look before you call.** Most registry records are public, so you can check a project before speaking to the seller. Our [registry comparison](/insights/carbon-credit-registries-compared/) walks through a lookup.

## A structural tension

Standards are funded largely by fees from the projects they register. That sets rigour against volume, and it is the best argument for the outside scrutiny the market has received. The counterweights are real: reputation, more demanding buyers, rating agencies and regulatory attention, including ICAO's own assessment. Several standards have narrowed or retired methodologies under that pressure, which suggests the counterweights work.

The practical lesson is not to distrust standards. It is to treat a standard's approval as a **floor**, and to do your own diligence above it, as set out in [how to assess carbon credit quality](/insights/carbon-credit-quality-assessment/).

## Short answers

**Is a Verra credit automatically CORSIA eligible?** No. Programme approval is necessary but not enough.

**Who verifies a project?** An accredited independent validation and verification body. The body that validated a design generally cannot verify its performance.

**What happens when a methodology is retired?** Registered projects usually continue under transitional rules, but new registrations stop.

**Does ISCC issue carbon credits?** No. ISCC certifies fuels and materials. See [ISCC CORSIA certification](/insights/iscc-corsia-certification-guide/).
