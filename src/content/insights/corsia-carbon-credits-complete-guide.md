A trader in Mumbai emails us a spreadsheet of 40,000 credits from a wind project and asks one question: can an airline use these? Answering it properly takes about a dozen checks, and most of them have nothing to do with the wind farm itself. They concern the registry, the vintage, the programme, and above all a decision the host government may or may not have taken.

That gap, between a credit that looks fine and a credit that is fit for a particular purpose, is what this reference is about. It is long because the questions buyers, sellers and reporting teams actually face are spread across the whole chain, from how a credit is created to what you are allowed to write in an annual report once it is gone.

![Illustration introducing carbon credits and the CORSIA scheme](/images/carbon-credits/hero-carbon-credits-guide.svg)

## The whole picture in eight lines

- A carbon credit stands for one tonne of CO2 equivalent kept out of, or taken out of, the atmosphere compared with a baseline.
- It is created by a project, checked by independent bodies and issued as a serialised entry in a registry.
- The voluntary market lets buyers choose their own quality bar. Compliance markets set it for them.
- CORSIA is ICAO's compliance scheme for international aviation, and it accepts only a narrow slice of the world's credits.
- The narrowing comes mostly from one requirement: the host country must make a corresponding adjustment.
- Quality is found in documents, not in labels or ratings.
- Price follows eligibility first and project type second.
- Credits are reported beside your emissions, never subtracted from them.

The rest of this page takes each line in turn.

## A credit is a documented claim, not an object

Strip away the market language and a credit is a registry entry backed by a file of evidence. The evidence argues that one tonne of CO2 equivalent did not reach the atmosphere, or was pulled out of it, because a project existed.

"CO2 equivalent" is doing real work there. Methane, nitrous oxide and industrial gases are converted to a CO2 figure using their global warming potentials. That conversion explains why a landfill gas or rice-methane project can produce a large number of credits from a physically modest change: each tonne of methane counts for many tonnes of CO2e.

The second distinction is between two kinds of credit:

| Kind | What it certifies | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| Avoidance | An emission that would have happened did not | Renewable power, cookstoves, methane capture |
| Removal | CO2 already in the air was taken out and stored | Tree planting, biochar, direct air capture |

Buyers who have to defend a public claim increasingly separate the two and pay differently for them.

The third element is the baseline: the estimate of what would have happened without the project. Nobody can observe it, so it is modelled, and a generous model produces more credits than the project deserves. Nearly every argument about credit integrity comes back to the baseline.

> Treat every credit as a claim about something that did not happen, supported by paperwork. How much it is worth depends on how well that paperwork holds up.

## From project idea to serial number

![Diagram of a carbon credit's life from project design to retirement](/images/carbon-credits/carbon-credit-lifecycle.svg)

Every major standard follows roughly the same path. What changes is the rulebook, not the order of steps.

| Stage | Who does the work | What a buyer can later inspect |
|---|---|---|
| Choose an activity and a methodology | Developer | The methodology name and version |
| Write the project design document | Developer, often with a consultant | Baseline, additionality case, monitoring plan, safeguards |
| Validation | Accredited third-party body | The validation report |
| Registration | Crediting programme | The public registry listing |
| Monitoring | Developer | Monitoring reports for each period |
| Verification | Accredited third-party body | The verification report and any qualifications |
| Issuance | Registry | Serial numbers and vintage |
| Transfer | Seller to buyer, directly or via a broker | The chain of accounts the units passed through |
| Retirement or cancellation | Final holder | The retirement record and its stated purpose |

Two points from this table catch people out.

First, if no approved methodology covers an activity, it cannot earn credits, however much carbon it saves. Developers sometimes discover this late.

Second, owning credits proves nothing. The claim is only made when the credits are retired or cancelled, and for CORSIA the cancellation must name CORSIA as its purpose.

Plan on eighteen months to three years from concept to the first issuance. Where the host government has to authorise the credits for international use, add time.

## Two markets, one project

![Chart comparing voluntary and compliance carbon markets](/images/carbon-credits/vcm-vs-compliance-markets.svg)

A single project can sell into both markets, and its units can be worth quite different amounts depending on which door they go through.

- **Voluntary market.** Companies buy because they choose to, usually to support a climate claim. They decide what quality to accept. If they buy badly, the cost is to their reputation.
- **Compliance markets.** Regulated entities buy because the law requires it. The regulator decides what counts. If they fail to comply, the consequence is a legal penalty. The EU ETS, CORSIA and India's CCTS are all compliance systems, though they work in different ways: some trade allowances from a capped pool, CORSIA uses project credits.

The asymmetry matters. A credit acceptable to the strictest compliance scheme will almost always be acceptable to a voluntary buyer. The opposite is seldom true. Compliance eligibility is a small subset of voluntary acceptability, and that is why the same project's output can split into a cheaper voluntary tier and a dearer compliance tier. Our [comparison of voluntary and compliance markets](/insights/voluntary-vs-compliance-carbon-markets/) goes further into the mechanics.

## Where CORSIA sits

CORSIA is the scheme ICAO created so that international aviation pays for growth in its CO2 emissions above a 2019 reference level. It was the first market-based climate measure applied to one industry worldwide. The authoritative source is ICAO's own [CORSIA site](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/default.aspx).

### The scope tests

Run the tests in order. Failing any one takes the flight or the operator out.

1. Is the flight international? Domestic flying is outside the scheme altogether.
2. Is the aircraft's maximum certificated take-off mass above 5,700 kg?
3. Does the operator emit more than 10,000 tonnes of CO2 a year from international flights?
4. Is the flight something other than humanitarian, medical, firefighting or a State aircraft flight?
5. For offsetting, do **both** States on the route take part in the scheme?

An operator can pass the first four and still owe nothing on a route because one end of it sits in a non-participating State. It will still have to monitor and report.

### The timeline

| Period | Years | State participation | How the obligation is calculated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reference year | 2019 | n/a | Sets the emissions level growth is measured against |
| Pilot phase | 2021–2023 | Voluntary | Sector-wide growth factor only |
| First phase | 2024–2026 | Voluntary | Sector-wide growth factor only |
| Second phase | 2027–2035 | Mandatory for most States | Sector-wide at first, then blended with the operator's own growth |

Two dates change the arithmetic. From 2027, States above defined aviation-activity thresholds must take part, which brings many more routes into offsetting. From 2030, an operator's individual growth counts for 15% of the calculation, and from 2033 for 30%.

The reference level itself has moved once. It was first set as the average of 2019 and 2020, but the collapse in 2020 traffic would have produced an unrealistically low figure, so the ICAO Council switched to 2019 alone. The parameters are decided by the Council and can change.

## The eligibility filter

This is where optimistic sellers and hurried buyers go wrong. A reputable standard, a good project and a recent verification do not, between them, make a unit usable for CORSIA.

Eligibility is tested twice.

- **At programme level.** ICAO's Technical Advisory Body assesses crediting programmes against the Emissions Unit Criteria, and the ICAO Council takes the decision. Approval can be full or conditional, and it can lapse.
- **At unit level.** A unit from an approved programme must still meet the criteria on its own.

Here are the criteria written as the questions a buyer should put to the seller:

| Question | The criterion behind it |
|---|---|
| Would the project have gone ahead without credit income? | Additionality |
| Was the tonnage worked out conservatively under an approved methodology? | Real and measurable |
| Could the carbon be released again, and is there a buffer if it is? | Permanence |
| Has an accredited body checked the figures? | Verification |
| Has the host government made a corresponding adjustment for these units? | No double counting |
| Does the vintage fall inside the window for my compliance period? | Eligible vintage |
| Has the project broken local law or caused social or environmental harm? | No net harm |
| Can each unit be traced by serial number in a registry? | Traceability |

The fifth question removes more supply than all the others together.

Under Article 6 of the Paris Agreement, a government that authorises a credit for international use has to make a corresponding adjustment: it adds the tonnes back to its own national emissions account, so it cannot also count them towards its own target. Many governments will not do that, because it makes their own pledge harder to meet. Many more have no designated authority or procedure, which in practice amounts to a refusal. The detail is in our note on [corresponding adjustments and Article 6](/knowledge-base/corresponding-adjustments-article-6/).

> The result: a large share of the world's credits, including many from sound projects, cannot be used for CORSIA. That is a limit set by national accounting, not a verdict on the projects. It is also why eligible units are scarce and sell at a premium.

Be wary of three labels you will see in offers: "CORSIA-ready", "CORSIA-aligned" and "eligible pending authorisation". None of them is a recognised standard, and all three describe a unit that is not eligible today.

## Who is competing for eligible units

Three groups want the same limited pool.

- **Aircraft operators with an offsetting obligation.** Their demand is fixed by law, and they have deadlines.
- **Corporate buyers with no aviation exposure.** A corresponding-adjusted unit is the strongest answer to the charge that a corporate claim double counts a host country's reductions, so some companies will pay the premium voluntarily.
- **Traders and intermediaries**, building inventory in anticipation of demand.

Supply forecasts often leave out the second group. An operator planning its purchases should assume it is bidding against corporates as well as other airlines.

## Judging quality: an offer, reviewed

![Checklist of the features that separate strong and weak carbon credits](/images/carbon-credits/carbon-credit-quality-checklist.svg)

Quality is not a number you can look up. It is the sum of answers to specific questions, and the answers sit in project documents. Here is how we would work through a typical offer. The project is invented for illustration.

**The offer.** An improved cookstove programme in central India, verified credits from a recent vintage, issued under a major voluntary standard, sold with a note that it is "CORSIA-aligned".

1. **Additionality.** Would households have bought these stoves anyway? We look for evidence that the stoves were distributed below cost because of carbon revenue, and that no government scheme was already supplying them. If either fails, the credits are weak.
2. **Baseline and quantification.** Cookstove methodologies depend on assumed fuel use and on how much of the wood burnt is non-renewable. Both have been contested. We check the values used against the methodology's defaults and any field surveys.
3. **Usage evidence.** Are stoves still being used after distribution? Sample surveys, and increasingly sensor data, answer this.
4. **Permanence.** Low reversal risk for an avoidance project of this kind, so less of a concern than for forestry.
5. **Verification.** We read the verification report for qualifications, not just the conclusion.
6. **Safeguards.** Any complaints, disputes or grievance records?
7. **Double counting.** Is there a Government of India authorisation and a corresponding adjustment for these specific units? In this offer there is not, so "CORSIA-aligned" means "not eligible".
8. **Traceability.** Can we see the serial range in the public registry, or only in the seller's own spreadsheet?

The verdict could be "acceptable for a carefully worded voluntary claim, unusable for CORSIA". That is a common outcome.

Ratings agencies help with triage, and we use them. They do not replace reading the documents. And a high rating says nothing about CORSIA eligibility: a top-rated project with no corresponding adjustment is still out. Our [quality assessment method](/insights/carbon-credit-quality-assessment/) sets out the full sequence.

One recurring confusion: ISCC is not a carbon credit programme. ISCC CORSIA certifies fuel, specifically sustainable aviation fuel that qualifies as a CORSIA Eligible Fuel and so reduces an operator's offsetting requirement. It never issues credits. ## Price: what actually moves it

![Diagram of the factors that set a carbon credit's price](/images/carbon-credits/carbon-price-drivers.svg)

A published "average carbon price" mixes products that have little in common, across a range wider than tenfold. An old grid-renewable credit with doubtful additionality and a verified engineered removal share a name and nothing else.

The drivers, from strongest to weakest:

1. **Corresponding adjustment.** The biggest single factor. Two units from one project, identical except that one carries an adjustment, trade at clearly different prices. The buyer is paying for a government's concession and for scarcity, not for a better tonne.
2. **Project type.** Engineered removals at the top, then nature-based removals, then methane avoidance, then cookstoves, with grid-connected renewables at the bottom.
3. **Vintage.** Units close to the edge of an eligibility window sell at a discount. Units well inside a window that buyers need sell at a premium.
4. **Removal or avoidance.** Removals attract more from buyers with claims to protect.
5. **Co-benefits.** They count in the voluntary market. A compliance buyer who simply needs to discharge an obligation cares much less.
6. **Volume and contract form.** Large orders get some discount, limited by thin liquidity. Forward purchases are cheaper than spot because the buyer carries the risk of non-delivery or failed authorisation.

For budgeting, collect real quotes for the vintage, type and volume you would actually buy. An index figure will not survive questioning from your finance committee. Gathering quotes takes effort, and that effort is what makes the number defensible.

## Buying in seven moves

![Checklist for a disciplined carbon credit purchase](/images/carbon-credits/carbon-buying-checklist.svg)

1. **Decide what the credits are for.** A compliance obligation and a voluntary claim have different eligibility rules and different prices. Getting this wrong costs money.
2. **Write down your quality bar before you see any offers.** If you don't, whatever is available becomes your standard.
3. **Screen out what cannot prove eligibility.** Rejecting offers whose only credential is "CORSIA-ready" clears a good part of the pile.
4. **Test evidence, not promises.** For CORSIA that means the programme's current approval status, the vintage against your compliance period, a host-government authorisation that names these units, proof of the corresponding adjustment, the verification report with any qualifications, and a clean registry trail.
5. **Contract for the risks.** More important than price: a warranty of eligibility with a real remedy, who carries authorisation risk, protection on vintage, delivery dates that fit your deadline, volume tolerance, and an obligation to hand over documents. If the contract is silent on any of these, you carry that risk.
6. **Take delivery and cancel.** For CORSIA the cancellation must be marked for CORSIA. It cannot be undone.
7. **Keep the record.** Serial numbers, evidence files, and the exact claim you then make.

The detailed buyer's walkthrough is in [how to buy carbon credits](/insights/how-to-buy-carbon-credits-guide/). Indian operators buying for compliance should also read [the CORSIA unit procurement process](/insights/corsia-unit-procurement-process-india/).

## Claims and disclosure

![The GHG Protocol's three scopes and where credits are reported](/images/carbon-credits/ghg-scopes-explained.svg)

Most self-inflicted damage happens here, after the purchase.

The rule under the GHG Protocol is simple: report scope 1, 2 and 3 emissions as they are, and show credits bought and retired as a separate line. Never deduct them from a scope. A company that publishes "net scope 1 after offsets" has misstated its inventory. The scopes themselves are explained in [GHG accounting: scope 1, 2 and 3](/insights/ghg-accounting-scope-1-2-3-explained/).

How the main frameworks treat credits:

| Framework | Position on credits |
|---|---|
| GHG Protocol | Reported outside the scopes; never netted |
| SBTi | Cannot stand in for the reductions a target requires |
| ISSB / IFRS S2 | Expects disclosure of how credits are used, their type and quality |
| CDP | Credits purchased and retired disclosed separately |
| India's BRSR | Disclosed under environmental attributes |
| CORSIA | A legal obligation, not a voluntary claim |

Units cancelled for CORSIA have already discharged a legal duty. Presenting the same units as a voluntary carbon-neutral achievement is a second claim on the same tonnes, and disclosure regulators are starting to look for it.

Wording has tightened too. "Carbon neutral" claims resting on avoidance credits have drawn regulatory action in several countries. A plain statement of what you did, such as "we retired a stated number of credits of a named type from a named project", holds up better than a label. Our [ESG reporting guide](/insights/esg-reporting-and-carbon-credits/) covers disclosure in more depth.

## The objections, taken seriously

Carbon markets have real critics with real evidence, and their arguments are shaping the rules. A buyer should know them.

- **Additionality is hard to show.** It asks you to prove what would have happened in a world that did not occur. Journalists and academics have found many projects, especially grid-connected renewables in markets where they had become the cheapest option, where the reduction would probably have happened regardless. Some standards have narrowed or withdrawn methodologies in response.
- **Some baselines were inflated.** Avoided-deforestation projects have drawn sustained criticism for forecasting more forest loss than then occurred, and so issuing more credits than the work justified. Jurisdiction-wide baselines are one structural answer. We discuss forest projects in [REDD+ and afforestation](/insights/redd-and-afforestation-carbon-projects/).
- **Stored carbon can be lost.** Fire, drought, disease or a new landowner can release carbon held in trees. Buffer pools exist for this, but they were sized on historic risk and climate-driven losses are rising.
- **Cookstove numbers have been challenged**, on both usage rates and the share of non-renewable biomass. Independent studies have found real reductions below the credited figures for some methodologies.
- **Offsetting can delay real cuts.** If compensating is cheaper than changing, some organisations will keep compensating. This is the reason SBTi and most credible frameworks will not accept credits in place of required reductions.
- **CORSIA counts CO2 only.** Contrails and nitrogen oxides add to aviation's warming effect, by some assessments roughly doubling it. The scheme does not address them.

None of this makes credits worthless, and none of it cancels a legal obligation. It does mean quality varies a great deal and checking it is part of the job.

## Errors we see repeatedly

A short checklist to run before any purchase:

- Was the purpose fixed before shopping? If not, you may have bought voluntary-grade units for a compliance need.
- Did the offers set the standard? Then you accepted what was available.
- Is there an authorisation document, or only the seller's word? No document, no eligibility.
- Was the budget taken from a published average? Expect it to be well off.
- Will the vintage still be eligible when you cancel? Units can age out while you hold them.
- Is everything from one programme or one host country? A single rule change then hits the whole position.
- Did a good rating stand in for an eligibility check? A well-rated, unadjusted unit is still ineligible.
- Are your registry accounts open? Onboarding can take weeks and hold up delivery.
- Was the cancellation purpose correct? A mistake is permanent.
- Were credits netted against reported emissions? That is a misstatement under every major framework.
- Are compliance units also being claimed voluntarily? That is a double claim.

Nearly every item on this list comes from an assumption nobody checked against a document.

## The Indian position

India takes part in CORSIA, and the [DGCA](https://www.dgca.gov.in/) is the national authority: it approves monitoring plans, receives reports, oversees verification and receives cancellation reports.

For Indian carriers the obligation gets steeper over time. More routes come into offsetting in 2027, and from 2030 the individual growth factor starts to count against fast-growing operators, which describes much of Indian aviation.

For Indian project developers the deciding question is whether the Government of India will authorise corresponding adjustments. India is building its domestic Carbon Credit Trading Scheme under the Energy Conservation Act framework, and how that scheme will interact with Article 6 authorisation and CORSIA eligibility is still being settled. A developer aiming at international buyers should confirm the route before choosing a methodology; changing course later is costly and sometimes impossible. We set out both sides in [CORSIA in India](/knowledge-base/corsia-in-india/).

## Questions we are asked most

::: accordion Is CORSIA mandatory?
Monitoring and reporting apply to in-scope operators from the start. Offsetting applies on routes where both States participate. From 2027, States above defined activity thresholds must participate; least developed countries, small island developing States and landlocked developing countries are exempt unless they opt in.
:::

::: accordion Can any credit be used for CORSIA?
No. It must come from an ICAO-approved programme, sit inside an eligible vintage window and carry a corresponding adjustment from the host country. Most credits fail at least one of these.
:::

::: accordion Why do CORSIA-eligible units cost more?
Because the corresponding adjustment makes them scarce. The premium pays for a government's concession and for scarcity. The tonne itself is no different.
:::

::: accordion Does buying credits lower my reported emissions?
No. Credits are shown separately from scope 1, 2 and 3 and are never subtracted from them.
:::

::: accordion How is a carbon credit different from a carbon tax?
A tax is a price paid to a government. A credit is an instrument bought from a project. They behave differently and give different kinds of certainty.
:::

::: accordion Who verifies credits?
Accredited validation and verification bodies, working under the rules of the crediting programme. A body that validated a project's design generally may not also verify its results.
:::

::: accordion Do credits expire?
Not as such. But CORSIA eligibility depends on vintage windows that the ICAO Council sets and has changed before, so a unit can stop being usable for a particular compliance period.
:::

::: accordion What if a unit I bought turns out to be ineligible?
Your contract decides, which is why the eligibility warranty and its remedy matter more than the headline price. Once a unit is cancelled it is used up whatever happens next.
:::

::: accordion Where can I confirm a programme's current approval?
Only on the [ICAO CORSIA emissions units page](https://www.icao.int/environmental-protection/CORSIA/Pages/CORSIA-Emissions-Units.aspx). Any list elsewhere is a snapshot.
:::
