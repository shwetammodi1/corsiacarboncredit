The first CORSIA budget most finance teams produce has one line: expected tonnes multiplied by an expected price. Both inputs are usually wrong, and the line itself leaves out a good share of what the company will actually spend. The better approach is not complicated, but it is a model of your own network rather than a trend drawn through last year's number.

This piece works through that model using an illustrative Indian carrier, then covers the cost lines that go missing, how to set a price assumption, and how to keep the forecast honest during the year.

![Chart of the cost lines that belong in a CORSIA budget](/images/corsia-consultant/corsia-budget-lines.svg)

## Volume matters more than price

Get the tonnes right before worrying about the rate. If the requirement estimate is off by 30% and the price estimate by 15%, the volume error dominates. Most of the effort in a good forecast goes into working out which emissions are actually covered.

## A worked forecast, step by step

Every figure in this section is **illustrative**. Operator Y is invented, and no figure here is a real company's data, a real growth factor or a market price.

**Step 1: filter to covered emissions.** Operator Y's total CO2 for the year is 1,000,000 tonnes. Most of it comes from a large domestic network, which CORSIA does not cover. Take out domestic flying, aircraft at or below 5,700 kg, exempt flight types, and international route pairs where one of the two States does not participate. What remains is 180,000 tonnes of covered emissions.

A budget built on the 1,000,000 figure would overstate the base more than five times. This is the single largest error we see, and it tends to set off alarm, poor decisions and then a credibility problem when the real number arrives.

**Step 2: project forward from the fleet and network plan.** If the plan adds two international routes to participating States next year, covered emissions rise from the day those routes start. Suppose that takes covered emissions to 200,000 tonnes.

**Step 3: re-run coverage for 2027.** In the second phase, participation becomes mandatory for States above the activity thresholds, and route pairs that generate nothing today start to count. Operator Y flies to several States that do not currently participate. Re-running the filter against its actual route list under mandatory participation lifts covered emissions to 240,000 tonnes.

This step cannot be replaced by a percentage uplift. A carrier flying mostly to States already in the scheme sees little change; a carrier with heavy traffic to non-participating States can see a large jump. A generic uplift is wrong for nearly everyone, in one direction or the other.

**Step 4: apply growth-factor scenarios.** ICAO confirms the factor later, but published industry traffic and emissions data supports an estimate. Operator Y uses a low, central and high case, with the reasoning for each written down. At an illustrative central sector factor of 10%, the 2027 requirement is 240,000 × 10% = 24,000 tonnes.

**Step 5: add the individual element from 2030.** From 2030 the factor is weighted 15% to the operator's own growth, rising to 30% from 2033. If Operator Y is growing faster than the sector, say an illustrative individual factor of 20% against a sector factor of 10%, the blended 2030 factor is (0.85 × 10%) + (0.15 × 20%) = 11.5%. For a growing Indian carrier this is not a rounding difference, and it ties fleet and network planning directly to compliance cost.

**Step 6: subtract a fuels reduction you can actually document.** Use fuel you can realistically obtain, with the certification and chain of custody you can realistically produce. A SAF volume taken from a sustainability target rather than a supply contract understates the obligation and the budget together.

**Step 7: price the result.** See the section on anchoring the price below.

**Step 8: add the non-unit cost lines**, covered next.

**Step 9: write down the assumptions and the sensitivities.** A forecast without its assumptions cannot be defended when someone questions it.

## The cost lines that go missing

Unit purchases, verification fees and advisory fees rarely get forgotten. Three other lines do, and each is real cash or real staff time.

- **Internal data and reconciliation work.** This is the biggest hidden cost. Building a pipeline that stands up to verification in year one, and running it every year after, takes time from flight operations, finance and the process owner. Costing it at zero because it is internal leaves the project short of people and the budget short of truth. Cost it at the loaded rate of the staff involved.
- **Registry fees and foreign exchange.** Charged per transaction and small individually, but cross-border payment costs on unit purchases can add up.
- **Due diligence and legal review.** On a first, small tranche these can be a noticeable share of the transaction. They spread out as volumes grow; on the first purchase they do not.

SAF premium is a sometimes-forgotten line of its own. It is an alternative route to meeting part of the obligation, and whether it is cheaper than units changes as unit prices and growth factors move. Run the comparison on current numbers each time rather than relying on last year's conclusion.

In a first year, building the system and getting through verification usually cost more than units. Once obligations become material, unit purchases take over as the largest line.

## Anchoring the price

Ignore published voluntary market averages. They mix products that are not interchangeable across a very wide range and describe nothing you could actually buy for CORSIA.

Anchor on real quotes for units with host-country authorisation, of the type and vintage you would buy, at the volume you would buy. Collecting those quotes takes effort, and it is exactly what makes the budget defensible. Our note on [what drives CORSIA credit pricing](/knowledge-base/corsia-credit-pricing/) explains why the range is wide.

## Stress tests worth running

| Scenario | Why it is plausible |
|---|---|
| Unit prices double | Supply depends on government authorisation decisions |
| Requirement 30% above the central case | Growth factor and coverage uncertainty stack on each other |
| A crediting programme loses ICAO approval | Approvals have lapsed before |
| The vintage window moves | Windows have been adjusted before |
| SAF not available at planned stations | Supply is regional and thin |

Two of these have close precedents in the scheme's own history. Presenting them to a board as remote understates the position.

## Cash timing and the no-banking rule

Obligations are calculated each year but settled once per three-year compliance period. That creates a cash question. Buying in stages across the period spreads the cash and averages the price. Waiting for the deadline concentrates both, at the moment the whole sector is buying.

There is also no carry-forward. Cancelling more than you owe earns nothing against the next period. In the illustrative case above, over-cancelling by 5% on a 24,000-tonne obligation means 1,200 tonnes bought and retired for no purpose. Forecast accuracy has a direct cash value.

## Keeping the forecast current

A budget set once and left alone drifts, because its inputs harden through the year. Update it when:

- **verified emissions arrive**, replacing the estimate with the actual covered figure (usually the biggest single reduction in uncertainty);
- **ICAO publishes growth factors**, at which point the obligation stops being a range and becomes a number;
- **the participation list changes**, since a State joining or leaving moves your coverage without any change to your flying;
- **quotes come in**, replacing assumed prices with observed ones;
- **the network plan changes**, since a new route to a participating State adds obligation from its first day.

Quarterly is a sensible rhythm, plus an extra update when growth factors are published. Record what changed and why each time, so the movement between versions reads as information rather than instability.

The practical reason for all this: an obligation understated all year removes the option of staged buying. The shortfall shows up when the only option left is a late purchase in a thin market. Under-forecasting is the dangerous direction; build in headroom rather than optimism.

## Presenting it upward

Give the board or audit committee the obligation as a range with the central case marked, the trajectory with 2027 and 2030 shown as steps, the named risks, and the levers with their effect quantified. Whoever owns the obligation should own the forecast, with finance validating the assumptions. ## India-specific points

Indian carriers with large domestic operations are the most exposed to the total-versus-covered error, because the domestic share is so high. Domestic aviation emissions are a separate policy question in India, discussed in [CORSIA or CCTS](/insights/corsia-vs-ccts-for-indian-companies/). And because unit purchases are usually cross-border, foreign exchange belongs in the budget from the first transaction.
