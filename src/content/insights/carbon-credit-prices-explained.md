This article is written for the person who has to put a carbon number in a budget and defend it later: a finance lead, a procurement manager, an airline's compliance head. If you want a quick map of which credit types sit where on price, our shorter piece on [what drives the cost of a credit](/insights/carbon-credit-price-what-drives-cost/) covers that. Here we deal with turning prices into a forecast that survives a board meeting and an auditor.

![Diagram of the factors that move a carbon credit's price](/images/carbon-credits/carbon-price-drivers.svg)

## Start from the right mental model

Quoted prices for "a carbon credit" run across more than an order of magnitude. That spread is not market noise. Two units that each represent one tonne of CO2e can differ on almost everything that matters to a buyer:

- whether the host State has given up the reduction in its own accounts
- whether the tonne was avoided or physically removed
- how convincing the additionality case is
- whether the storage could reverse
- the year the reduction took place
- which standard issued it, and whether that standard is approved for your use
- what co-benefits come with it

So a higher price usually buys a different risk profile, not a mark-up. A much lower price usually means the buyer has taken on risk they have not priced.

## The drivers, ranked by how much they move a budget

**Corresponding adjustment status** matters most. Units from the same project, identical except for whether the host State has authorised transfer and adjusted its accounts, trade at clearly different prices. The tonnes are physically the same. The premium pays for a sovereign concession and for scarcity, since each authorising government forgoes those tonnes against its own target, and many choose not to. Our explainer on [Article 6](/insights/article-6-paris-agreement-explained/) walks through the mechanics.

**Removal versus avoidance** is now the sharpest divide in the voluntary market. Companies with net zero claims to defend pay more for removals, because avoidance credits sit awkwardly with a residual-emissions approach.

**Project type** follows from that. Engineered removals sit highest, then nature-based removals (discounted for permanence risk), then methane avoidance, then cookstoves, with grid renewables lowest because their additionality is contested.

**Vintage** decides eligibility outright for compliance use. For voluntary use, recent vintages are generally preferred as a stand-in for current methodology.

**Standard and reputation** can add a premium or, after public criticism of a project category, a discount that applies whatever the individual project's merits.

**Co-benefits** support higher prices for voluntary buyers. Compliance buyers, who need to discharge an obligation rather than tell a story, get far less value from them.

**Volume and contract structure** matter at the margin. Size earns a discount, but a seller holding a small parcel of genuinely adjusted units is under little pressure to give one. Forward contracts price below spot because the buyer carries the delivery and authorisation risk.

## Why the published average will break your budget

Market reports publish average prices and budgets quote them. They should not. An average mixes products that cannot substitute for each other, is weighted towards whatever traded heavily that period, and tells you nothing about the vintage, type, standard and authorisation you would actually buy.

A budget built on an average is usually too low, and nobody finds out until procurement goes looking and cannot find anything at that price that meets the quality bar.

## What a usable quote contains

A number without its attributes tells you nothing. Ask every seller for these eight items:

1. Project ID and registry ID, so you can look it up yourself
2. Standard and methodology version
3. Vintage
4. Volume available
5. Corresponding adjustment status
6. Removal or avoidance
7. Delivery timing: spot, or forward against future issuance
8. The documentation that comes with the units

A seller who cannot supply all eight either does not hold the units or has not examined them. When two quotes differ by a factor of three, set them side by side on these attributes. They are often different products, and the cheaper one is often cheaper for a reason you would not accept.

## A worked example: volume beats price

The following figures are **illustrative only**, not market data.

An operator estimates it must buy 10,000 units in a year and uses a central price of 20 per unit, giving a budget of 200,000.

- If the price estimate is 15% out, the cost moves by 30,000.
- If the volume estimate is 30% out, the cost moves by 60,000.

The volume error does twice the damage. For CORSIA that means filtering properly to covered emissions before worrying about price; budgeting from total emissions is the most common way to get the volume wrong. The calculation itself is set out in [how CORSIA offsetting requirements are calculated](/insights/corsia-offsetting-requirements-calculation/).

## Building the budget

**Get real quotes** for the vintage, type, volume and authorisation status you would buy. It is slow work, and it is exactly the work that makes the budget stand up when questioned.

**Present a range, not a point.** Low, central and high, each tied to a stated assumption. A single figure in a board paper will be read as a forecast, and it will be wrong.

**Add transaction costs.** Due diligence, legal fees, registry charges, treasury and foreign exchange. On a first purchase they are not small next to the unit cost.

**Run sensitivities.** Price doubling; requirement above the central case; a programme losing approval; a vintage window moving. Two of the four have close precedents.

### Across several years

Carry a single-year assumption forward and it drifts. Treat each input on its own terms:

| Input | How it moves | How to model it |
|---|---|---|
| Volume | On a known schedule. For CORSIA, route coverage widens in 2027 and the individual growth factor starts to count from 2030 | As steps in the years they occur |
| Price | Depends on how many governments authorise adjustments, and when. Not forecastable | A range that widens in later years |
| Transaction costs | Mostly one-off in the first cycle | High in year one, recurring items only after |

A forecast that gets more precise the further out it goes is claiming a confidence nobody has. Revisit the numbers whenever growth factors are published or a participation list changes.

## Direction of travel, without a forecast

Nobody knows where prices will go, and a confident forecast is usually a sales pitch. What can be said:

- **Demand grows on a timetable.** CORSIA's second phase from 2027 widens route coverage, and from 2030 the individual growth factor raises obligations for faster-growing operators. Corporate demand for adjusted units is growing separately.
- **Supply depends on government decisions** about authorisation that no one can forecast.
- **The risks are lopsided.** If supply improves faster than expected, a buyer who contracted forward has overpaid slightly. If it falls short, a buyer who waited faces high prices, thin availability and, for compliance buyers, an unmet legal obligation.

That imbalance, not a price prediction, is the case for buying early.

## Ways to pay less

- **Buy in stages** instead of in the deadline window when everyone else is buying.
- **Contract forward** where you are confident of your volume.
- **Spread purchases** across programmes, project types, host States and vintages, so one regulatory change does not hit the whole book.
- **Shrink the obligation.** In aviation, fuel efficiency and qualifying SAF both reduce the requirement. A tonne you do not owe costs nothing.
- **Get the requirement right.** Surplus cancellations do not carry over to the next period.
- **Pay for due diligence.** An ineligible unit is the most expensive unit you can buy.

## Short answers

**Why are CORSIA-eligible credits dearer?** The corresponding adjustment makes them scarce.

**Is there an index I can use?** Indices cover parts of the voluntary market. None reliably tracks the adjusted segment.

**Should we wait for prices to fall?** Waiting pushes your buying into the same window as everyone else's. Buying in stages is usually the safer position.

**Can we budget without quotes?** Not defensibly.
