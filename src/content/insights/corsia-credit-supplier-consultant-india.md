Here is a pattern we have seen more than once, told as an illustrative composite. A biomass developer in central India hires a firm to write a project design document. Eighteen months later the document is polished and the project is registered, but nobody ever checked whether the activity could obtain a corresponding adjustment. The credits that eventually arrive are voluntary-market credits. The airline buyers the business plan relied on cannot use them.

That is the typical failure on the supply side. Airline-side advisers who get it wrong produce verification findings. Supply-side advisers who get it wrong produce a project with no compliance buyer, and the discovery comes after the money has gone.

## The job, in three stages

A consultant working for a project developer covers far more than document writing. It helps to think of the work in three stages.

**Deciding whether to proceed**
- Eligibility screening: a written opinion on whether CORSIA supply is realistic for this activity.
- Corresponding adjustment assessment: the policy position, the route to authorisation and a realistic timeline.
- Programme and methodology choice: which approved programme, which methodology, and the reasons.
- Baseline and additionality: the case, with evidence, before a validator sees it.

**Building the project**
- Monitoring design: parameters, instruments, frequency, data flow and retention.
- The project design document that validation will assess.
- Support through validation and verification: evidence, responses and closing findings.

**Selling the output**
- A buyer documentation pack matching what compliance diligence asks for.
- Commercial support: price expectations, contract terms and introductions to buyers.

The first stage should be short and inexpensive. A capable firm can say within a week or two whether the CORSIA route is plausible, looking at programme approval, methodology fit, additionality and the adjustment position. A firm that skips that and quotes straight for a design document is selling development hours, not advice.

## Eight screening questions your adviser should raise unprompted

1. Does an approved programme cover this activity type today?
2. Is there an approved methodology, and does the activity fit it without stretching?
3. What is the adjustment position for this activity, and what is the route to authorisation?
4. Is the activity genuinely additional, and what evidence shows it?
5. What annual volume is realistic, estimated conservatively?
6. Does that volume cover the fixed costs of validation, verification and registry fees?
7. Can monitoring be done defensibly, with instruments that exist and staff who will use them?
8. Who owns the credits, in writing?

The last question kills more deals at diligence than any technical point. When the landowner, equipment owner, operator and lender are separate parties, title to the credits has to be settled on paper before validation, not argued over when a buyer asks. A project on a leased site with outside finance is the typical case.

## An interview script for shortlisted firms

Put these to every firm, and listen for specifics:

- **"How many projects have you taken all the way to issuance?"** Registration is not issuance. Ask how many produced credits that were then sold.
- **"Which programmes and methodologies, exactly?"** Methodology experience is specific. Names and numbers, please.
- **"Tell me about a validation or verification you supported, and the findings."** People who have done it have stories.
- **"What is the adjustment position for my activity?"** A credible answer admits policy uncertainty and describes the route. Anyone calling it "just paperwork" has ruled themselves out.
- **"Can I see a redacted design document you wrote?"** This single document tells you more than any credentials slide.
- **"Which projects have you told clients not to pursue?"** If the answer is none, they have not been screening.
- **"How are you paid, and do you take credits?"** Both approaches exist. Know which one you are buying and what pressure it creates.
- **"Could you also find buyers?"** Some firms can. Ask how they are paid on a sale and whether that sits uneasily with their pricing advice.

## Common mistakes in supply-side advice

**Inflated volumes.** The most frequent and most harmful error. Verifiers cut claimed reductions, and a financial model built on the developer's top estimate breaks when verified volume lands 15–20% lower.

**Monitoring bolted on afterwards.** Adding measurement to a finished plant is costly and sometimes not possible. It belongs in the engineering design.

**Additionality claimed, not shown.** A barrier analysis needs documents: board papers, financing terms, market data. A narrative is not evidence.

**The adjustment treated as admin.** It is a gating policy question and should be treated as one from the first week.

**Documents assembled when a buyer asks.** Diligence then takes months, and buyers find other supply.

**A familiar programme instead of an approved one.** The adviser's favourite programme may not be approved for your scope.

## Fee arrangements and their side effects

- **Fixed fee per deliverable.** Priced per document and stage. Read the exclusions around validation findings.
- **Monthly retainer.** Runs through development. Watch for scope creep and a vague end point.
- **Success fee on issuance.** Paid when credits are issued. It rewards optimism in volume estimates.
- **Share of credits.** A percentage of issuance. The same optimism risk, and it cuts into your revenue for the whole crediting period.
- **Hybrid.** A reduced fixed fee with a smaller success element. Usually the most balanced.

A share of credits across a seven-year crediting period is far bigger than it looks when you sign. Work it out in rupees over the full term before agreeing, not as a percentage in the abstract.

## Honest timelines to first credits

What a good adviser should tell you before you raise money against the project:

| Phase | Steps and typical duration | Controlled by |
|---|---|---|
| Deciding | Screening 1–2 weeks; feasibility and modelling 3–6 weeks; methodology and programme 2–3 weeks | Adviser and developer |
| Authorisation track | Opens at feasibility and closes when it closes | Government |
| Documenting | Design document 8–16 weeks; validation 8–16 weeks; registration 4–12 weeks | Adviser, validation body, programme |
| Operating | First monitoring period 6–12 months | The physical project |
| Issuing | Verification 6–12 weeks; issuance 4–8 weeks | Verification body, programme, registry |

In total, roughly **18 to 36 months to first credits**, assuming nothing stalls. The authorisation track runs in parallel, which is why it should start at feasibility rather than after registration.

Two consequences follow. Funding is front-loaded across two to three years before any revenue, and an adviser who does not take you through that cash flow is not doing the job. And the monitoring period cannot be shortened: the project has to physically operate to produce the reductions that get verified.

Treat any promise of credits within a year, for a project that does not yet exist, with suspicion. Either it describes a project already well advanced, or something that will not survive validation.

## What you should hold when the engagement ends

- A written eligibility assessment, including the adjustment position.
- A project registered under a suitable approved programme.
- A monitoring system that produces audit-ready data without heroics.
- A clean validation and first verification.
- A full buyer pack, assembled before you go to market.
- A volume forecast with the verification haircut already applied.
- Your own staff able to run the monitoring cycle.

The last point is easy to overlook. Monitoring continues for the whole crediting period, with periodic verification and registry administration alongside. A developer who cannot run it in-house pays adviser rates for routine data collection for years. Ask every shortlisted firm what annual running costs look like in year four, when development is over and the asset is simply producing credits. Firms with several verification cycles behind them answer at once.
