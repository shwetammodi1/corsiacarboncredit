**How are carbon credits used in practice?** Organisations buy verified credits and **retire** them for three main purposes:

- to compensate for emissions they cannot yet cut
- to support voluntary climate goals
- to meet legal obligations in compliance schemes, such as airlines under **CORSIA**

Each credit generally represents one tonne of CO₂ equivalent reduced or removed by a project elsewhere. The most credible uses come **after** direct emission cuts, not instead of them. For aviation-specific guidance, see our [CORSIA Carbon Credits](https://corsiacarboncredit.in/) resources.

This guide focuses on **real-world use cases by sector**, with worked examples. For a broader introduction, see our beginner's guide to [carbon credits explained](/blog/carbon-credit-explained/).

## What Are Carbon Credits and How Do They Work?

A **carbon credit** is a tradable certificate showing that a project reduced or removed greenhouse gases, generally **one metric tonne of CO₂e per credit** under the issuing programme's rules.

Credits come from two kinds of projects:

- **Reduction projects**, such as wind farms replacing coal power, methane capture at landfills or efficient cookstoves. These prevent emissions that would otherwise happen.
- **Removal projects**, such as reforestation, biochar or direct air capture. These take CO₂ out of the air and store it.

A project earns credits through these steps:

1. It follows an approved **methodology**.
2. It sets a realistic **baseline**, meaning what would have happened without it.
3. It shows **additionality**, meaning the reductions would not have happened anyway.
4. Its results are **verified** by an accredited auditor.
5. A programme such as Verra or Gold Standard then **issues** credits into a **registry**, each with a unique serial number.

The registry records who holds each credit and when it is **retired**, so it can never be used twice.

**Simple example:** a landfill captures methane equal to 1,000 tCO₂e in a year. After verification, it receives 1,000 credits, which it can sell to buyers who want to compensate for their own emissions.

## How Are Carbon Credits Used by Businesses and Organisations?

### 1. Addressing residual emissions

Companies first **measure** their greenhouse gas emissions, then **reduce** what they can through efficiency, clean power and process changes. Credits are then used for the **residual emissions** that remain, which is the core of carbon offsetting.

### 2. Corporate climate commitments

Credits help companies act on emissions that are hard to eliminate quickly, such as business travel or heat-intensive processes, alongside long-term reduction targets. Claims must be transparent. Statements such as "carbon neutral" need a clear scope, the tonnes covered and retirement evidence, otherwise they risk being seen as greenwashing.

### 3. Supply chain emissions

Emissions are grouped into three scopes:

- **Scope 1:** direct emissions from the company's own operations, such as fuel burned on site
- **Scope 2:** emissions from the electricity and heat it buys
- **Scope 3:** everything else in its value chain, from suppliers to customers

Most of a company's footprint is often in Scope 3. Some companies support supplier reductions directly. Others consider credits as part of a wider supply chain strategy, but credits do not replace engaging suppliers to cut emissions.

### 4. Sustainability reporting

Companies document credit purchases, keep **registry and retirement records**, and report:

- **reductions** in their own emissions
- **offsetting** with credits

These must be reported **separately**. Mixing them up overstates progress.

**Carbon credits should complement direct emissions reduction, never replace it.**

## How Are Carbon Credits Used in Different Industries?

### 1. Aviation

International airlines with obligations under **CORSIA** must acquire and cancel **eligible emissions units** to cover emissions growth on routes between participating States. Credits sit alongside sustainable aviation fuel, newer aircraft and operational efficiency.

### 2. Manufacturing

Manufacturers use credits for residual emissions after efficiency upgrades and fuel switching. In some countries, they also use credits or certificates in regulated schemes. India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme, for example, uses its own Carbon Credit Certificates.

### 3. Energy

Renewable energy and energy transition projects have historically been a major **source** of credits. Energy companies may also buy credits for residual emissions or take part in compliance markets.

### 4. Transportation and logistics

Shipping, trucking and courier firms manage transport emissions through fleet efficiency and cleaner fuels. They may use credits for remaining emissions, sometimes offering "offset delivery" options to customers.

### 5. Agriculture and forestry

These sectors often **generate** credits through:

- reforestation and forest protection
- improved soil carbon practices
- methane reduction, for example from manure management or rice cultivation

### 6. Technology and services

These companies mainly emit through electricity use, travel and purchased goods. They often switch to renewable power and use credits for residual emissions to support voluntary climate targets.

Which credits each sector can use, and for what purpose, depends on the programme, the market and the applicable regulations.

## How Are Carbon Credits Used for Carbon Offsetting?

**Carbon offsetting** means compensating for your emissions by retiring credits that represent reductions or removals elsewhere. The usual sequence is:

1. **Calculate the footprint** across the relevant scopes.
2. **Reduce first**, through efficiency, clean energy and process change.
3. **Identify remaining emissions** to be compensated.
4. **Buy suitable verified credits** from a reputable programme.
5. **Transfer** them into your account, or have them retired on your behalf.
6. **Retire** them in the registry, naming your organisation and the purpose.
7. **Keep evidence**, such as retirement certificates and serial numbers.

**Buying credits does not, by itself, make a company carbon neutral.** Neutrality claims also depend on what emissions are covered, how they were measured, the quality of the credits and whether the claim follows recognised guidance. Our explainer on the [difference between a carbon credit and a carbon offset](/blog/difference-between-carbon-credit-and-carbon-offset/) covers this in more detail.

### Example 1: A manufacturing company (hypothetical)

*All figures are illustrative.* A packaging manufacturer measures 20,000 tCO₂e a year. It installs efficient motors and switches to renewable electricity, cutting 8,000 tonnes. For 4,000 tonnes of hard-to-cut process heat emissions, it buys verified credits from a landfill gas project and retires them in its name. Its report shows the 8,000-tonne reduction and the 4,000-tonne offset **separately**, and states that the remaining 8,000 tonnes were not offset.

## How Are Carbon Credits Traded and Purchased?

The market's participants are:

- **Project developers**, who run reduction or removal projects
- **Programmes and standards**, which set the rules and issue credits
- **Buyers and sellers**, such as companies, traders and airlines
- **Brokers and intermediaries**, who match buyers and sellers
- **Exchanges and trading platforms**, for standardised contracts
- **Registry accounts**, where credits are held and moved

The key steps are different from each other:

- **Generation:** the project achieves reductions.
- **Issuance:** the programme creates serial-numbered credits after verification.
- **Purchase:** a buyer and seller agree a deal.
- **Transfer:** the credits move between registry accounts.
- **Retirement or cancellation:** the credits are permanently removed for a stated purpose.

There are two kinds of market:

- **Voluntary markets** serve companies' own climate goals.
- **Compliance markets** serve legal obligations, and only accept units that meet the scheme's rules.

Not every credit is available on every platform or eligible for every scheme. Credits are also **not Renewable Energy Certificates (RECs)**, which represent renewable electricity rather than emission reductions.

## How Are Carbon Credits Used Under CORSIA in the Aviation Industry?

**CORSIA**, the Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation, is ICAO's global market-based measure for international aviation's CO₂.

- **Monitoring and reporting** apply to operators emitting more than 10,000 tonnes of CO₂ a year from international flights with aeroplanes above 5,700 kg.
- **Offsetting** applies only to flights between participating States, with growth measured against a baseline of 85% of 2019 emissions from 2024.
- The airline's **State calculates its offsetting requirement** for each compliance period from verified emissions.
- The airline acquires **CORSIA eligible emissions units**: credits from programmes approved by the ICAO Council that meet unit-level conditions on scope, vintage, host-country authorisation and labelling.
- Units are tracked in programme **registries** by serial number.
- **Cancellation** for CORSIA, recording the operator and compliance period, completes compliance, and the airline then reports to its State.

ICAO does not sell credits or run an exchange. **Ordinary voluntary credits are not automatically eligible.** Our guide to [CORSIA eligible carbon credits](/blog/corsia-eligible-carbon-credits/) explains the conditions.

### Example 2: An airline operator (hypothetical)

*This example is hypothetical.* An airline's State informs it of its offsetting requirement for the 2024–2026 compliance period. The airline checks ICAO's current eligibility document. It then buys first-phase units from an approved programme, after confirming their vintage, Letter of Authorization and CORSIA label in the registry. The units are cancelled for CORSIA in its name, and the airline submits an Emissions Unit Cancellation Report to its State.

### Example 3: A forestry project (hypothetical)

*This example is hypothetical.* A community reforests degraded land under a programme methodology. It measures tree growth in sample plots, deducts a share of credits into a buffer pool against fire risk, and has the results verified. The programme then issues removal credits, which buyers can retire to compensate for their emissions. If the programme and units qualify, they may also be eligible for compliance uses.

## Benefits and Limitations of Using Carbon Credits

### Potential benefits

- channels finance to **verified climate projects**
- funds reduction and removal activities that might not happen otherwise
- supports **corporate climate strategies** for residual emissions
- enables **carbon market participation** and price signals
- supports **community and environmental co-benefits** in some projects
- helps airlines meet **CORSIA obligations**

### Limitations and challenges

- **Quality varies** widely between projects and methodologies.
- **Additionality:** some projects would have happened anyway.
- **Permanence:** stored carbon can be released by fire or logging.
- **Leakage:** emissions can simply move elsewhere.
- **Double counting:** the same tonne can be claimed twice without proper safeguards.
- **Verification challenges**, especially for land-based projects.
- **Prices vary** widely, so cheap credits may carry more risk.
- **Misleading claims** can damage trust and attract regulatory action.

Credit quality, transparency and appropriate use are what make carbon credits a credible tool rather than a reputational risk.

## How to Choose and Use Carbon Credits Responsibly

- **Define the purpose:** a voluntary claim, compliance, or supporting a specific project type
- **Measure and document** your emissions footprint
- **Prioritise direct reductions** before buying credits
- **Evaluate the project type and methodology**
- **Check independent validation and verification**
- **Review programme and registry records**, including serial numbers
- **Assess additionality and permanence**, including buffer arrangements for removals
- **Check for double-counting risks**, including host-country authorisation where relevant
- **Confirm the eligibility rules** for your intended use
- **Keep records** of purchase, transfer and retirement
- **Make transparent, evidence-based claims**

Organisations using credits for **CORSIA** must also check ICAO's latest eligibility conditions for the relevant compliance period. ## Key Takeaways

So, how are carbon credits used? Businesses retire them for residual emissions and climate goals. Industries from manufacturing to logistics use them alongside decarbonisation, and forestry and agriculture generate them. Compliance schemes accept only units that meet their rules, and airlines cancel eligible units under CORSIA.

Credits support climate action only when they represent credible, verified reductions or removals and are used transparently. Direct emissions reduction should always come first.
